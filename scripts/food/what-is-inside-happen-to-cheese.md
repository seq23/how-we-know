### Happen: What happens to meat when you cook it?

What happens to meat when you cook it?

{{stat: 100 | degrees Celsius | the boiling point of water at sea level | Royal Society of Chemistry}}
A wet steak surface stalls near 100 degrees Celsius, however hot the pan. When you cook meat, heat unfolds its muscle proteins, which then tighten and squeeze out water, while connective collagen shrinks and, with long moist heat, dissolves into gelatin. That stalling figure is the boiling point of water at sea level, a reference value in handbooks from the Royal Society of Chemistry. As long as the surface stays wet, the incoming energy goes into evaporation, so browning waits. But the boiling point only governs the surface. Inside, meat loses juice long before boiling, and each protein gives way at its own temperature.

### Happen: Heat changes shape before it changes chemistry

{{cross_section: raw muscle | fibers, myosin and actin, collagen, myoglobin}}
Start with what raw muscle is. It is mostly water, held in bundles of long cells called fibers. Inside each fiber, two proteins, myosin and actin, are arranged in overlapping filaments that slide past each other when a living muscle contracts. Around the fibers runs a sheath of connective tissue built mostly from collagen. Dissolved in the cell fluid is myoglobin, the protein that stores oxygen and gives raw meat its red. Cooking is the story of what heat does to each of these, one after another.

{{define: denaturation | a protein unfolds as heat breaks its weak bonds | it does not break the chain into pieces | Royal Society of Chemistry}}
Proteins hold their shapes with weak bonds, hydrogen bonds and other attractions that fold a long chain into a working structure. Heat makes the chain vibrate until those bonds let go, and the protein unfolds. That is denaturation: a protein unfolds as heat breaks its weak bonds. On its own, it does not break the chain into pieces. What follows matters more. Unfolded proteins expose sticky regions that were buried inside, and they latch on to their neighbors. Strands that were separate become a tangled, tighter network. Nothing has been added, and very little has been destroyed. The meat has been rearranged.

### Happen: A calorimeter records each protein giving way

{{steps: differential scanning calorimeter | warms a tiny sample of muscle | an empty reference side by side | records how much extra energy | >every time a protein population gives way, the trace shows a bump}}
So how do you know which protein goes first? You do not watch it. You measure heat. The instrument is a differential scanning calorimeter. It warms a tiny sample of muscle and an empty reference side by side at a steady rate, and it records how much extra energy the sample needs to keep pace. Unfolding a protein absorbs energy. So every time a protein population gives way, the trace shows a bump, a peak of heat flowing in.

{{contrast: peak order | is=very stable | not=fixed constants}}
For muscle, food chemists repeatedly report three main peaks. The first is attributed to myosin, in roughly the mid-fifties Celsius. A middle peak is linked to collagen and to the dissolved proteins of the cell fluid. The last, attributed to actin, sits near eighty degrees Celsius. Those positions are not fixed constants. Published values shift by several degrees with the species, the cut, the acidity of the muscle and how fast the instrument heats. The order, however, is very stable. The calorimeter gives you a reliable sequence, and only an approximate degree for each step.

### Happen: Tightening fibers wring out the water

{{chain: what you can see on the plate | myosin and then actin unfold and bond | the fibers contract | Water that was held between the filaments has less room | >the juice that pools on the board}}
That sequence explains the timing of the next change, the one you can see on the plate. As myosin and then actin unfold and bond, the fibers contract, both across their width and along their length. Water that was held between the filaments has less room. The tightening network pushes it out, the way a fist wrings a wet cloth. That expelled liquid is the juice that pools on the board, and it carries dissolved proteins, salts and flavor with it.

{{define: cooking loss | the difference between raw and cooked weight | no single percentage describes meat in general | USDA Agricultural Research Service}}
This is measured with nothing more exotic than a balance. Meat scientists, including those at the USDA Agricultural Research Service, weigh a sample raw, cook it to a set internal temperature read by a probe, blot it and weigh it again. The difference between raw and cooked weight is called cooking loss. Across many such studies the pattern is consistent. Loss is modest at lower internal temperatures and rises steeply once actin begins to denature. The size of the loss varies widely between cuts and methods, so no single percentage describes meat in general. What holds is the direction and the timing, and both line up with the calorimeter's peaks.

{{text: fibers shrunken in diameter}}
Microscopes confirm the mechanism rather than just the outcome. Electron micrographs of cooked muscle show fibers shrunken in diameter and the spaces between them widened. That empty space is where the water was. The balance tells you how much left. The microscope shows you the route it took.

### Happen: Collagen shrinks quickly and dissolves only with time

{{text: three protein chains wound together}}
Connective tissue runs on a different clock. Collagen is a triple helix, three protein chains wound together and cross-linked, and it is strong because of that winding. When it is heated into the sixties Celsius, the helix loses its order and the sheath around the fibers shrinks sharply. That shrinkage squeezes the meat further, which is one reason a quickly cooked piece can tighten and curl in the pan.

{{contrast: tender | is=the collagen around them dissolves | not=its muscle fibers soften}}
But hold collagen hot and wet for long enough and something else happens. The cross-links and the helix come apart for good, and the chains dissolve into the surrounding liquid as gelatin. This conversion is slow. It takes hours rather than minutes, and it depends on moisture, which is why tough, connective-rich cuts are braised or stewed. Here the measurements correct an easy assumption. A tough cut becomes tender not because its muscle fibers soften, since they keep drying, but because the collagen around them dissolves. Your fork registers the sum of two opposite trends.

### Happen: Color change is a protein measurement too

{{checklist: myoglobin | +With oxygen attached, it is bright red | +Without oxygen, purplish | +When the iron is oxidized, brown | +the denatured pigment is a dull gray-brown}}
Myoglobin holds an iron atom at its center, and the color of meat depends on what that iron is bound to and how it is charged. With oxygen attached, it is bright red. Without oxygen, purplish. When the iron is oxidized, brown. Heat adds another state. It unfolds the globin protein wrapped around the iron, and the denatured pigment is a dull gray-brown that does not turn back to red.

{{chain: reflectance spectrophotometers | shine light on the cut surface | record which wavelengths come back | >Color reports on myoglobin, not on the whole piece}}
You can measure this rather than judge it by eye. Colorimeters and reflectance spectrophotometers shine light on the cut surface and record which wavelengths come back, and each form of myoglobin has its own signature. The reading tracks how much of the pigment has been denatured. Yet color is the least direct guide to what has happened elsewhere in the meat. Acidity, storage and how much oxygen reached the cut all shift the color at a given temperature. Researchers have documented meat that browns early and meat that stays pink after reaching higher temperatures. Color reports on myoglobin, not on the whole piece.

### Happen: Browning waits for the surface to dry

{{timeline: 1912=Louis-Camille Maillard | 1953=John Hodge}}
Now the surface. In 1912, the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard reported to the French Academy of Sciences that heating amino acids with sugars produced brown compounds. The reaction now carries his name. In 1953, the chemist John Hodge published a scheme organizing its steps, in a journal published by the American Chemical Society. What his scheme made clear is that this is not one reaction but a large network of them. A sugar and an amino group join, rearrange, fragment and recombine, and the products include the brown pigments of a crust and a great many aroma compounds.

{{kitchen: 100 | Maillard}}
Here the cold open's figure does its work. A wet surface is held near 100 degrees Celsius, because the incoming energy goes into evaporating water rather than raising temperature. The Maillard network runs slowly at that level. It accelerates once the surface dries and its temperature climbs well past the boiling point. That is the physical reason a wet piece of meat steams before it sears, and why a crust forms on the outside while the inside is still far cooler. Probes inserted at different depths map that steep gradient directly.

{{sources: Who recorded the crust | French Academy of Sciences=heating amino acids with sugars produced brown compounds | PubChem=Structures for many of them}}
How do chemists know what the crust is made of? Gas chromatography separates the vapors driven off a cooked sample, and mass spectrometry identifies each one by the way it breaks into fragments. Those methods have catalogued pyrazines, which carry roasted notes, along with sulfur-containing compounds and products of fat breaking down in the heat. Structures for many of them are recorded in public chemistry databases such as PubChem. Which compounds dominate a particular aroma is still debated, because the amount of a compound and the strength of its smell are not the same thing.

### Happen: Fat melts across a range, not at a point

{{contrast: fat melting | is=a broad range | not=a single temperature}}
Fat follows physics more than chemistry. Animal fat is a mixture of many different triglycerides, each with its own melting behavior, so it does not melt at a single temperature. It softens and liquefies across a broad range, which a calorimeter records as a wide smear rather than a sharp peak. As it melts, it renders out of the tissue, some lost to the pan and some coating the fibers. At the hottest surfaces, fats also break down and oxidize, adding their own volatile products to the aroma. The American Oil Chemists' Society publishes standard methods that laboratories use to characterize fats like these.

### Happen: What to notice in the edit

{{steps: different kind of measurement | Heat flow showed the order of unfolding | A balance showed where the water went | Reflected light tracked the pigment | Separated vapors named the aroma}}
As you watch the sequence back, notice that each change on screen was established by a different kind of measurement. Heat flow showed the order of unfolding. A balance showed where the water went. Reflected light tracked the pigment. Separated vapors named the aroma. None of these instruments sees the whole steak. Each one isolates a single process, and the picture of cooked meat is assembled from where they overlap.

### Happen: Evidence limit

{{checklist: what is settled | +The mechanisms are well established | -a single temperature table that holds for every piece of meat | ?The precise numbers belong to the samples}}
What this evidence does not give you is a single temperature table that holds for every piece of meat. The calorimeter peaks move with species, age, acidity and heating rate. Cooking loss depends on the cut, its shape and how quickly heat reaches the center. Color can mislead in either direction. Most of the cleanest measurements come from small, uniform laboratory samples heated evenly, while a real roast holds a hot rim, a cool core and every stage in between at once. The mechanisms are well established. The precise numbers belong to the samples that produced them.

### Happen: Closing

{{ambient}}
Cooking meat, then, is not one event. It is a series of proteins letting go, each at its own point, while water leaves, collagen tightens or dissolves, and the surface waits to dry before it browns. The heat is simple. What it undoes is not.

### To: What to do when chocolate is not melting?

What to do when chocolate is not melting?

{{stat: 34 | degrees Celsius | stable crystal form | American Oil Chemists' Society}}
A few drops of water can turn a bowl of glossy chocolate into stiff paste. Chocolate that stays thick and grainy over heat has usually seized or scorched, and more heat will not rescue it. That surprises people, because the fat in chocolate should be the easy part. Research published through the American Oil Chemists' Society places the melting point of cocoa butter's stable crystal form at roughly 34 degrees Celsius, just below the temperature inside your mouth. Warm water is more than enough to melt it. So when a bowl will not loosen, the problem is rarely too little heat. Something else in the bowl has changed. The evidence for what that something is comes from crystallography, from calorimetry, and from the ordinary behaviour of sugar.

### To: Chocolate is solid sugar suspended in fat

{{cross_section: dark chocolate | sugar and cocoa particles in cocoa butter}}
To see why heat is not the fix, picture the bar cut open. A piece of dark chocolate is mostly solid particles: finely ground sugar crystals and fragments of roasted cocoa solids, packed so closely that your tongue cannot feel them separately. Between them runs a continuous film of fat. So the honest picture is sugar and cocoa particles in cocoa butter, with very little water anywhere. Milk chocolate adds milk solids, and white chocolate leaves out the cocoa solids but keeps the butter. When you melt chocolate, only the fat actually melts. The sugar does not. It stays solid the whole time, and the melted bar flows only because each grain is coated and lubricated by liquid fat.

{{define: polymorphism | one fat able to crystallise in several distinct forms | describes structure, not the fat's chemical recipe | American Oil Chemists' Society}}
That fat is unusual. Cocoa butter is dominated by a small number of triglycerides built from palmitic, stearic and oleic acids, and because those molecules are so alike, they can stack together in several different ways. Chemists call this polymorphism: one fat able to crystallise in several distinct forms. Cocoa butter has six recognised forms, numbered one to six, each with its own melting range. The term describes structure, not the fat's chemical recipe. The molecules are the same across all six forms; only their packing differs. Form five is the one chocolate makers aim for when they temper, and it is the form that melts near 34 degrees Celsius.

### To: A little water locks the sugar, not the fat

{{contrast: seized | is=sugar glued together by a little water | not=fat that has failed to melt}}
Here is where the kitchen comes in. Sucrose, ordinary table sugar, dissolves readily in water, and its entry in the PubChem chemical database, run by the National Institutes of Health, records that high solubility. Now let a little water into melted chocolate, from a wet spoon, a splash, or steam condensing on the side of the bowl. Those few drops wet the surfaces of a great many sugar grains at once. A thin syrup forms wherever grains touch, and it bridges them together, the way damp sand holds a shape that dry sand cannot. The fat is still liquid. But the particles it was lubricating are now stuck in clumps, and the whole mass turns stiff, dull and grainy. That is seizing: sugar glued together by a little water, not fat that has failed to melt.

### To: More liquid, not less, rescues a seized bowl

{{steps: rescuing seized chocolate | take the bowl off the heat | add warm liquid a spoonful at a time | stir until smooth | >the result is a sauce, not tempered chocolate}}
This is also why turning up the heat fails. Heat can drive off some water, but as it does, the syrup between the grains thickens and grips harder. The fix follows from the same physics, and it runs the other way. A small amount of water makes bridges between grains. A larger amount does something different: it dissolves enough sugar and surrounds enough particles that the mixture becomes a smooth, fluid sauce. That is the same principle behind a ganache, where cream and chocolate are combined on purpose. Rescuing seized chocolate therefore runs in the opposite direction from instinct. First, take the bowl off the heat. Then add warm liquid a spoonful at a time, cream, milk, water or a neutral oil, and stir until smooth after each addition. The paste breaks up and loosens into something glossy. The limit is worth saying plainly: the result is a sauce, not tempered chocolate. It will not set with a clean snap again, but it still works in a batter, a glaze or a drink.

### To: Scorched chocolate has changed chemically, and it stays changed

{{kitchen: 100 | where water boils}}
The second way chocolate refuses to melt looks similar but has a different cause. Chocolate scorches when parts of it get far hotter than they need to, usually the layer touching a hot pan or the edges of a bowl in a microwave. A water bath beneath the bowl tops out where water boils, 100 degrees Celsius at sea level, which is why it is gentler than direct heat, though its steam is also exactly the stray moisture that seizes chocolate. Milk and white chocolate are the most vulnerable. They contain milk proteins and the milk sugar lactose, and when proteins and sugars are heated together they begin Maillard reactions, the browning chemistry studied extensively in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry. The result is thick, crumbly, darkened and tasting burnt.

{{checklist: reading the bowl | +stiff and grainy | +burnt smell | -reversible once scorched | ?both at once}}
Reading the bowl is mostly a matter of looking, then smelling. Seized chocolate is stiff and grainy but still smells like chocolate. Scorched chocolate has a burnt smell and often darker flecks through it. Unlike seizing, the change is not reversible once scorched. New compounds have formed, and no amount of added liquid unmakes them. The open case is a bowl showing both at once, seized by steam and then overheated as someone pushed harder to fix it, and for that bowl the only dependable answer is to start again.

### To: Some bars are built not to flow

{{contrast: compound coating | is=a confection made with vegetable fats other than cocoa butter | not=what may be labelled chocolate}}
There is a third case, and here nothing has gone wrong at all. Some products are simply designed to behave differently. In the United States, federal standards of identity define what may be labelled chocolate, and those standards are built around cocoa butter. Candy melts and many coatings are compound coatings: a confection made with vegetable fats other than cocoa butter. Those fats melt over different ranges and do not form cocoa butter's six crystal forms, so they set without tempering, but they also respond to heat in their own way. Chips are another case. Many are made to hold their shape in a hot oven, and a chip that keeps its outline in a cookie is doing its job. That does not mean it will not melt, only that it flows less. Formulations vary by brand, and labels rarely give figures, so the most general guidance is to read the ingredient list and expect thicker flow.

### To: Bloom changes the surface, not the melt

{{define: fat bloom | a pale film of fat crystals on the surface | not mould and does not stop melting | Royal Society of Chemistry}}
Old chocolate raises one last worry. A pale, streaky surface makes people think the bar has turned into something that will not melt. That film has a name. Fat bloom is a pale film of fat crystals on the surface, often linked to the slow shift from form five to the higher-melting form six, or to fat migrating outward through the bar. Sugar bloom is different: moisture dissolves surface sugar, which then recrystallises as a dry white dust. The Royal Society of Chemistry's education material uses chocolate as a classroom example of polymorphism. Either way, the boundary matters. Bloom is not mould and does not stop melting. The bar melts normally, and once it is fully melted, its old crystal history is erased.

### To: Gentle heat and dry tools prevent all of it

{{steps: melting cleanly | dry the bowl and spoon | chop the chocolate evenly | warm gently, stirring | stop while a few pieces remain | >residual heat finishes the job}}
Prevention follows from everything above, and none of it needs a number. Melting cleanly comes down to a few habits. Dry the bowl and spoon completely, because the failure starts with drops. Chop the chocolate evenly, so small shards do not overheat while large chunks are still solid. Warm gently, stirring, over a water bath that does not touch the bowl, or in short bursts in a microwave with a stir between each one. Then stop while a few pieces remain unmelted. Residual heat finishes the job, and stirring those last pieces through keeps any part of the bowl from overshooting. If you want the chocolate to set with a snap afterwards, that is tempering, a separate process with its own temperature curve, and recipes differ on its exact targets.

### To: Evidence limit

{{text: no published threshold}}
Most of what explains seizing is laboratory physics applied to a kitchen. The calorimetry and diffraction were done on purified cocoa butter and controlled samples, not on your bowl, with its particular brand, its unknown water content and its uneven heat. There is no published threshold for how many drops of water will seize a given amount of chocolate, because the answer depends on the sugar, the particle size and how quickly you stir. The figure of roughly 34 degrees Celsius describes form five in cocoa butter; a real bar, with milk fat or other fats mixed in, softens across a broader range. And the rescue with added liquid is well established in practice, but how much liquid a particular bowl needs is something you find by watching, not by formula.

### To: Closing

{{ambient}}
So a bowl that will not melt is rarely asking for more heat. It is usually telling you that water reached the sugar, that heat reached the milk, or that the bar was never built around cocoa butter to begin with. The fat itself was ready all along, at the temperature of a warm mouth.

### Cheese: How is cheese made?

How is cheese made?

{{stat: 105 | amino acid position | Kappa-casein is cut between positions 105 and 106 | National Institutes of Health}}
Break one bond in one milk protein, and a liquid becomes a solid. That bond sits between amino acid positions 105 and 106 of a protein called kappa-casein, a cut mapped in research indexed by the National Institutes of Health. Cheese is made by destabilising milk's casein proteins so they clump into a gel, then cutting that gel to expel the watery whey and ageing what remains. But a broken bond only gives you curd. It does not give you cheddar, brie or mozzarella. Every difference you can taste between those comes after the gel forms: how finely it is cut, how hot it is cooked, how much salt goes in, and which microbes keep working for weeks or years afterwards. On screen, a glass of milk and a wheel of cheese sit side by side. The rest of this episode is the distance between them, and how each step across that distance was actually measured.

### Cheese: Milk is a suspension built to stay liquid

{{define: casein micelle | a suspended particle of casein and calcium phosphate | not a dissolved molecule | American Chemical Society}}
On screen, a drop of milk under a microscope. It looks uniform in the glass, but it is not a solution in the way salt water is. Most of milk's protein is casein, and casein does not drift around as single molecules. It gathers into clusters of thousands of protein chains, stitched together by tiny nanoclusters of calcium phosphate. Each casein micelle is a suspended particle of casein and calcium phosphate, not a dissolved molecule, small enough to stay dispersed and to scatter light, which is part of why milk looks white rather than clear. The structure was not seen directly at first. It was inferred from light scattering and from how micelles behaved when calcium was added or removed, and later pictured with electron microscopy. Much of that work appears in the American Chemical Society's Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, alongside decades of other dairy research.

{{contrast: kappa-casein | is=the outer layer that keeps micelles apart | not=the bulk of the casein inside the micelle}}
One protein in that cluster matters more than its share suggests. Kappa-casein sits mostly at the surface, where it forms the outer layer that keeps micelles apart. Its tails stick out into the surrounding liquid like a fringe, carrying negative charge and taking up space, so two micelles that drift close simply bounce off each other. The bulk of the casein inside the micelle would happily stick to its neighbours. It never gets the chance, because the fringe is in the way. That arrangement is why fresh milk stays liquid in the fridge. Cheesemaking, at its core, is a set of ways to remove that protection, either chemically with acid or surgically with an enzyme.

### Cheese: Bacteria start the process by turning sugar into acid

{{steps: Preparing the milk | pasteurisation | starter culture | acidification | >the pH meter tracks every stage}}
On screen, a steel vat of milk and a probe dipped into it. Most industrial cheese begins with pasteurisation, a brief, controlled heating of the milk. The word honours Louis Pasteur, who founded the Institut Pasteur in Paris. That heat kills most of the microbes already in the milk, including ones a cheesemaker might have wanted, so the next step puts chosen ones back. This is the starter culture: lactic acid bacteria, measured out and stirred in. They consume lactose, the sugar in milk, and release lactic acid. That acidification is slow and invisible, so it is followed with an instrument. The pH meter tracks every stage, and cheesemakers log its readings as carefully as temperatures.

{{chain: Measuring acidity | pH meter | falling pH | >casein loses its charge}}
The reason acid matters is electrical. As the pH meter reads a falling pH, more hydrogen ions are available to neutralise the negative charges on casein, and the calcium phosphate holding each micelle together begins to dissolve. Work published by the American Chemical Society and others places the isoelectric point of casein near pH 4.6, the acidity at which its net charge is close to zero. There, casein loses its charge and the micelles stop repelling one another. Some cheeses are made almost entirely this way. Paneer and many fresh cheeses rely on heat and acid rather than an enzyme, which is part of why they stay soft, do not melt into strings, and are not aged. Acid alone can make a curd. It just makes a particular kind of curd.

### Cheese: Cutting and cooking decide how much water stays

{{steps: From gel to curd | cutting | stirring | cooking | draining | >smaller curds release more whey}}
Now the gel is cut, on screen with a frame of wire knives drawn through the vat. Cutting creates surface, and surface is where whey escapes. Then comes stirring, which keeps the pieces from fusing back together, and cooking, a gentle warming that makes the curds contract. Finally, draining separates the curds from the whey. The rule a cheesemaker works by is simple: smaller curds release more whey, and more whey lost means a harder, drier, longer-keeping cheese. Large, barely cut curds give soft cheeses. Small curds, cooked warmer, give hard ones.

{{contrast: syneresis | is=the gel contracting and squeezing out whey | not=water evaporating from the curd}}
The process has a name: syneresis. It is the gel contracting and squeezing out whey, not water evaporating from the curd. The protein network keeps tightening as acid builds and temperature rises, and it pushes liquid out the way a sponge does when you twist it. Most of the milk's volume leaves at this point. The whey carries away most of the lactose, the whey proteins, and the caseinomacropeptide that chymosin clipped off. What stays is mostly casein, fat trapped inside the network, and some of the water.

{{text: Cheddaring and stretching}}
Two techniques, cheddaring and stretching, add a step here that changes texture entirely. In cheddaring, slabs of curd are stacked and turned so their own weight presses out whey while the acid continues to rise; the curd becomes dense and fibrous, almost like cooked chicken. For mozzarella, acidified curd is worked in hot water until the proteins line up into long strands. Both depend on reaching the right acidity first, which is why the pH meter appears again at this stage. Too little acid and the curd will not stretch. Too much and it crumbles.

### Cheese: Salt and pressing fix the shape

{{define: salting | drawing out water and slowing starter bacteria | steers ripening, does not stop it | American Chemical Society}}
Salt goes in next, either rubbed onto the curd, mixed through it, or applied by floating the formed cheese in brine. Salting does several jobs at once, drawing out water and slowing starter bacteria so acidification does not run too far, and it shapes which microbes can grow later. Salt steers ripening, it does not stop it, a balance described in dairy chemistry published by the American Chemical Society. Pressing then knits the curds into a single body. On screen, moulds are filled and stacked under weights. The pressure, how long it lasts, and the temperature all affect how closed or open the final texture is, which is why two cheeses from the same milk can look completely different when cut.

### Cheese: Ripening is chemistry, not storage

{{chain: Reading flavour | gas chromatography | mass spectrometry | >volatile compounds identified}}
A freshly pressed cheese tastes mild. Flavour arrives during ripening, and ripening is chemistry. Enzymes from the rennet, from the milk and from bacteria break proteins into peptides and then into amino acids. Other enzymes break fat into fatty acids, which give sharp and peppery notes. To find out which molecules are responsible, researchers warm a sample, capture the vapour above it, and separate it by gas chromatography, then identify each peak by mass spectrometry. That pairing is how volatile compounds identified in an aged cheese are matched to particular smells, often with a trained person sniffing the instrument's outlet at the same moment.

{{checklist: What ripening changes | +proteins broken into peptides | +fat broken into fatty acids | ?which compound defines a flavour}}
Microbes add the signatures you can see. In Swiss-type cheeses, Propionibacterium freudenreichii consumes lactate and releases carbon dioxide, and the gas gathers into the round eyes. Blue cheeses are pierced so Penicillium roqueforti can grow in veins through the paste. On the surface of brie and camembert, Penicillium camemberti forms the white rind and softens the cheese from the outside inwards. The chemistry confirms that proteins broken into peptides and fat broken into fatty acids are central everywhere. What it does not yet settle is which compound defines a flavour, because aroma comes from mixtures, and the same molecule can read differently depending on what surrounds it.

### Cheese: The oldest cheesemaking is known from fat, not recipes

{{stat: 2012 | year | Nature reported pottery sieves | Nature}}
Cheese is far older than any written recipe, and the evidence for that is chemical too. In 2012, a paper in Nature reported pottery sieves from sites in Poland, dated to the sixth millennium BC. Perforated vessels like these had long been suspected of being cheese strainers, but shape alone could not prove it. Researchers extracted fat residues absorbed into the clay and measured their carbon isotope ratios, which separate milk fat from the fat of meat. The sieves carried milk fat. That made them some of the earliest direct evidence of cheesemaking yet found. The method is the point: no one read about this cheese, they measured what it left behind.

### Cheese: Evidence limit

{{checklist: What this evidence settles | +curd formation | -any particular cheese | ?how compounds combine into flavour}}
The chemistry of curd formation is well established, down to the single bond chymosin cuts. It does not explain any particular cheese. Two dairies can follow the same steps and produce noticeably different results, because milk itself varies with animal, feed and season, and the microbial community in a ripening room is not fully characterised. Residue chemistry shows that people strained milk through pottery sieves; it does not tell us what that cheese tasted like, how it was salted, or whether it was the first. And flavour analysis can list compounds without fully explaining how compounds combine into flavour on your tongue.

### Cheese: Closing

{{ambient}}
Cheese begins with one broken bond, and then a short list follows: acid, heat, salt, pressure and time. The first steps are chemistry you can write down to a single amino acid position. The last one is still being read, peak by peak, from the air above a slice of cheese.
