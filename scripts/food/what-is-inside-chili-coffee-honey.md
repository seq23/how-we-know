### Title card

Chili, Coffee, Honey. What's Inside?

### Chili: Why do chili peppers taste hot?

Why do chili peppers taste hot?

{{stat: 43 | degrees Celsius | the heat threshold of the receptor capsaicin opens | Nature}}
A thermometer on your tongue reads normal while the burn builds. Chili peppers taste hot because capsaicin binds to TRPV1, the same receptor on pain-sensing nerves that normally fires when tissue is heated above about 43 degrees Celsius. The chili never warms your mouth; the nerve only reports that it does. That threshold comes from the work that identified the receptor, published in the journal Nature in 1997. It settles less than it seems to. It explains why the burn feels like heat. It does not explain why a plant would make such a molecule, why some animals eat chilies without flinching, or how anyone put a number on a sensation that exists only inside a nervous system.

### Chili: Heat is a nerve signal, not a taste

{{text: where the burn is not}}
Start with where the burn is not. Taste buds carry receptors for a small set of tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and savoury. Heat is not on that list, and the evidence for that needs no instrument. Chili juice on the skin of a hand, or near an eye, produces the same burn in tissue that has no taste buds at all. Whatever capsaicin is doing, it is not doing it through taste. That simple observation was the first clue, and it pointed researchers away from the tongue's taste machinery entirely.

{{contrast: chili heat | is=chemesthesis | not=taste}}
So the sensation travels by a different route. In the mouth, the burn is carried largely by the trigeminal nerve, the same nerve that reports touch, temperature and pain across the face. Researchers call this family of sensations chemesthesis: chemicals triggering senses that normally respond to physical events. The sting of mustard and the cool of mint belong here too. Chili heat is chemesthesis, not taste, and that distinction told researchers where to look. The answer would be found on a nerve ending, not inside a taste bud.

### Chili: The receptor was found by fishing with the molecule itself

{{steps: Fishing for the receptor | a library of genes | cells that normally ignore capsaicin | watched for calcium | >until one gene remains}}
For decades, physiologists knew capsaicin excited a particular class of pain-sensing neurons, but nobody knew what it actually bound to. The laboratory of David Julius approached the problem by fishing with the molecule itself. The team built a library of genes active in sensory neurons, then put pools of those genes into cells that normally ignore capsaicin. They added capsaicin and watched for calcium flooding into the cells, made visible by a dye that glows when calcium rises. A pool that lit up contained the gene they wanted. Split the pool, test again, and keep going until one gene remains.

{{define: TRPV1 | an ion channel | it does not respond to cold | Nature}}
The gene they isolated encoded an ion channel, a pore in the nerve cell's membrane that opens to let charged particles through. They called it the vanilloid receptor, after a chemical group capsaicin shares with vanillin, the main flavour molecule of vanilla. Today it is known as TRPV1. The paper, published in Nature in 1997, reported something that turned the whole question around. The channel did not only open for capsaicin. It also opened for heat.

### Chili: The nerve cannot tell the molecule from real heat

{{chain: Why the burn feels like heat | capsaicin binds TRPV1 | the channel opens | the nerve fires | >the brain reports heat}}
In the same experiments, cells carrying TRPV1 responded when the surrounding solution was warmed above about 43 degrees Celsius, a temperature at which contact begins to register as painful. That is the load-bearing fact. A nerve ending has one way to signal: it fires or it stays quiet. When capsaicin binds TRPV1, the channel opens, the nerve fires, and the brain receives the same message a scald would send. It has no separate line that says this came from a molecule. So the brain reports heat. Nothing in a chili warms your mouth, and no thermometer would ever show the burn.

{{checklist: What TRPV1 responds to | +capsaicin | +heat | +acidity | -cold}}
Later work filled in the receptor's other sensitivities. Alongside capsaicin and heat, TRPV1 also responds to acidity. It does not respond to cold; the cooling of menthol works through a different channel. And the receptor sits mostly on pain-sensing nerves, which is why the chili sensation arrives as a burn rather than a pleasant warmth. Capsaicin also explains the lingering. It is an oily molecule that dissolves poorly in water, so it clings to tissue and keeps the channel busy after the food is gone.

{{chain: The knockout test | lacked the TRPV1 gene | offered capsaicin water | drank it as if it were plain water | >a mechanism}}
Identifying a receptor is not the same as proving it is the one that matters. The decisive test came from mice, in work published in the journal Science in 2000. Researchers bred animals that lacked the TRPV1 gene and offered them capsaicin water, ordinary drinking water laced with the molecule. Normal mice avoid it. The mice without the receptor drank it as if it were plain water. Their response to high heat was also dulled, though not erased, which hinted that other heat sensors work alongside TRPV1. That combination, a molecule that opens a channel in a dish and an animal that stops caring once the channel is gone, is what turns a candidate into a mechanism.

### Chili: The plant puts the molecule exactly where it does the most work

{{cross_section: chili fruit | the placenta}}
A pepper plant fits that description. It is not a passive bowl of flavour; it is running chemistry. Capsaicin is made in the placenta, the pale tissue inside the fruit that holds the seeds. The seeds themselves make little of it, but they sit pressed against the placenta and pick it up, which is why the pith carries more heat than the outer flesh. Capsaicin is the best known of a small family of related compounds called capsaicinoids. PubChem, the public chemical database maintained by the National Institutes of Health, records its makeup: eighteen carbon atoms, twenty-seven hydrogens, one nitrogen and three oxygens.

{{contrast: bell pepper | is=zero heat | not=a mild chili}}
Bell peppers belong to the same species as many hot chilies, yet a bell pepper registers zero heat. It is not a mild chili. It does not make capsaicin at all. That single difference, a working pathway or a broken one, shows that heat is a property of the plant's chemistry, not of the species name, the shape of the fruit or its colour. Flavour and heat are separate traits, and a plant can keep one while losing the other.

### Chili: Birds carry the seeds because the alarm stays silent for them

{{text: directed deterrence}}
The obvious next question is why a plant would spend resources making this molecule. The leading explanation is called directed deterrence, and it comes from feeding studies, including one published in Nature, that watched which animals ate wild chilies. Rodents avoided the fruit. Birds ate it freely. That split matters because the two groups treat seeds differently. Rodents chew and digest seeds, destroying them. Birds tend to pass seeds intact and drop them far from the parent plant, which is exactly what a plant needs.

{{contrast: capsaicin for birds | is=largely silent | not=a deterrent}}
The receptor explains the split. Later work from the Julius laboratory compared the bird version of TRPV1 with the mammalian one. The bird channel still responds to heat, but it barely responds to capsaicin. For birds the molecule is largely silent, not a deterrent. So capsaicin works as a filter: it deters the animals that would destroy the seeds and stays quiet for the animals that spread them. The same molecule, meeting two versions of one channel, produces two completely different experiences.

{{checklist: What capsaicin may do | +deters mammals | ?slows fungi}}
Capsaicin may have more than one job. Field work on wild chilies has also suggested that it slows fungi that attack seeds, and that wild plants in wetter, fungus-heavy places tend to be hotter. That second role is less settled than the first. The firm part is narrow: it deters mammals. It may also slow fungi. Both are inferences about evolutionary pressure, and no experiment can rerun the history that produced them.

### Chili: Heat was measured by tongues before it was measured by machines

{{timeline: 1912=Wilbur Scoville | 1997=TRPV1}}
Long before anyone knew the receptor existed, people wanted to rank chilies. In 1912 a pharmacist named Wilbur Scoville published a method that used the only detector available, human tongues. An extract of dried pepper was diluted in sugar water, step by step, until a panel of tasters could no longer detect any heat. The amount of dilution needed became the score, now called Scoville heat units. A pepper that needed more dilution was hotter. It was clever, and it was entirely subjective. Not until the TRPV1 work of 1997 did anyone know what those tasters were actually detecting.

{{steps: From tongues to instruments | sugar water | a panel of tasters | high-performance liquid chromatography | >converted back into Scoville heat units}}
The weakness is built in. Tongues tire, people differ, and a panel's threshold drifts from day to day. So laboratories now measure capsaicinoids directly with high-performance liquid chromatography, which separates the compounds in an extract and quantifies each one. The result is a concentration, a chemical fact rather than a sensation. That concentration is then converted back into Scoville heat units, so the familiar scale survives even though the sugar water and the panel of tasters are gone.

{{contrast: Scoville heat units | is=a concentration | not=a measurement of experience}}
On that converted scale, pure capsaicin sits at the very top, in the millions, and the exact figure quoted for it depends on the conversion used. Treat any such number as a convention rather than a measurement of experience. Scoville heat units now report a concentration of a molecule. They do not measure how much a given person feels, which depends on their receptors, their history of exposure and the food the chili is cooked into.

### Chili: Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +the receptor | +the heat threshold | -how much a given person feels | ?why chilies evolved capsaicin}}
Here is what this evidence does not establish. The receptor work settles the mechanism of the burn and the heat threshold behind it; it does not measure how much a given person feels, which varies and is shaped by exposure. Published Scoville figures for named varieties span wide ranges, because a plant's heat changes with growing conditions, so any single number for a variety is a midpoint, not a fact about every fruit. The 43 degree threshold is an approximate value from cells in a dish, and the channel's sensitivity shifts with its chemical surroundings. And the account of why chilies evolved capsaicin is the best supported inference, not a recorded event.

### Chili: Closing

{{ambient}}
A chili is a message written in the wrong language on purpose. The molecule speaks to a channel built to detect heat, and the nerve, with only one way to answer, says burn. Nothing is warming. You are hearing a plant's alarm, set for mammals, ignored by birds, and read by your brain as fire.

### Coffee: Why does coffee taste bitter?

Why does coffee taste bitter?

{{stat: 25 | types of bitter receptor | the human count | NIH}}
Take the caffeine out of a cup, and most of the bitterness stays. Coffee tastes bitter mainly because roasting breaks the bean's chlorogenic acids into new bitter molecules, which bind bitter taste receptors on the tongue; caffeine contributes, but only part of the effect. Those receptors are a family, not a single sensor. Research supported by the NIH puts the human count at about 25 types of bitter receptor. That number tells you the tongue has many locks. It does not tell you which keys a roasted bean is carrying, or how anyone found out. That second part is the story, and it starts with an awkward fact: decaf still bites, so something else must be doing the work.

### Coffee: Decaf is the experiment that rules caffeine out

{{contrast: caffeine | is=a contributor | not=the main source}}
Start with the cheapest experiment in food science: a cup of decaffeinated coffee. Decaffeination removes most of the caffeine from green beans before they are roasted, and the result is still unmistakably bitter. If caffeine were the main source, decaf should taste noticeably flat. It does not. Caffeine is genuinely bitter on its own. Dissolve the pure compound in water and you get a clean, sharp bitterness. So caffeine is a contributor, but it is not the main source. Coffee stays bitter without caffeine, because its bitterness was never mostly caffeine to begin with.

{{define: caffeine | a bitter alkaloid in the raw bean | its bitterness does not change much with roasting | PubChem}}
Stability is the second clue. Caffeine is a bitter alkaloid in the raw bean, and it is fairly heat-stable, so its bitterness does not change much with roasting. PubChem, the chemical database run by the National Institutes of Health, records its structure: a small, ring-shaped molecule built from carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and oxygen. Yet anyone who has compared a pale roast with a very dark one knows the bitterness changes a great deal. A molecule whose amount barely moves cannot explain a taste that moves that much. Whatever drives the difference has to be made, or destroyed, in the roaster.

### Coffee: Bitterness is built in the roaster, not grown on the tree

{{cross_section: green coffee bean | chlorogenic acids}}
A green coffee bean, taken from the fruit and dried, tastes grassy and astringent rather than like coffee. Among its most abundant soluble compounds is a group called chlorogenic acids. Despite the name, they have nothing to do with chlorine. Each one is an ester, a joining of caffeic acid, or a close relative, with another molecule called quinic acid. PubChem holds entries for members of the family, with their structures. In the raw bean they are more astringent than bitter. They are better thought of as raw material, waiting for heat.

{{kitchen: 200 | MAILLARD}}
Then comes the heat. The American Chemical Society, describing the chemistry of a roast, puts the beans above 200 degrees Celsius. At those temperatures the bean behaves like a small pressure vessel. Water flashes to steam, sugars and amino acids run through Maillard reactions that make brown pigments and aroma, and the chlorogenic acids start to come apart. Some of their mass simply breaks down. Some of it is rebuilt into molecules that were never in the raw bean. Roasting is not cooking the bitterness out. It is cooking it in.

### Coffee: A light roast and a dark roast are bitter for different reasons

{{steps: From acid to bitterness | chlorogenic acids | chlorogenic acid lactones | phenylindanes | >harsher with darker roasts}}
The path from acid to bitterness runs in stages. The first is a loss of water inside the molecule. When a chlorogenic acid loses water, its quinic acid portion closes into a tighter ring, and the products are called chlorogenic acid lactones. Lactones taste distinctly bitter, and research reported by the American Chemical Society identified them as major bitter compounds in light to medium roasts. Push the roast further, and the lactones themselves break down. Their caffeic acid portions are freed, fragment, and link together into compounds called phenylindanes. Those carry a harsher, more lingering bitterness, and they accumulate in dark roasts. So the bitterness is harsher with darker roasts, and it carries a different chemical signature.

{{checklist: What the roast pathway explains | +darker roasts taste more bitter | +decaf stays bitter | ?the exact share of each compound}}
Notice what this explains at once. It explains why darker roasts taste more bitter, since the pathway keeps generating harsher products as the heat continues. It explains why decaf stays bitter, because decaffeination targets caffeine and leaves the chlorogenic acids behind to be transformed in the roaster. What it does not hand you is the exact share of each compound in a given cup. That depends on the bean, the roast, and the brew, and published figures vary from one study to the next.

### Coffee: The culprits were named by tasting fractions, not by guessing

{{chain: sensory-guided analysis | liquid chromatography | tasting panels | mass spectrometry | >the structure is confirmed}}
How do chemists know which of the many molecules in a cup is bitter? A brewed coffee contains hundreds of compounds, and an instrument alone cannot taste anything. The method used is often called sensory-guided analysis. First, liquid chromatography separates the brew into fractions, each holding a narrower set of molecules. Then trained tasting panels sample each fraction, diluting it step by step until the bitterness disappears. A fraction that stays bitter through many dilutions holds something potent. Only then do the instruments take over, and mass spectrometry weighs the molecules and their fragments, while nuclear magnetic resonance maps how their atoms connect, until the structure is confirmed.

{{define: dilution threshold | the weakest concentration at which a trained panel still detects bitterness | it varies between tasters | Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry}}
The next step closes the loop. Once a structure is proposed, chemists make the compound from scratch, purify it, and taste the synthetic version. If it reproduces the bitterness at the expected strength, the identification holds. A key measure here is the dilution threshold, the weakest concentration at which a trained panel still detects bitterness. Comparing that threshold with how much of the compound is actually in a cup tells you whether it matters, or is merely present. This kind of work fills the pages of the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, published by the American Chemical Society. The threshold is not a law of nature: it varies between tasters, which is why panels are trained and results are averaged.

{{text}}
There is a stricter test still, and it is the closest thing this field has to a controlled experiment. Researchers rebuild the cup. They take each identified compound at the concentration measured in a real brew, dissolve the set in water, and ask a panel to compare the mixture with the coffee it came from. If the rebuilt version tastes about as bitter, the list is reasonably complete. Then they leave compounds out, one group at a time. When removing a group makes the mixture noticeably less bitter, that group is pulling its weight. Omission tests of this kind are how caffeine's modest role was measured, rather than assumed.

### Coffee: Bitter is a verdict reached by receptors

{{cross_section: taste bud | bitter receptors}}
Molecules are only half of the story. Bitterness is something a cell decides. On the tongue, and on the soft palate, taste buds hold receptor cells, and some of those cells carry bitter receptors. These are proteins that span the cell membrane. When the right molecule docks on the outside, the receptor changes shape and triggers a signal inside the cell, and a nerve carries the message on. As research supported by the NIH has described, humans carry about 25 types of bitter receptor, and many of them respond to several different molecules.

{{steps: receptor assay | receptor gene | cultured cells | calcium signal | >which molecule activates which receptor}}
The way scientists learned which molecule triggers which receptor is not by tasting. The method is a receptor assay. They take a single receptor gene and insert it into cultured cells that do not normally taste anything. They load those cells with a dye that glows when calcium floods in, which is what happens when the receptor fires, producing a calcium signal. Then they add a test compound. If the cells light up, that receptor responds. Repeating this across the family maps which molecule activates which receptor. Caffeine, tested this way, activates several of them. Roast compounds can be screened the same way, though fewer have been fully mapped.

{{contrast: bitterness | is=a response in the taster | not=a fixed property of the molecule}}
This is why bitterness is a response in the taster, not a fixed property of the molecule. The receptor genes vary between people. Some versions of a receptor bind a given compound strongly, others weakly, and some people barely detect certain bitter molecules at all. The same cup can register as harsh to one person and mild to another, without either of them being wrong. The chemistry in the cup is the same. The sensors are not.

### Coffee: Brewing decides how much of the bitterness reaches you

{{chain: extraction | a finer grind | longer contact time | hotter water | >over-extraction}}
The roaster makes the bitter molecules. The brew decides how many of them leave the grounds. Hot water is a solvent, and different compounds dissolve at different rates. Bright acids and many aroma compounds come out early. Some of the heavier, more bitter roast products come out more slowly. So anything that pushes extraction further, whether a finer grind, longer contact time or hotter water, tends to pull more bitter compounds into the cup. Coffee professionals call the extreme over-extraction, and its usual signature is a dry, lingering bitterness.

{{text}}
There is also what goes in afterwards. Sugar does not remove a single bitter molecule from the cup. It lowers how bitter the cup seems, because sweet and bitter signals interact as the brain combines taste information. Milk is a different case. Proteins in milk can bind some of coffee's polyphenols, and fat changes how compounds spread across the tongue, though how much each effect matters in a real cup is still debated. In both cases the bitterness is changed in perception, not deleted from the chemistry.

### Coffee: Evidence limit

{{checklist: What remains open | +roasting creates bitter compounds | -a fixed recipe for bitterness | ?the share from melanoidins}}
This evidence does not establish a fixed recipe for bitterness. Most studies work on particular beans, roasted in particular ways, brewed by particular methods, and the proportions they report do not transfer cleanly to every cup. The contribution of other roast products is less settled. Melanoidins, the large brown polymers built during roasting, are proposed as contributors, but the share from melanoidins is hard to pin down because they are too large and varied to isolate cleanly. Receptor maps come from cells in dishes, not from a living mouth, where saliva, aroma and temperature all intervene. What is firm is narrower: roasting creates bitter compounds that the raw bean lacks, and caffeine alone cannot account for the taste.

### Coffee: Closing

{{ambient}}
A green bean holds the raw material. Heat rebuilds it into molecules the plant never made, water carries some of them out, and a family of receptors renders its verdict. The bitterness in the cup is not one substance. It is a record of the roast.

### Honey: Why does honey never spoil?

Why does honey never spoil?

{{stat: 18.6 | percent | moisture cap for the top grades of extracted honey | USDA}}
Add a little water to honey, and yeast can start eating it. That is the clue. Honey resists spoiling because it holds so little water, and so much sugar, that microbes cannot draw enough water to grow. On screen is the figure that sets the line in practice. USDA grade standards for extracted honey cap the moisture of the top grades at 18.6 percent. That number does not describe a preservative that someone added. It describes something that is missing. And it overturns a familiar assumption. Honey is not immortal by nature. It stays protected only as long as it stays dry.

### Honey: Microbes starve for water, not for food

{{define: water activity | how much of the water in a food is free to move and react | pure water sits at 1, a completely dry material sits near 0 | American Chemical Society}}
To a bacterium, a jar of honey looks like an enormous meal. It is almost entirely sugar, the fuel most microbes prefer. The problem is not food. The problem is water. Food scientists measure that with a quantity called water activity, the term now on screen. Food chemists, in research published by the American Chemical Society, treat it as the water in a food that is free to move and take part in reactions, rather than the total amount present. The scale is simple. Pure water sits at 1, and a completely dry material sits near 0. Every microbe has a floor on that scale below which it cannot grow. Most bacteria need conditions close to the wet end. Some molds and specialised yeasts can manage drier ground, but each still has a limit.

{{contrast: water content | is=how much water is present | not=how much water is available}}
Here is the distinction that matters, and the screen splits it in two. Water content is how much water is present. Water activity is how much of that water is actually available. Honey contains a fair amount of water by weight. But nearly all of it is held tightly by sugar molecules, which bind to water through hydrogen bonds. A water molecule clinging to a sugar is not free to cross into a cell. So a food can be wet to the touch and still be a desert to a microbe. Honey is exactly that kind of desert. Its water activity sits well below the line most bacteria need, although the published values vary from sample to sample and from one floral source to another.

{{chain: measuring water activity | sealed chamber | dew point | >water activity}}
How is that known, rather than assumed? On screen is the instrument chain. A small sample of honey goes into a sealed chamber. Water escapes from the sample into the air above it until the two reach balance. The instrument then measures the humidity of that trapped air, commonly by cooling a tiny mirror until dew forms on it. The temperature of that dew point tells you how much water vapour is present, and from that the device reports water activity. Total moisture, the figure in the grade standards, is usually measured a different way. A refractometer reads how strongly the honey bends light, and because sugar and water bend light differently, that reading converts to moisture. Two instruments, two different questions, one consistent picture.

### Honey: Bees do the drying before you ever see the jar

{{steps: from nectar to honey | collect nectar | add enzymes | fan to evaporate | cap with wax | >moisture falls before sealing}}
That dryness is manufactured, and the screen walks through the stages. Worker bees collect nectar, which is far wetter than finished honey. Back in the hive, they add enzymes from their own glands. One of them, invertase, splits sucrose into its two simpler sugars, glucose and fructose. Then the drying begins. Bees spread the liquid thinly through the comb and fan their wings, driving a current of warm air across the cells. Water evaporates. Only when the honey has thickened enough do the bees cap each cell with wax. The moisture falls before the seal goes on, not after.

{{text: The wax cap is a sign the drying is done}}
Beekeepers read that sign. A frame with most of its cells capped is usually ripe enough to harvest, and many beekeepers confirm it with a handheld refractometer before extracting. Honey taken too early, from open cells, can carry too much water. That is exactly the honey most likely to go wrong later. So the stability you find in a jar is partly the work of the colony and partly the judgement of whoever harvested it. Nothing about the sugar alone guarantees it. The process does.

### Honey: Sugar pulls water out of a living cell

{{cross_section: a bacterial cell in honey | water drawn outward through the membrane}}
Now follow a single microbe into the jar. The glowing cross-section shows a bacterial cell with a thin membrane around it. Inside, the cell's contents are watery. Outside, the honey is a concentrated sugar solution. Water moves across a membrane from the side where it is more available toward the side where it is less available. This is osmosis. So instead of the cell drawing water in, water is drawn outward through the membrane, into the honey. The cell shrinks. Its internal machinery, which depends on water to function, slows and stops. Many cells simply cannot recover.

{{contrast: osmotic stress | is=water leaving the cell | not=a poison}}
It is worth being exact about what is doing the work here, and the screen holds both sides. Osmotic stress is water leaving the cell. It is not a poison. Glucose and fructose, whose structures are catalogued by PubChem, are ordinary sugars that cells use for energy every day. Diluted, they would feed the very organisms they now suppress. That is why the same substance can be a preservative at one concentration and a growth medium at another. The protection is a property of the mixture, not of any single molecule in it.

### Honey: Acid and peroxide add a second wall

{{chain: glucose oxidase | glucose | gluconic acid | hydrogen peroxide | >a second barrier}}
Dryness is the main defence, but it is not the only one, and the chain on screen shows the next. Bees add an enzyme called glucose oxidase. It acts on glucose and turns it into gluconic acid, releasing hydrogen peroxide as a by-product. Gluconic acid is the main acid in honey, and it is a large part of why honey is acidic. Many bacteria grow poorly in acidic conditions. Hydrogen peroxide, also catalogued by PubChem, is reactive and damages cells. Together they form a second barrier behind the first.

{{sources: composition of honey | USDA Agricultural Research Service=surveys of honey composition | PubChem=structures of gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide}}
There is a subtlety in that chemistry, and it comes from careful measurement rather than intuition. Composition surveys from the USDA's Agricultural Research Service, and laboratory work since, found that glucose oxidase is fairly quiet in full-strength honey. It works much more actively once honey is diluted. That means the peroxide defence is strongest at precisely the moment the dryness defence weakens. It is a fallback, not a front line. The acidity, meanwhile, is real but variable. Published values differ across floral sources, so no single number describes every honey.

### Honey: The protection fails where water returns

{{checklist: what keeps honey stable | +low water | +concentrated sugar | +acidity | -protection against absorbed moisture | ?how long flavour lasts}}
The checklist on screen sorts what honey has from what it lacks. It has low water. It has concentrated sugar. It has acidity. What it does not have is protection against absorbed moisture. Honey is hygroscopic, meaning it pulls water vapour out of the air. Leave a jar open in a humid kitchen and the surface layer slowly takes on water. The sugar near the top is diluted, and the water activity there rises.

{{text: Fermentation is how honey spoils}}
That is the failure point. Some yeasts are specialists in sugary, dry environments, and they are often already present in honey in small numbers, dormant. Give them a slightly wetter layer and they can wake up. They ferment the sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. The honey can foam, smell sour, and taste of alcohol. This is the same basic chemistry brewers use to make mead on purpose. So the honest answer to whether honey never spoils is that honey spoils readily, by fermentation, once its water rises. The claim of permanence is really a claim about a sealed, dry jar.

### Honey: Crystals and darkening are change, not decay

{{contrast: crystallization | is=glucose coming out of solution | not=spoilage}}
Many people throw out honey that is not spoiled at all. The screen shows why. Over time, a lot of honey turns cloudy and grainy. This is crystallization, glucose coming out of solution as solid crystals. Glucose dissolves less readily than fructose, so honeys richer in glucose tend to crystallize sooner. It is not spoilage. The sugar is the same sugar, arranged differently. There is one catch worth knowing. As glucose locks into crystals, the remaining liquid holds a little more of the water, which can raise the fermentation risk in that liquid portion.

{{text: Change is not the same as spoilage}}
Stored honey also changes chemically, even sealed and dry. Its colour gradually darkens and its flavour shifts, especially when it is warmed. One of the compounds that builds up is hydroxymethylfurfural, a breakdown product of sugars in acidic conditions, also catalogued by PubChem. Laboratories track it with liquid chromatography, which separates the compounds in a sample so each can be measured. Rising levels indicate that honey has been heated or stored for a long time. That makes it a quality marker, not proof of microbial decay. A jar can be old, darker, and stronger tasting, and still be free of anything growing in it.

### Honey: What to notice in the edit

{{ambient}}
As the sequence plays out, notice what the visuals never do. No graphic shows honey as a substance that kills on contact, because that is not the mechanism. The cross-section shows water moving, not a toxin acting. The checklist keeps one line unticked, because the protection really does have a gap. And the instruments on screen, the dew point chamber, the refractometer, the chromatograph, each answer a different question. Stability is not one measurement. It is several measurements agreeing.

### Honey: Evidence limit

{{checklist: what the evidence settles | +why microbes cannot grow in dry honey | +how honey ferments when wet | -how long any particular jar stays unchanged | -the age claims in popular stories}}
Here is what this evidence does not establish. It explains why microbes cannot grow in dry honey, and how honey ferments when wet. It does not tell you how long any particular jar will stay unchanged, because moisture, floral source, storage temperature and handling all vary. It also does not settle the popular stories of edible honey recovered from ancient tombs, repeated widely, including in a Smithsonian feature on honey's shelf life. Those accounts are mostly anecdotal, and the published record of laboratory analysis on such samples is thin. And nothing here is a statement about what is safe to eat. It describes chemistry in a jar.

### Honey: Closing

{{ambient}}
So the honey on your shelf is not immortal. It is an engineered scarcity, a liquid made so short of free water that life cannot get a grip on it. Bees build that condition, and a sealed lid keeps it. Take the water back, and the oldest preservation trick in the kitchen quietly undoes itself.
