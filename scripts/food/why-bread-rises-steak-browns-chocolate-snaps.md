## YouTube title

6 crystals, a breathing fungus and a crust you never see

## Direct-answer lock

Bread rises because living yeast breathes carbon dioxide into a web of gluten, steak browns because sugars and amino acids react on its hot, dry surface, and chocolate snaps because its fat is set in one chosen crystal, form five. Each answer was found with an instrument that never tasted anything: Pasteur's microscope in 1857, the gas chromatograph that pulls apart a crust's smell, and the X-ray diffraction that told six crystals apart in 1966.

## Narration

### Cold open

{{stat: 6 | crystal forms | one of them snaps | Wille and Lutton, 1966}}
Your chocolate can set six different ways, and only one of them snaps. Your bread is full of holes that something alive breathed out. And the best part of a steak is a reaction that only happens on its outside. Bread rises because living yeast breathes carbon dioxide into a web of gluten. Steak browns because sugars and amino acids react on its hot, dry surface. Chocolate snaps because its fat is set in one chosen crystal, called form five.

{{timeline: 1857=Pasteur and the yeast | 1912=Maillard and the crust | 1966=six crystals of cocoa butter}}
None of this was found by tasting. It was found with instruments that never tasted anything. A microscope in 1857, when Louis Pasteur argued that fermentation is the work of living cells. A flask in 1912, when Louis-Camille Maillard heated sugars with amino acids and watched them brown. And a beam of X-rays in 1966, which told six crystals of cocoa butter apart. Three questions, three instruments, and three things in your kitchen that run on chemistry you never see.

{{contrast: how it was known | is=instruments that measure | not=a cook's taste}}
Taste was where every one of these questions started. A baker knew a good rise, a cook knew a good crust, and a chocolate maker knew a good snap, for centuries, without knowing why. The why had to wait for a way to look inside: at a single cell, at a single molecule, at the way fat stacks itself into a crystal.
