### Chocolate: Why does chocolate melt in your mouth but not in your hand?

Why does chocolate melt in your mouth but not in your hand?

{{stat: 6 | crystal forms | cocoa butter can set six ways | Wille and Lutton, 1966}}
The same chocolate can set six different ways, and only one of them snaps. That one is called form five. It is the crystal your chocolate is supposed to be made of. Form five melts at about thirty-four degrees Celsius, just below the temperature inside your mouth. That is the whole trick. Cocoa butter, the fat in chocolate, sits solid in a cool room, holds on in your hand, and gives way on your tongue, because one crystal was chosen for it. The question is how anyone could see a crystal in a chocolate bar at all.

### Chocolate: The fat is the part that decides

{{define: cocoa butter | the fat pressed from cacao beans | sets solid as crystals | PubChem}}
Look at the bar on the screen. Most of what holds it together is not sugar. It is cocoa butter, the pale fat pressed out of cacao beans. Sugar and cocoa solids are suspended in that fat, the way sand is held in wax. So when chocolate melts, it is really the fat that melts. Everything else simply lets go. Hold on to that idea, because it is the key to the whole question.

{{text}}
Cocoa butter is made mostly of molecules called triglycerides. Each one is a small backbone, glycerol, carrying three long chains, the fatty acids. In cocoa butter the two outer chains are usually saturated, palmitic acid or stearic acid. The middle chain is usually oleic acid. That pattern is unusually tidy. Most fats are a messy crowd of different shapes. Cocoa butter is a crowd of near-identical ones.

{{ambient}}
Tidy molecules can stack. When warm cocoa butter cools, those triglycerides line up and lock together into crystals, layer on layer, like folded chairs in a stack. And because they are so alike, they tend to let go together, over a narrow band of temperature, rather than slowly softening the way butter does.

### Chocolate: One fat, six ways to be solid

{{cross_section: chocolate bar | form five crystals}}
Here is the surprise. Those molecules can stack in more than one way. Packed loosely, they make a crystal that melts easily. Packed tightly, they make a crystal that needs more heat. Cocoa butter has six of these arrangements. Chemists number them from one to six, loosest to tightest. Picture the bar cut open, with the form five crystals glowing in the break.

{{timeline: 1966=six forms named | 1966=form V melts near 34 C}}
In 1966, two chemists, Wille and Lutton, published a careful study of cocoa butter in the journal of the American Oil Chemists' Society. They cooled and warmed samples under controlled conditions and sorted the solid fat into six forms. They reported a melting point for each. Form one melted at about seventeen degrees Celsius. Form five at about thirty-three point eight. Form six at about thirty-six point three.

{{kitchen: 34 | form five melts}}
So form five melts at about thirty-four degrees Celsius. It sits just under the warmth of a living body. Solid on the shelf. Solid, for a while, in your fingers. Liquid in your mouth.

### Chocolate: How you see a crystal you cannot see

{{chain: X-ray diffraction | X-rays through the fat | layers scatter the beam | rings of different spacing | >a fingerprint for each form}}
Nobody can see these crystals with their eyes. They are far too small. Wille and Lutton used X-ray diffraction. You shine a narrow beam of X-rays through the fat. The regularly spaced layers of molecules scatter the beam, and the scattered rays land on a detector as a pattern of rings. The spacing of the rings depends on how far apart the layers are.

{{text}}
A loose crystal leaves one pattern. A tight one leaves another. So each of the six forms has its own fingerprint. You do not need to see a crystal to know which one you have. You only need to read its rings.

{{ambient}}
On the screen, the chocolate keeps pouring. Somewhere inside a bar like this, layer on layer of molecules sit stacked in rows, far too fine to see, and a beam of X-rays can read them.

### Chocolate: Melting, measured as heat

{{steps: differential scanning calorimetry | a small sample and an empty pan | heat both at a steady rate | measure the extra heat | >a peak when a form melts}}
The second instrument measures melting directly. It is called differential scanning calorimetry. A few milligrams of fat sit in a tiny pan, beside an empty pan. Both are warmed at the same steady rate. The machine records how much extra heat the sample pan needs to keep pace.

{{ambient}}
While the fat is simply warming, the extra heat is small and steady. Then a crystal form reaches its melting point. Now the heat goes into breaking the crystal apart, not into raising the temperature. The sample pan falls behind, the machine pushes in more heat, and the trace rises into a peak. The peak sits at the melting point of that form. Its size tells you how much of that form was there.

{{contrast: melting | is=a peak of absorbed heat | not=a gradual softening}}
That is why good chocolate can feel faintly cool on the tongue. As form five melts in your mouth, it is drawing heat from you to do it, just as the sample pan drew heat from the machine.

### Chocolate: Why your hand is not your mouth

{{contrast: the hand | is=cooler skin on the outside of the bar | not=the warm, wet inside of a mouth}}
So why does the hand lose? Partly temperature. The skin of your palm usually runs cooler than the inside of your mouth. Partly contact. Your fingers touch only the outside of the bar, through dry skin. Your tongue wraps the piece in warm saliva and presses it against the roof of your mouth. Heat flows in from every side.

{{text}}
Hold a square long enough on a warm day and it will give way in your hand too. Form five is not refusing to melt. It is waiting for enough warmth, delivered fast enough, and a mouth delivers it far better than a palm.

{{ambient}}
Watch the chocolate on the screen running in slow folds. Above its melting point it has no crystal at all. It is only fat, sugar and cocoa, flowing, and waiting for something to tell it how to set.

### Chocolate: Tempering is choosing the crystal

{{steps: tempering | melt every crystal | cool until crystals form | warm a little | >only form five remains}}
Left to itself, cooling chocolate does not reliably choose form five. It sets as a mixture of looser forms, and the result is soft, dull and greasy to the touch. So chocolate makers temper it. They melt it completely, erasing every crystal. They cool it until crystals begin to form. Then they warm it slightly, just enough to melt the looser forms away, and leave form five behind as seeds.

{{ambient}}
Those seeds teach the rest of the chocolate how to set. Every new crystal grows on the pattern of the old ones. Watch the chocolate on the screen slowly fold and settle. When it cools, the whole bar is form five, all the way through.

### Chocolate: A melted bar never comes back the same

{{contrast: a bar that melted in a warm car | is=reset in the looser forms | not=back to form five}}
You may already have run this experiment by accident. A bar left in a warm car melts, and when it cools it sets again. But it does not come back the same. It is softer, duller, sometimes crumbly, and it smears on your fingers. Nothing was added and nothing was taken away. The fat simply set without a guide, and chose the looser forms.

{{text}}
That is the strongest everyday evidence that the crystal, not the recipe, decides how chocolate behaves. The same molecules, in the same proportions, packed a different way, make a different solid. To get the snap back, the whole bar would have to be melted again and tempered from the start.

{{ambient}}
Look at the melted chocolate on the screen. Right now it remembers nothing. Whatever crystal it held before is gone, and the next one depends entirely on how it cools.

### Chocolate: Snap and shine are the same evidence

{{checklist: a tempered bar | +a clean snap | +a glossy surface | +slight shrinkage in the mould | ?how long it lasts}}
You can hear form five. Its crystals are small, dense and evenly packed, so a tempered bar breaks with a clean snap instead of bending. You can see it too. A surface made of small, even crystals reflects light like a mirror, which is the gloss. And as the bar sets, it shrinks slightly, which is how it releases from its mould.

{{ambient}}
Look at the wall of pieces on the screen. Every one of them was melted, cooled with care and set in the same crystal. Break any one of them and you would hear the same clean snap.

### Chocolate: What to notice in the edit

{{text}}
The glowing line in the cross-section is a drawing, not a photograph, laid over a computer-made picture. The crystals are real, and they are too small for any camera. Every pour, drip and bar you see is real footage, repeated into walls and mirrors to show scale.

### Chocolate: Evidence limit

{{checklist: what this does not settle | +six forms are the standard convention | -pure cocoa butter is not a whole bar | ?how the forms should be divided}}
The melting points belong to pure cocoa butter, measured in a laboratory. A real bar also holds sugar, cocoa solids and, often, milk fat, and those shift the numbers a little. Researchers have also argued about how best to divide and name the forms. Six is the standard convention, not a law of nature.

{{text}}
And form five does not last forever. Over months, or after a warm day and a cool night, it can slowly rearrange into form six. The surface turns dull and streaked with grey. That is fat bloom. The chocolate is still chocolate. Its crystal has simply moved on.

### Chocolate: Closing

{{ambient}}
So the next time a square of chocolate disappears on your tongue and refuses to do the same in your hand, you can thank a crystal you will never see, two chemists in 1966, and a beam of X-rays. And if a bar ever melts in your pocket, that was not a failure of chemistry. It was a very warm pocket. Put it in the fridge, wait, and accept that what comes back is a looser crystal with ambitions.

### Bread: Why does bread rise?

Why does bread rise?

{{stat: 1857 | year | fermentation is the work of living cells | Louis Pasteur}}
Every hole in a slice of bread was first inflated by something alive. That something is yeast, a living single-celled fungus. In wet dough it feeds on sugars and gives off carbon dioxide gas, and the stretchy gluten network traps that gas as bubbles. The dough rises. In the oven the bubbles swell and the crumb sets around them. In 1857, Louis Pasteur argued that fermentation is the work of living cells. Today, X-ray scanners can watch the bubbles grow inside a loaf as it bakes.

### Bread: Flour and water do not rise on their own

{{contrast: dough without yeast | is=flat and dense | not=risen and open}}
Mix flour and water, knead it, and leave it on the counter. Without yeast or another leavening, it will sit there. It will not grow. Bake it, and you get something closer to a cracker than a loaf. So the rise is not in the flour. It is added.

{{text}}
For most of history, bakers added it without knowing what it was. A piece of yesterday's dough went into today's. A spoonful of foam from a brewer's vat went into the bowl. It worked. The dough swelled, soured slightly, and baked light. Bakers passed the habit down, along with the starter itself, the way a family passes down a recipe it cannot fully explain. Why it worked was a different question, and it took a long time to answer.

### Bread: Pasteur made fermentation a living thing

{{timeline: 1857=Pasteur's memoir on fermentation | 1860=alcoholic fermentation by yeast}}
In 1857, Louis Pasteur, then a young professor in Lille, presented a memoir on fermentation. Many chemists of the time thought fermentation was a purely chemical breakdown, a kind of slow decay that needed no life at all. Pasteur argued the opposite. Fermentation, he said, was the work of living microorganisms. By 1860 he had extended the argument to the fermentation that makes alcohol, and to yeast.

{{chain: Pasteur's evidence | fermenting liquid | under the microscope | cells that grow and multiply | >no living cells, no fermentation}}
His evidence was partly what he could see. Under the microscope, a fermenting liquid was full of small round cells, and those cells grew and multiplied as the fermentation went on. Where he kept living cells out, the fermentation did not start. Where he let them in, it did. The yeast was not a by-product of the process. It was the process. That sounds obvious now. At the time it was a fight, and the people on the other side were some of the most respected chemists in Europe.

{{ambient}}
Watch the dough on the screen. Pasteur never saw a loaf rise in a scanner. He saw cells under glass, multiplying, and he trusted what they told him.

### Bread: The yeast is eating

{{define: Saccharomyces cerevisiae | baker's and brewer's yeast | a single-celled fungus | PubChem}}
The yeast in most bread is a species called Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Each cell is a tiny oval, far too small to see without a microscope. In dough, it feeds on sugars, some from the flour itself, some released from starch by the flour's own enzymes.

{{steps: fermentation | sugar in | yeast breaks it down | carbon dioxide and ethanol out | >the gas is what rises}}
Without much oxygen, yeast breaks those sugars down by fermentation. Two main things come out. Ethanol, the same alcohol as in beer, and carbon dioxide, a gas. In a brewery the alcohol is the point. In a bakery the gas is the point. The alcohol mostly leaves the loaf in the oven, along with steam.

{{ambient}}
Look at the dough on the screen. Nothing will ever be pumped into it from outside. Countless cells inside it will breathe out the gas, very slowly, all at once.

### Bread: Gluten holds the breath in

{{define: gluten | a stretchy protein network | forms when wheat flour is wetted and worked | USDA Agricultural Research Service}}
Gas on its own would simply escape. Something has to hold it. In wheat dough that something is gluten, the protein network wheat chemists, including those at the USDA Agricultural Research Service, have studied for generations. When wheat flour is wetted and worked, two families of its proteins link into a stretchy, elastic network, a little like a sheet of very thin balloon rubber running through the dough.

{{text}}
Kneading lines that network up and strengthens it. The dough also carries tiny pockets of air, folded in while it was mixed. Those pockets are the seeds of every hole to come. The carbon dioxide from the yeast diffuses into them, and the gluten walls stretch to hold it. The dough grows. A dough with weak gluten lets the gas slip away and stays low and dense.

{{ambient}}
Look at the dough on the screen as it stretches. That give is the gluten network, pulling thin around each pocket of gas instead of tearing.

### Bread: Warmth sets the pace

{{contrast: rising dough | is=faster in a warm kitchen | not=stopped by the cold}}
Yeast is alive, so it keeps its own time. In a warm kitchen it feeds quickly and the dough rises in an hour or two. In a cool room it slows down. In a refrigerator it slows almost to a stop, but it does not die. Bakers use that on purpose. A dough left overnight in the cold rises slowly, and the long, slow fermentation gives the yeast and the flour's own enzymes time to make more flavour.

{{text}}
Too much warmth works against you. The yeast races, the gas comes too fast for the gluten to hold, and the dough can rise and then sag. Salt slows the yeast a little and tightens the gluten. Sugar feeds it, up to a point. Every recipe is quietly a set of instructions for managing a small, living population.

{{ambient}}
On the screen, the loaves have risen. Nothing was pumped into them. Every hole inside them was made from within.

### Bread: The oven does the rest

{{kitchen: 100 | the crumb sets}}
In the oven, the rise speeds up for a few minutes. Bakers call it oven spring. Heat makes the gas in every bubble expand. Carbon dioxide that was dissolved in the dough comes out of solution. Water and alcohol turn to vapour and push the bubbles wider still. Then the heat catches up with the yeast, and the yeast dies. The rise stops.

{{chain: setting the crumb | starch swells with water | proteins firm up | bubble walls stiffen | >the crumb sets around the holes}}
Now the structure sets. Starch granules in the dough soak up water and swell. The gluten proteins firm. The thin walls between the bubbles stiffen into place. By the time the centre of the loaf is near the boiling point of water, one hundred degrees Celsius, the crumb has set around its holes and will keep its shape as it cools.

{{ambient}}
On the screen, the loaves sit in the heat. Inside every one of them the same quiet sequence runs, from rise, to spring, to set.

### Bread: Watching a loaf from the inside

{{chain: X-ray tomography | dough inside the scanner | X-rays from many angles | a three-dimensional image | >bubbles growing and merging}}
For a long time, all of this was inferred from loaves cut open after the fact. You could see the holes, but not how they got there. X-ray tomography changed that. It is the same idea as a medical scanner. X-rays pass through the dough from many angles, and a computer rebuilds a three-dimensional picture of the inside.

{{cross_section: loaf of bread | gas cells merging}}
Researchers have put dough into these scanners and baked it there, imaging it again and again as it rose. The images show the bubbles growing. They show neighbouring bubbles touching and merging. And they show a turning point. Early on, each bubble is closed, like a foam. Later the walls between bubbles open up, and the crumb becomes a connected sponge, so steam can escape instead of bursting the loaf.

{{text}}
That is why a slice of bread looks the way it does. Look closely at the slice on the screen. Every hole is a bubble that stopped growing at the moment the walls around it set. The large, ragged holes are bubbles that merged. The fine, even ones are bubbles that were held apart until the crumb set.

{{ambient}}
Look at the crumb on the screen. Each hole is a frozen moment, the exact size its bubble had reached when the walls around it set.

### Bread: The crust is a different story

{{contrast: the loaf | is=pale, soft crumb inside | not=the brown, crisp crust outside}}
One part of the loaf never reaches the quiet ending. The inside stays moist and never gets much hotter than boiling water. The surface dries out and keeps heating, well past that point. There, the sugars and proteins of the dough start to react with each other, and the crust turns brown and starts to smell like bread. That reaction has a name, and it belongs to the next question.

### Bread: Evidence limit

{{checklist: what this does not settle | +yeast makes the gas | +gluten holds it | -every loaf behaves alike | ?exactly when the foam opens}}
This explains a yeasted wheat loaf. Sourdough adds bacteria that sour the dough as well as raise it. Rye and gluten-free breads hold their gas in other ways, with different structures. And the X-ray work is done on small samples in special ovens. The exact moment a foam opens into a sponge depends on the dough, the heat and the recipe. The scanners show it happening. They do not give one number for every loaf.

### Bread: Closing

{{ambient}}
So a loaf is a record of a quiet event. Countless cells breathed out, a web of wheat protein held its breath, and an oven made the moment permanent. It is also, if you think about it, the only time most of us happily eat something because it was full of holes. Next, the crust. Something happens on the outside of a steak that the inside never gets to taste.

### Steak: Why does seared meat taste so good?

Why does seared meat taste so good?

{{stat: 1912 | year | sugars and amino acids brown together | Louis-Camille Maillard}}
The inside of a steak never gets hot enough to taste like its crust. The flavour lives on the surface. When the surface of the meat is hot and dry, its sugars and amino acids react together. They make brown pigments and a large family of savoury, roasted aroma compounds. That is the Maillard reaction. Louis-Camille Maillard reported it in 1912. John Hodge of the US Department of Agriculture mapped its steps in 1953. And gas chromatography can pull the smell of a crust apart, one molecule at a time.

### Steak: Boiled is not seared

{{contrast: the same cut of beef | is=brown and savoury when seared | not=grey and mild when boiled}}
Take two pieces of the same beef. Boil one. Sear the other in a very hot pan. Both are cooked through. Both are safe to eat. But one is grey and mild, and the other is brown, crisp at the edges, and smells like dinner. Nothing was added to the seared piece. Something was made on it. And it was made in a layer so thin you could scrape it off with a fingernail, if you could bear to waste it.

{{kitchen: 100 | water boils}}
The difference is water. Water boils at one hundred degrees Celsius at sea level. As long as the surface of the meat is wet, it cannot get much hotter than that, because the heat goes into turning water into steam. A boiled piece of meat spends its whole life at or below that ceiling. A seared one dries its surface first, and then climbs well past it.

{{text}}
That is why cooks pat meat dry before it goes in the pan, and why a crowded pan makes grey meat. Too many pieces release too much water, and the surface stays wet. The browning only begins once the water is gone.

{{ambient}}
Listen to the pan on the screen. That hiss is water leaving the surface of the meat. Until it stops, the browning cannot begin.

### Steak: Maillard was not thinking about dinner

{{timeline: 1912=Maillard reports the reaction}}
In 1912, a French chemist named Louis-Camille Maillard reported an experiment to the French Academy of Sciences. He heated solutions of sugars together with amino acids, the small building blocks of proteins. The mixtures turned yellow, then brown, and gave off gas and new smells.

{{text}}
Maillard was not trying to improve a recipe. He was interested in chemistry inside living things, and in how proteins and sugars might combine in the body. The kitchen had been running his reaction for as long as anyone had held meat over a fire. He was the one who wrote it down and gave it a place in chemistry. What Maillard found was not a new flavour. It was a name for an old one.

{{ambient}}
Look at the crust on the screen. Every brown speck on it is a small record of the reaction he described.

### Steak: Hodge drew the map

{{timeline: 1953=Hodge's scheme of browning reactions}}
For decades the reaction stayed a tangle. Many chemists studied browning, especially in dried and stored foods, which darkened and changed flavour on the shelf. In 1953, John Hodge, a chemist at a US Department of Agriculture laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, published a single scheme that pulled the work together, in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry.

{{steps: Hodge's scheme | a sugar meets an amino acid | the product rearranges | it breaks into small reactive pieces | >brown pigments and aroma}}
Hodge divided the reaction into stages. First, a sugar joins an amino acid. That first product rearranges into a more stable form. Then it breaks apart into smaller, very reactive pieces. Those pieces react with each other and with more amino acids, making the brown pigments and the smells. His diagram is still drawn in food chemistry teaching today, with his name on it.

{{define: Strecker degradation | an amino acid broken down by those reactive pieces | releases small aroma molecules | Hodge's scheme}}
One step in that web matters a great deal for meat. Some of the reactive pieces tear amino acids apart in a step called Strecker degradation. That releases small, volatile molecules that smell strongly, even at tiny amounts. Amino acids that carry sulfur add sulfur compounds, which give the deep, meaty notes.

{{ambient}}
On the screen, the meat keeps searing. Every step of Hodge's map is running at once on that surface, all tangled together, faster than any diagram could show.

### Steak: Pulling a smell apart

{{chain: gas chromatography | aroma drawn off the crust | through a long, thin column | each compound leaves at its own time | >a list of molecules}}
A crust smells like one thing. It is actually a crowd. To see the crowd, chemists use gas chromatography. The aroma is collected from the cooked meat and sent through a very long, very thin column. Different molecules travel through it at different speeds. They come out the far end one by one, each at its own moment.

{{cross_section: seared steak | the browned crust}}
Picture the steak cut open, with a glowing line along the thin brown band at the edge. That band is where nearly all of this chemistry happens. The pale interior never got hot or dry enough to join in.

{{text}}
Joined to a mass spectrometer, the instrument weighs each molecule as it comes out and identifies it. In some laboratories, a person sits at a second outlet and sniffs, calling out what each one smells like. Studies of roast beef aroma have used this kind of separation to identify sulfur compounds present in tiny amounts that still shape the smell.

{{checklist: what a crust contains | +pyrazines with roasted notes | +sulfur compounds with meaty notes | +furans with sweet, caramel notes | ?which ones matter most}}
What comes out is a family of compounds. Pyrazines, with roasted, nutty notes. Sulfur compounds, savoury and meaty. Furans, sweet and caramel-like. No single molecule is the smell of a steak. It is the mixture, in its proportions, that your nose recognises. Change the heat or the time, and the proportions shift, which is why the same steak can taste quite different from two different cooks.

{{ambient}}
Watch the meat on the screen turn in the heat. Each piece is building its own crowd of molecules, in its own proportions, in the thin brown layer at its surface.

### Steak: The fat writes the accent

{{contrast: beef and lamb | is=the same Maillard chemistry | not=the same smell}}
If the Maillard reaction makes the savoury, roasted part of the smell, why does seared lamb not smell like seared beef? Part of the answer is fat. As meat heats, its fats break down too, especially where they meet oxygen, and they make their own family of aroma molecules. Those fragments also join the Maillard reactions, and the mixture changes.

{{text}}
Fats differ from one animal to the next, so the fat-derived notes differ too. The Maillard reaction gives the meal its roasted, meaty base. The fat writes the accent that tells you which animal it came from. Reviews of cooked meat aroma group the compounds this way: those from the browning reaction, those from the fat, and those that come from both at once.

{{ambient}}
Watch the fat on the screen as it sizzles and spits. It is not only cooking the meat. It is adding its own molecules to the smell.

### Steak: The same reaction, everywhere

{{checklist: the Maillard reaction at work | +the crust of bread | +roasted coffee | +toast | +the seared steak}}
Once you know what to look for, the reaction is everywhere in a kitchen. The brown crust of the loaf in the last question is the Maillard reaction. So is toast. So is the colour and much of the smell of roasted coffee beans and roasted nuts. In each case, sugars and amino acids meet on a hot, dry surface, and the same family of brown pigments and aroma molecules appears.

{{text}}
That is part of why the smell of something browning is so easy to recognise, and so hard to describe. You are smelling the same chemistry in different proportions, tuned by the food it happens on. A cook who sears, toasts or roasts is steering one reaction toward different ends.

{{ambient}}
On the screen, the grills go on and on, and the sound is the same everywhere: water leaving, and the browning beginning.

### Steak: Not caramel, not char

{{contrast: Maillard browning | is=sugars with amino acids | not=sugar alone, or burning}}
Two other things also turn food brown, and they are easy to confuse with this one. Caramelisation is sugar on its own breaking down under heat, with no amino acids involved. Charring is what happens past both, when the surface simply burns. A good crust lives between them. It needs heat and dryness. It does not need fire.

{{ambient}}
Watch the sear on the screen. The pan is quiet now. The browning has moved slowly across the surface, and the smell is the reaction itself, drifting up.

### Steak: Evidence limit

{{checklist: what this does not settle | +the reaction and its stages | +the main families of compounds | -a full recipe for the smell | ?how each compound adds up in the nose}}
The chemistry of the reaction is well mapped. The full list of what forms in a given crust, and in what amounts, depends on the cut, the fat, the heat and the time. Identifying a molecule is not the same as knowing how much it matters to the flavour. Researchers still debate which few compounds carry most of the smell. And taste, in the end, is measured on people, who do not always agree.

{{ambient}}

Neither of them, as far as we know, was trying to make dinner better. They did it anyway, and every cook since has been grateful.
