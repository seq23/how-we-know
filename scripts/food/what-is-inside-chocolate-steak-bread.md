### Chocolate: Why does chocolate taste so good?

Why does chocolate taste so good?

{{text}}
Cocoa butter can freeze into six different solids, and only one feels right. Chocolate tastes so good largely because of how it melts and what that melt releases: cocoa butter, tempered into a crystal form that melts at about 34 degrees Celsius, stays solid in your hand and turns liquid on your tongue, carrying aroma built during fermentation and roasting up to your nose. The six forms were mapped with X-ray diffraction, in work published through the American Oil Chemists' Society. But that number explains texture, not flavour, and it says nothing about why anyone likes it. Those are separate questions, answered by separate instruments, and one of them is barely answered at all.

### Chocolate: The pleasure starts as a change of state

{{kitchen: 34 | MELT}}
Start with the moment of eating, because that is where the physics is easiest to see. A square of well-made dark chocolate snaps cleanly. Held in a warm palm for a minute it softens, but on the tongue it collapses into liquid within seconds. That contrast is not luck. Cocoa butter, the fat pressed from cacao beans, has a melting range that sits just under the temperature inside your mouth. Solid on the shelf, solid in the hand, liquid in the mouth: a narrow window, and good chocolate lives inside it.

{{contrast: melting | is=a phase change | not=a chemical reaction}}
Melting is a phase change, not a chemical reaction, and it takes energy. As the fat turns from solid to liquid it draws heat from your tongue, which is part of why tempered chocolate can feel faintly cool as it goes. The liquid fat then coats the mouth, and with it come sugar, cocoa solids and the volatile molecules that carry aroma. Texture comes first, and texture is the delivery system for everything else. A chocolate that melts too high feels waxy and holds its flavour back. One that melts too low goes soft in the wrapper.

### Chocolate: One fat can set into six different solids

{{define: polymorphism | several different crystal arrangements | the molecules are the same; only the packing changes | American Oil Chemists' Society}}
Here is the part that surprises people. Cocoa butter is not one solid. Its fat molecules, mostly triglycerides, can stack together in several different crystal arrangements, a property called polymorphism. The molecules are the same; only the packing changes. Different packing means a different melting point, a different hardness and a different surface. The standard scheme, published in the Journal of the American Oil Chemists' Society and still the reference point in confectionery science, describes six forms, numbered One to Six.

{{chain: X-ray diffraction | angles of the scattered beams | how far apart those layers are | >each form leaves its own pattern}}
The way chemists tell these forms apart is X-ray diffraction. A beam of X-rays passed through a crystalline sample scatters off the regular layers of molecules, and the angles of the scattered beams reveal how far apart those layers are. Each form leaves its own pattern, a kind of fingerprint for packing. Paired with an instrument that measures how much heat a sample absorbs as it warms, known as differential scanning calorimetry, the patterns let researchers match each crystal arrangement to its melting behaviour. Nobody had to taste their way to this result. It was measured.

{{stat: 34 | degrees Celsius | Form Five melts at about 34 degrees Celsius | American Oil Chemists' Society}}
Of the six, Form Five is the one chocolate makers want. It melts at about 34 degrees Celsius, it is hard enough to snap, and it sets with a glossy surface. The lower forms melt too easily and feel greasy. Form Six is more stable still, but it forms slowly, over long storage, and as it does the surface turns dull and the texture grainy. That is why the number in the cold open matters: it is the melting point of one specific crystal, not of cocoa butter in general.

### Chocolate: Tempering is a method for choosing one crystal

{{steps: Tempering | heated until every existing crystal is gone | cooled, so crystals begin to form | warmed slightly | >the Form Five crystals survive}}
Left to cool on its own, melted chocolate sets into a mixture of forms, and the result is soft, streaky and dull. Tempering is the fix. First the chocolate is heated until every existing crystal is gone. Then it is cooled, so crystals begin to form. Then it is warmed slightly, just enough to melt the unstable forms while the Form Five crystals survive. Those survivors act as seeds, and as the chocolate sets they template the rest of the fat into the same arrangement. Professionals do it on marble slabs or in machines; the principle is the same.

{{contrast: fat bloom | is=fat recrystallising | not=mould}}
When tempering fails, or when a bar is stored warm, you can see it. Grey-white streaks and a dusty film appear on the surface. This is fat bloom, and it is often mistaken for mould. It is fat recrystallising, frequently into Form Six, scattering light instead of reflecting it. It is a change in structure, not something growing. But the melt changes, and so does the eating. The bloom is visible proof that crystal form is part of what you taste.

### Chocolate: The flavour is started on the farm

{{steps: From pod to bean | yeasts ferment the sugars in the pulp | bacteria turn the products into lactic and acetic acid | the acids enter the bean | >flavour precursors}}
Fresh cacao beans taste nothing like chocolate. They are bitter and astringent, packed inside a pod in sweet white pulp. After harvest, beans and pulp are heaped or boxed and left for several days. Yeasts ferment the sugars in the pulp, then bacteria turn the products into lactic and acetic acid. Heat builds, the acids enter the bean, and the bean's internal structure breaks down. Its own enzymes begin cutting proteins into smaller pieces and splitting sugars. Those fragments are flavour precursors: they do not yet smell of chocolate, but without them roasting has little to work with.

{{chain: Maillard reaction | amino acids and reducing sugars | heated together | >pyrazines}}
Roasting is where the precursors pay off. Heated together, amino acids and reducing sugars undergo the Maillard reaction, the same family of reactions that browns bread crust and seared meat. It produces a cascade of new molecules, among them pyrazines, which carry roasted and nutty notes. Researchers established the role of fermentation partly by comparison: beans dried without proper fermentation and then roasted develop only weak chocolate aroma. The farm, in other words, writes much of the recipe before the beans ever reach a factory.

{{ambient}}
After roasting, the beans are cracked, the shells removed, and the nibs ground into a paste. Sugar and often extra cocoa butter are added, the mixture is ground until the particles are too fine for the tongue to notice as grit, and then it is conched: stirred and kneaded for hours. Conching lets some harsh, volatile acids escape and coats every solid particle in fat. That coating is why smooth chocolate feels smooth. Once again, texture and flavour turn out not to be separable.

### Chocolate: Bitterness is part of the design

{{define: theobromine | a bitter compound closely related to caffeine | bitterness here is not a flaw to be removed | PubChem}}
Chocolate is not only sweet. Cacao contains theobromine, a bitter compound closely related to caffeine, along with polyphenols that taste bitter and feel astringent, a drying pull on the mouth. Theobromine takes its name from the genus of the cacao tree, Theobroma, from the Greek for food of the gods. Its structure and properties are catalogued in PubChem, the open chemistry database run by the National Institutes of Health. Bitterness here is not a flaw to be removed. Makers balance it against sugar and fat, and that balance is much of what separates a dark bar from a milk one.

{{text}}
This is also why preference splits so sharply between people. The same chemistry reaches every tongue, but sensitivity to bitterness differs from person to person, and so does what someone grew up eating. A bar one person finds richly complex, another finds harsh. Food science can describe the bar precisely. It cannot, from the bar alone, predict the verdict.

### Chocolate: The famous molecule story outruns its evidence

{{checklist: The pleasure molecule story | +present in cocoa | -shown to explain the pleasure | ?how much liking is learned}}
Now to what people repeat without checking. A popular story says chocolate tastes good because it contains special molecules that act on the brain, sometimes called a love chemical. Some of the compounds named in that story are genuinely present in cocoa; that part checks out. What has not been shown is that they reach the brain from an ordinary piece of chocolate in amounts that explain the pleasure. How much liking is learned rather than triggered remains open. The story survives because it is simpler than the real one, which is a crystal, a melt, a nose and a long chain of processing.

{{sources: Who measured what | American Oil Chemists' Society=six crystal forms | Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry=aroma compounds in cocoa | PubChem=theobromine}}
Notice what the stronger explanation rests on. Crystal forms are measured by diffraction and calorimetry, in work published through the American Oil Chemists' Society. Aroma compounds are separated by chromatography and confirmed by trained noses, in journals like the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry. The bitter compounds are catalogued in PubChem. Each step has an instrument behind it, and each step can be checked by someone else repeating it. The pleasure molecule story has none of that chain. It has a plausible name and a satisfying shape.

### Chocolate: Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +why chocolate melts the way it does | +where much of its aroma comes from | -why a particular person loves it}}
Be clear about what this evidence does not establish. It explains why chocolate melts the way it does, where much of its aroma comes from, and why processing changes the result. It does not explain why a particular person loves it, or why liking differs across people and places. Those are questions about perception, memory and culture, and the instruments in this episode do not measure them. Nothing here says anything about chocolate and health; that is a different body of evidence, and not one this episode examined.

### Chocolate: Closing

{{ambient}}
So the next square you eat is doing something precise. A crystal chosen by tempering gives way just below the heat of your mouth, and the fat releases molecules built by microbes and a roaster, long before and far away. The pleasure is yours. The melt, at least, has been measured.

### Steak: Why does steak taste better seared?

Why does steak taste better seared?

{{text}}
A wet steak cannot brown until its surface climbs past boiling water. Searing tastes better because a dry, very hot surface drives the Maillard reaction, in which amino acids and sugars in the meat recombine into brown pigments and a large family of new aroma compounds that raw or gently cooked meat does not have. The reaction carries the name of Louis-Camille Maillard, who described it to the French Academy of Sciences in 1912. That date does not explain why a frying pan produces this flavour on meat and a pot of simmering water does not. The answer is mostly about water, and about the places on a steak where water cannot stay.

### Steak: The brown crust is new chemistry, not concentrated meat

{{cross_section: steak | dry crust over wet interior}}
Look at a seared steak cut in half. On screen is a dry crust over a wet interior, and the two layers cooked in the same pan. The thin outer layer is brown, firm and fragrant. A few millimetres in, the meat is pink and still wet. Those two zones did not simply cook to different degrees. They went through different chemistry. The interior was heated, and its proteins changed shape and tightened. The crust was transformed: molecules that were there in the raw meat reacted with each other and became molecules that were not there before.

{{contrast: browned crust | is=new molecules made in the pan | not=the same meat with the water driven off}}
That distinction is the whole episode. It is tempting to think a crust tastes stronger because it is concentrated, the way a sauce gets stronger as it reduces. Some of that happens; drying does concentrate what is already present. But concentration cannot explain new smells. Raw beef has a faint, metallic, slightly sweet aroma. A seared crust smells roasted, nutty and savoury. Those notes come from compounds that have to be built, and building them needs a specific reaction under specific conditions.

### Steak: A chemist in a test tube found the reaction a century before the pan explanation

{{stat: 1912 | year | Maillard reports to the French Academy of Sciences | French Academy of Sciences}}
Maillard was not studying steak. He was a physician and chemist interested in how amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, behave. In his report to the French Academy of Sciences in 1912, he described heating solutions of amino acids with sugars and watching them turn yellow, then brown, while giving off new odours. The important part of his observation was that neither ingredient did this on its own at those temperatures. It took the pair.

{{define: Maillard reaction | amino acids and reducing sugars forming brown pigments and aroma compounds | not caramelization, which needs sugar alone | American Chemical Society}}
Chemists now define the Maillard reaction as a network of reactions between amino groups, usually on amino acids or proteins, and reducing sugars, a class of sugars with a reactive group that can open up and bond. The American Chemical Society, which publishes extensively on this chemistry, treats it as distinct from caramelization. Caramelization is what sugar does alone when it is heated hard. Meat contains very little free sugar, so a steak crust is overwhelmingly Maillard chemistry, with caramelization playing at most a minor part.

That raises an obvious question about the meat. If there is so little sugar, where does the reaction get its partner? Muscle contains small amounts of glucose and, importantly, ribose, a sugar that is part of the energy-carrying molecules muscle cells use. Small quantities are enough, because each reaction can feed many downstream ones.

### Steak: Water holds the surface below the browning zone

{{kitchen: 140 | MAILLARD}}
Here is where the pan matters. Chemistry explainers from the American Chemical Society describe the Maillard reaction as slow at ordinary cooking temperatures and speeding up sharply above roughly 140 degrees Celsius, while a wet surface stalls near 100 degrees. Treat that 140 as a convenient landmark rather than a switch. The reaction does occur at lower temperatures, very slowly, which is why some foods brown over long storage. In a kitchen timeframe of minutes, though, browning needs a surface well above the boiling point of water.

{{text}}
And a wet surface cannot get there. While liquid water sits on the meat, the energy arriving from the pan goes into turning that water into steam. The surface temperature is pinned close to boiling until the water is gone. This is why a steak dropped into simmering stock turns grey, not brown, however long it cooks. It is also why a crowded pan steams instead of searing: the meat releases moisture faster than the pan can drive it off.

{{chain: Why searing needs a hot pan | surface water boils away | surface dries | temperature climbs past boiling | >Maillard reaction speeds up}}
So the sequence on screen is a chain of conditions. The hot pan boils away surface water. The surface dries. Only then can its temperature climb past boiling, and only then does the Maillard reaction speed up enough to build a crust in the minutes before the inside overcooks. A sear is a race between drying the outside and heating the inside. A very hot pan wins that race because it dries the surface quickly while the interior lags behind.

### Steak: Hodge turned a brown smear into a sequence of stages

{{timeline: 1912=Maillard reports to the French Academy of Sciences | 1953=Hodge publishes his scheme in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry}}
For four decades after Maillard, browning was a well-known effect without a clear map. In 1953, John Hodge, a chemist at a USDA research laboratory in Peoria, Illinois, published a scheme in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry that organised the reaction into a sequence. His paper became one of the most cited in food chemistry, because it gave researchers a framework for asking which step produced which colour or smell.

{{steps: Hodge's scheme | early stage | intermediate stage | final stage | >brown pigments and aroma compounds}}
Hodge divided the reaction into an early stage, an intermediate stage and a final stage. In the early stage, a sugar and an amino group join and rearrange into a more stable intermediate. Nothing is brown yet, and little has an aroma. In the intermediate stage, those intermediates break apart and recombine into smaller, reactive fragments. Many of the smells appear here. In the final stage, fragments link into large brown polymers, the pigments you see as the crust.

{{text}}
The scheme mattered because it was testable. If a smell comes from the intermediate stage, you should be able to block or boost it by changing the conditions that govern that stage, such as acidity, moisture or the particular amino acid involved. Decades of model experiments, mixing one sugar with one amino acid and heating them, have done exactly that. The broad outline Hodge drew has held, although later work showed the network is far more branched than a three-stage diagram suggests.

### Steak: Instruments separated the smell into molecules

{{chain: From aroma to molecule | gas chromatography separates the volatiles | mass spectrometry identifies them | sniffing port links each molecule to a smell | >which compounds carry seared flavour}}
Knowing that browning makes smells is different from knowing which smells. That answer came from instruments. Gas chromatography separates the volatile molecules escaping from cooked meat, sending them through a long, thin column where they emerge one after another. Mass spectrometry then identifies each one by how it breaks into fragments. And a sniffing port, where a trained person smells the gas leaving the column, links each molecule to a smell. That last step matters, because the most abundant compounds are often not the ones you notice.

{{sources: Who documented the chemistry | French Academy of Sciences=the 1912 report | Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry=Hodge's scheme | PubChem=compound records}}
Studies of this kind, many published in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, have catalogued a large number of volatiles in cooked beef. Published counts vary with the method and the cut, so no single total is worth quoting. Several families keep recurring: pyrazines, with roasted and nutty notes; furans; and sulfur-containing compounds with savoury, meaty character. PubChem, the public chemical database, lists one of them, 2-methyl-3-furanthiol, which forms when the sulfur-bearing amino acid cysteine reacts with ribose. Small amounts of compounds like it carry an outsized share of what people call meaty.

{{text}}
One more process runs alongside. Strecker degradation, a side reaction of the Maillard network, breaks amino acids into small aldehydes with malty, toasty smells. It is another reason the crust smells of more than the meat it came from. Many of these molecules are perceived by smell rather than by the tongue, often through the back of the mouth as you chew. When people say a seared steak tastes better, much of what they detect is aroma.

### Steak: Searing does not seal anything in

{{contrast: searing | is=adding flavour chemistry | not=sealing in juices}}
There is a popular explanation that searing locks in juices, and it does not survive testing. Cooks and food scientists have weighed steaks before and after cooking, comparing ones seared first with ones that were not. Seared steaks lose about as much moisture, and sometimes more, because the surface reaches a higher temperature. The crust is porous. The sizzle you hear the whole time is water escaping as steam, which is direct evidence that nothing has been sealed.

{{text}}
This is worth holding onto, because it shows how the right explanation was reached. The sealing idea predicted something measurable, a heavier steak, and the scale disagreed. The Maillard explanation predicts other measurable things: no browning while the surface is wet, faster browning at higher temperatures, and specific new compounds in the crust. Each of those has been observed. The question is answered by what weighs, smells and separates as predicted.

### Steak: Fat runs parallel reactions of its own

{{checklist: What builds seared flavour | +Maillard reaction in the crust | +Strecker degradation | +reactions in heated fat | -sealing in juices}}
The Maillard reaction is not alone in the pan. Fat on and in the meat also changes when heated. Its components oxidise and break into fragments with their own aromas, and some of those fragments then join the Maillard network, reacting with amino acids to make compounds neither process would make separately. Researchers who compare beef with other meats attribute much of the difference between them to fat chemistry, while the roasted character common to all of them traces back to Maillard browning.

### Steak: What to notice in the edit

{{ambient}}
When the edit cuts to the pan, notice the order of events. First there is steam and noise, as surface water boils off. Only after that does the colour deepen. The visual delay between sizzle and brown is the experiment playing out in real time, and the sequence of the cuts should keep that order.

### Steak: Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +browning needs a dry surface | +specific compounds form in the crust | -searing seals in juices | ?why people prefer the result}}
This evidence explains which molecules a sear creates and why a dry, hot surface is needed to make them. It does not establish that seared steak is better in any absolute sense. Preference is learned and varies between people and cuisines. The chemistry also comes from model systems and measured samples; a real steak, with its own cut, fat and history, will not match any published profile exactly.

### Steak: Closing

{{text}}
A wet steak cannot brown. Dry it, heat it past boiling, and a reaction first seen in a test tube in 1912 begins building flavour that was never in the raw meat.

### Bread: Why is my bread not rising?

Why is my bread not rising?

{{stat: two | molecules of carbon dioxide | one molecule of glucose | American Chemical Society}}
One molecule of glucose yields two of carbon dioxide, yet the loaf stays flat. Bread fails to rise for one of two physical reasons: the yeast is not producing enough carbon dioxide, or the dough cannot hold the gas it does produce. The gas itself follows a fixed recipe. In yeast fermentation, as chemistry references from the American Chemical Society set it out, one molecule of glucose becomes two molecules of ethanol and two molecules of carbon dioxide. That figure tells you the gas gets made, even when the fermenter is alive and well. It does not tell you where the gas goes, and that is where many flat loaves are actually lost.

### Bread: Yeast inflates bubbles it never made

{{cross_section: proofing dough | gas cells around a gluten network}}
Start with the bubble, because the obvious picture is wrong. It is natural to imagine yeast cells blowing brand new bubbles into the dough, like tiny balloons appearing out of nowhere. Cereal chemists who study dough structure describe something different. Carbon dioxide from the yeast dissolves first into the water of the dough. Once that water is saturated, the gas has to go somewhere, and it diffuses into gas cells that already exist: small pockets of air folded in during mixing and kneading. Yeast is poor at creating a bubble from nothing. It is very good at inflating one that is already there. Slice through a proofing dough and what you see is gas cells around a gluten network.

{{text}}
That network is a web of hydrated wheat proteins, and as each gas cell swells, the web around it stretches. Rising, then, is two events stacked on top of each other: gas being made, and gas being kept. A failure in either one gives you the same flat dough sitting in the bowl, which is why the symptom alone rarely tells you the cause. Everything that follows sorts into one of those two halves, and the useful habit is to ask which half you are looking at before you change anything.

### Bread: Living yeast can be slowed to a standstill

{{checklist: what stops the gas being made | -yeast killed by hot water | -dough kept too cold | -salt or sugar drawing water away | ?yeast weakened by age}}
The first group of failures is about the fermenter: what stops the gas being made. Yeast killed by hot water is the classic case. Published thermal limits for baker's yeast vary with the strain and with how long the cells are held at temperature, so there is no single safe line worth quoting here. What is consistent is the direction: water that feels hot rather than warm is in the range that damages the cells. Dead yeast produces nothing, and no amount of waiting changes that.

{{text}}
The opposite failure is quieter. A dough kept too cold is not dead; its enzymes are simply working slowly. Bakers use this deliberately, retarding dough in a refrigerator to stretch fermentation over a much longer period and develop flavour. In a cool kitchen, the same slowing can look like failure when it is only delay. The test here is patience. A cold dough with living yeast will eventually move, and if it does, the fermenter was never the problem.

{{contrast: salt in dough | is=a flavour and a gluten strengthener | not=harmless to yeast at close contact}}
Salt or sugar drawing water away is the third failure. Both dissolve in the dough's water and raise its osmotic pressure, which pulls water out of the yeast cells. In a normal recipe, salt in dough is a flavour and a gluten strengthener, and the yeast copes. Poured directly onto the yeast, or added in excess, it is not harmless to yeast at close contact. Rich doughs heavy with sugar are known to rise slowly for the same osmotic reason, and some yeast strains are sold specifically for sweet doughs.

{{text}}
Finally, yeast weakened by age is an open case rather than a settled one. Dried yeast loses activity in storage, but how fast depends on packaging, moisture and temperature, and the date printed on a packet is a manufacturer's estimate, not a measurement of the yeast in your cupboard. The direct check is the old one: stir a little into warm water with a pinch of sugar and watch whether it foams. That small test separates a dead fermenter from a slow one.

### Bread: A strong fermenter cannot fill a broken net

{{steps: how the gluten network forms | water hydrates gliadin and glutenin | kneading aligns and links the proteins | the network stretches around gas cells | >weak or torn gluten lets gas escape}}
Now the second group: gas that is made but not kept. This is how the gluten network forms. Wheat flour contains two families of protein, and water hydrates gliadin and glutenin as soon as it reaches them. Then kneading aligns and links the proteins. The glutenins join into long elastic chains, while the gliadins let the mass flow and extend. Together they form gluten. In a well-developed dough, the network stretches around gas cells in thin films, like the skin of a balloon, so each cell swells instead of bursting.

{{ambient}}
Weak or torn gluten lets gas escape, and it has several sources. Too little kneading leaves the proteins unaligned. Flour from lower-protein wheat simply supplies less network to work with. Whole wheat flour carries bran, and those sharp particles cut through the gluten films. Flours from rye, barley or oats behave differently again, because their proteins do not form the same elastic web, so they never rise like wheat even when fermentation is vigorous. A dense loaf made from those grains is often not a failure at all. It is the chemistry of the grain.

{{sources: Who measures the rise | USDA Agricultural Research Service=bake test loaves | Institute of Food Technologists=dough as a material}}
This is why protein quality is measured rather than assumed, and it is worth knowing who measures the rise. The USDA Agricultural Research Service runs wheat quality laboratories that mill grain samples and bake test loaves, because protein content alone does not reliably predict how a flour will perform. Flours with similar protein can make very different bread, depending on how those proteins link. Research published through the Institute of Food Technologists treats dough as a material to be characterised, not a recipe to be trusted.

### Bread: Too much rising can look like no rising

{{contrast: an overproofed dough | is=a dough that rose and then collapsed | not=a dough that never rose}}
There is one failure that looks like the others but is really the reverse. Leave a dough to proof too long, and the yeast keeps making gas after the gluten network has stretched as far as it can. The thinnest films tear, neighbouring gas cells merge, and the structure sags. By the time you come back, an overproofed dough is a dough that rose and then collapsed, not a dough that never rose. The yeast may also have used up much of the available sugar, leaving little for the oven.

{{text}}
That oven phase is the last chance for rise, and it explains why some loaves look fine on the counter and disappoint later. In the oven, gas cells expand with heat, dissolved carbon dioxide comes out of solution, and ethanol and water turn to vapour, all pushing outward. Bakers call this oven spring. It ends when the starch gelatinises and the proteins set, fixing the crumb in place. A dough that has already exhausted its network has nothing left to spring with.

### Bread: Instruments separate gas made from gas kept

{{chain: separating the two failures | rheofermentometer | gas produced and gas retained | dough height over time | >which half of the rise failed}}
So how does anyone know which half of the rise failed? In a cereal laboratory, the job of separating the two failures is handed to instruments. A rheofermentometer holds a dough in a sealed chamber and tracks gas produced and gas retained at the same time: the total carbon dioxide the yeast releases, and how much of it the dough actually holds. A sensor resting on the dough records dough height over time. If production is high but retention is low, the problem is the network. If both are low, the problem is the fermenter. That separation is exactly what a home kitchen cannot do by eye.

{{define: farinograph | the resistance of dough to mixing | measures dough development, not gas | USDA Agricultural Research Service}}
Dough strength gets its own instrument. A farinograph mixes dough at a constant speed and records the resistance of dough to mixing as the gluten develops and then weakens. The curve shows how much water a flour absorbs, how long it takes to develop, and how quickly it breaks down. It measures dough development, not gas. For the inside of the loaf, researchers use X-ray microtomography, which builds a three-dimensional image of the crumb, and magnetic resonance imaging, which can follow a dough while it proofs. Those images show gas cells growing, merging and, in an overproofed dough, collapsing.

### Bread: Evidence limit

{{checklist: what this evidence settles | +gas must be made and kept | -the cause of any single loaf | ?how fast stored yeast declines}}
Here is what this evidence settles, and what it does not. Laboratory instruments separate gas made from gas kept under controlled conditions, with measured flour, water and temperature. A home kitchen varies all of them at once, and failures often overlap: a slightly cool room, a weaker flour and a little extra salt can together do what none of them would do alone. The evidence settles that gas must be made and kept. It does not identify the cause of any single loaf. Nor does it fix how fast stored yeast declines, and published figures for yeast tolerance vary enough that a single threshold would be false precision.

### Bread: Closing

{{ambient}}
A flat dough is a record of two systems, one alive and one structural. Before changing the recipe, it is worth asking which of them gave out: the yeast that makes the gas, or the gluten that was meant to hold it.
