### Butter: How does butter get made from cream?

How does butter get made from cream?

{{stat: 80 | percent milkfat | federal definition of butter, finished product | USDA}}
A liquid that is mostly water ends up as a solid that is mostly fat. Butter is made by agitating cream until its fat globules collide, break their membranes and clump together, turning a fat-in-water emulsion into a water-in-fat one. Under the federal definition of butter that the USDA's grade standards for butter rely on, the finished product must contain at least 80 percent milkfat by weight. That figure tells you where the process ends. It does not tell you how a liquid with fat scattered through water becomes a solid with water scattered through fat. That reversal is the real subject, and every step of it was worked out by looking inside the material.

### Butter: Cream is fat hiding inside water

{{cross_section: cream | fat globules wrapped in the milk fat globule membrane}}
Start with what is on screen: a cross-section of cream. The background is water, carrying dissolved milk sugar, minerals and proteins. Floating in it are fat globules, each one wrapped in a thin skin called the milk fat globule membrane. That membrane is built from phospholipids and proteins, the same broad classes of molecule that form the membranes of living cells, and that is no accident. It is pinched off from the secreting cells of the mammary gland as the fat leaves them. Under an ordinary light microscope, the globules appear as separate bright spheres that do not merge, even when they drift against each other. The membrane is what keeps them apart.

{{define: emulsion | droplets of one liquid held in another liquid | cream is an oil-in-water emulsion | food scientists}}
Food scientists call this arrangement an emulsion: droplets of one liquid held in another liquid they would not normally mix with. Cream is an oil-in-water emulsion. The fat is the dispersed phase, the water is the continuous phase, and the continuous phase decides how the material behaves. That is why cream pours, wets your finger and rinses off in water. It also explains how cream is collected in the first place. Fat is less dense than water, so if milk is left standing, the globules slowly rise. Modern dairies speed that up with centrifugal separators, which spin milk so the lighter, fat-rich fraction can be drawn off. Either way, what you get is still an emulsion, just a more crowded one.

### Butter: Churning breaks membranes rather than squeezing out fat

{{steps: Partial coalescence | air is beaten in | membranes are damaged | crystals bridge globules | >clumps grow}}
Now the churn. A common picture is that churning presses fat out of cream like juice from fruit. Butter is not pressed out of cream; microscopy shows something different. Agitation beats air into the cream, and the bubbles do much of the work. Fat globules collide with the surfaces of those bubbles, and their membranes spread and thin there. Meanwhile, the fat crystals inside a globule can poke through its weakened skin. When two damaged globules touch, the crystals of one can bridge into the liquid oil of the other, and they stick without fully merging into one sphere. This is called partial coalescence. The steps on screen run in order: air is beaten in, membranes are damaged, crystals bridge globules, clumps grow.

{{define: whipped cream | a foam held up by partly coalesced clumps of fat | overwhipped cream carried to its end | microscopy}}
If you have whipped cream by hand, you have already seen the halfway point. Whipped cream is a foam held up by these partly coalesced clumps of fat, forming a network around the air bubbles. That is why whipped cream holds its shape. Keep going and the network tightens. The clumps grow larger, they pull fat away from the bubble surfaces, the foam can no longer support itself, and it collapses. Microscopy of whipped cream sampled at different moments traces this progression: first separate globules, then clusters lining the bubbles, then larger aggregates that no longer belong to any bubble at all. Butter is, in a sense, overwhipped cream carried to its end.

### Butter: The break is a phase inversion you can see and hear

{{contrast: butter | is=a water-in-fat material | not=a fat-in-water one}}
Then comes the moment butter makers call the break. Quite suddenly, the smooth cream turns into small yellow grains floating in a thin, watery liquid. The sound in the churn changes from a soft thud to a slosh, because there is now free liquid moving around solid lumps. What has happened is a phase inversion. Before the break, water was the continuous phase and fat was dispersed. After it, the fat grains join into a connected mass, and the water that remains inside them is trapped as small droplets. Butter is a water-in-fat material, not a fat-in-water one. That is the reversal from the cold open, and it is why butter feels greasy and repels water, where cream wets everything it touches.

{{text}}
The liquid left behind is the original buttermilk. It is not the thick cultured drink sold under the same name in many supermarkets. It is the water phase of the cream, carrying lactose, proteins, minerals and much of the torn membrane material. Because those membrane phospholipids are good emulsifiers, food scientists have studied buttermilk as an ingredient in its own right. Draining it is the first separation in the process, and a substantial share of what went into the churn leaves as buttermilk rather than becoming butter.

### Butter: Washing and working decide what butter keeps

{{steps: Washing and working | washed with cold water | drained again | working | >salt dissolves into those droplets}}
The grains are still coated in buttermilk, so they are usually washed with cold water, then drained again. Washing removes residual milk sugar and protein that bacteria could otherwise feed on. Then comes working: kneading the mass, traditionally with paddles, industrially between rollers or inside continuous butter-making machines. Working presses the grains into one block and squeezes out loose water, but its more important job is to break the water that stays behind into a very large number of very small droplets, spread evenly through the fat. If salt is added, it dissolves into those droplets rather than into the fat.

{{chain: confocal laser scanning microscopy | different fluorescent dyes | sharp optical slices | >the water appears as fine, scattered droplets}}
This internal structure is visible with confocal laser scanning microscopy. Researchers stain the fat and the water with different fluorescent dyes, and the instrument scans a focused laser through the sample, building sharp optical slices at different depths. In images of well-worked butter, the water appears as fine, scattered droplets, separated by a continuous fat phase that contains crystals, surviving whole globules and liquid oil. That droplet pattern matters for keeping quality. Bacteria that do enter the butter are confined to tiny isolated pockets of water, many carrying little nutrient, and in salted butter a concentrated brine. Poorly worked butter, with larger connected water channels, gives microbes more room. This is a description of structure, not guidance about storing anything.

### Butter: Cultured butter adds bacteria before the churn, on purpose

{{define: diacetyl | aroma compounds produced by certain strains | catalogued in PubChem | PubChem}}
Everything so far describes sweet cream butter, made from cream that has not been fermented. Cultured butter adds a step at the start. The cream is inoculated with lactic acid bacteria and allowed to ripen before churning. The bacteria convert some of the milk sugar into lactic acid, which sours the cream, and certain strains also produce aroma compounds. The best known is diacetyl. It is catalogued in PubChem, the chemical database run by the National Institutes of Health, where its structure and properties can be checked. Flavor chemists have long linked it to the characteristic aroma of cultured butter.

{{chain: Identifying aroma | gas chromatography | sniffing port | mass spectrometry | >aroma molecules in dairy products have been pinned down}}
Identifying which molecule smells of what took a particular pairing of instruments. Gas chromatography separates a sample's volatile compounds by how quickly they travel through a long, coated column. In gas chromatography olfactometry, the stream leaving the column is split: one part goes to a detector, the other to a sniffing port, where a trained person notes what they smell and when. Matching a buttery note to the moment a known compound emerges is how aroma molecules in dairy products have been pinned down. Mass spectrometry then confirms the identity from the compound's fragmentation pattern. Research of this kind appears in journals published by the American Chemical Society.

### Butter: Grading judges arrangement, not just amount

{{contrast: grading | is=how it is arranged | not=how much fat is present}}
Once butter is made, someone has to decide whether it is any good. In the United States, the USDA publishes voluntary grade standards for butter, and the top grade is Grade AA. Graders assess flavor, body, color and salt. Body is where the physical chemistry from earlier returns: a grader is judging texture, spreadability and whether water beads out of the cut surface, all consequences of crystal structure and droplet dispersion. The 80 percent milkfat requirement in the federal definition is a separate, compositional line. A product can meet it and still grade poorly, because composition says how much fat is present, not how it is arranged.

### Butter: Evidence limit

{{checklist: What this evidence settles | +partial coalescence explains the main mechanism | ?how much each factor contributes | -no single temperature recipe is universal | ?other countries set their own standards}}
Here is what this evidence does not settle. Microscopy shows structures in small samples, often after staining or preparation that can disturb what is being imaged, so each picture is a careful snapshot rather than a live film of a churn. The partial coalescence account explains the main mechanism well, but how much each factor contributes, from fat crystal shape to membrane composition to churn speed, varies with the cream and is still studied. Milk fat composition shifts with feed and season, so no single temperature recipe is universal. And the 80 percent figure is a United States legal definition; other countries set their own standards, which this episode has not surveyed.

### Butter: Closing

{{ambient}}
Cream becomes butter when its structure is turned inside out. The fat that was scattered becomes the frame, and the water that was everywhere is left in small, sealed pockets.

### Rice: Why does rice get sticky?

Why does rice get sticky?

{{cross_section: rice grain | starch granules}}
A grain sold as glutinous contains no gluten whatsoever. Rice gets sticky because cooking makes its starch granules swell and break down, and starch released at the grain surface acts as a glue between neighbouring grains. The word glutinous describes a texture, glue-like, not an ingredient. Gluten is a wheat protein, and rice stickiness is not a protein story at all. It is a starch story built from two molecules, which chemical databases such as PubChem list as separate entries: amylose and amylopectin. That much is settled. What it does not settle is why the same two molecules give you loose, separate grains of basmati in one pot and a single cohesive mass of sticky rice in another.

### Rice: Stickiness lives in starch, not in protein

{{contrast: glutinous | is=glue-like | not=gluten}}
Start with what is inside a grain of milled white rice. Most of it is endosperm, the stored food the seed would have used to grow, and most of that endosperm is starch. Proteins are present, but they are minor partners here. Wheat dough becomes elastic because two families of wheat proteins link into a stretchy network when the flour is wetted and worked. Rice has no comparable network. So when rice clumps, the glue has to be coming from somewhere else, and every line of evidence points to the starch.

{{define: amylose | a mostly linear chain of glucose | amylopectin is the heavily branched form | PubChem}}
Starch is not one substance. It is glucose, linked into long chains, and those chains come in two architectures. Amylose is mostly linear: a long chain that can coil into a helix. Amylopectin is heavily branched, a bushy molecule with many short chains hanging off a backbone, and it is far larger than amylose. Both are catalogued by PubChem, the chemical database run under the National Institutes of Health. Inside the grain, these molecules are packed into tiny starch granules, and rice granules are among the smallest of the cereal starches. Within each granule, the branches of amylopectin line up side by side into ordered, crystalline layers, with amylose threaded through the less ordered regions.

{{checklist: What the uncooked granule is | +packed with amylose and amylopectin | +partly crystalline | -swollen | -sticky}}
That ordered packing is the key to everything that follows. A raw granule is dense, partly crystalline and resistant to water. Uncooked rice is hard and not sticky, because its starch is locked away in that structure. Stickiness only appears when that structure is taken apart, and the methods that show it happening are worth looking at one by one.

### Rice: Heat and water take the granule apart below boiling

{{chain: Seeing the granule change | polarized light | X-ray diffraction | differential scanning calorimetry | >the crystalline order is lost}}
The process is called gelatinization, and it was not inferred from texture alone. Three instruments show it from different angles. Under polarized light, a raw starch granule glows with a dark cross across it, called a Maltese cross, because its ordered layers bend light. As the granule is heated in water, the cross fades and disappears. X-ray diffraction gives the same verdict in another language: raw cereal starch scatters X-rays into a characteristic pattern of sharp rings, and after heating in water that pattern collapses into a diffuse blur. Differential scanning calorimetry measures heat flow, and it records a distinct absorption of energy at the moment the crystals melt. Three independent signals, one conclusion: the crystalline order is lost.

{{kitchen: 100 | boiling point}}
Here is a detail that surprises many cooks. Rice starch gelatinizes well below the boiling point. Water at sea level boils at 100 degrees Celsius, and a pot of simmering rice sits close to that, but the granules begin swelling and losing their order at noticeably lower temperatures. Different varieties gelatinize at different temperatures, and rice breeders classify grains as low, intermediate or high on that scale. The published values vary with variety and method, so this script will not quote a single figure. What matters is that by the time the pot is simmering, gelatinization is well under way.

### Rice: What leaks out of the grain becomes the glue

{{steps: From granule to glue | granules swell | starch leaches out | the surface film dries | >grains bond where films touch}}
Once the crystals melt, water floods in and the granules swell, many times their original volume. Swelling is not tidy. As the granules swell, some starch leaches out, both from granules at the surface of each grain and from the fine starch dust left behind by milling. That released starch forms a thin, viscous film on the outside of every grain. When cooking stops and steam escapes, the surface film dries and thickens. Where two grains touch, their films merge, and the grains bond where films touch. Electron microscopes show this directly: images of cooked rice surfaces reveal a layer of smeared, gelled material that is absent on raw grains. Food chemists publishing in journals such as the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, from the American Chemical Society, have analysed that surface material and linked its quantity and composition to measured stickiness.

{{contrast: rinsing | is=removing surface starch | not=changing the starch inside}}
This is also why rinsing rice before cooking can change the result. Rinsing washes away loose starch dust from the outside of milled grains, so less material is available to form that first surface film. It does not alter the starch locked inside each grain, which still gelatinizes and still releases some starch during cooking. Studies of rinsing report effects that differ by variety and by how thoroughly the rice is washed, so the honest summary is modest. Rinsing reduces one source of glue. It does not remove the mechanism.

### Rice: One gene sets most of the ratio

{{define: Waxy gene | encodes granule-bound starch synthase | without it, the grain makes almost no amylose | USDA Agricultural Research Service}}
So why is glutinous rice so much stickier than basmati, if both gelatinize and both release starch? The answer lies in the ratio of the two molecules, and much of that ratio is controlled by a single gene. It is called the Waxy gene, and it encodes an enzyme called granule-bound starch synthase, which builds amylose inside the granule. When that enzyme is broken, without it, the grain makes almost no amylose. Its starch is almost entirely amylopectin. Glutinous rice varieties carry broken versions of this gene. Rice geneticists, including those at the USDA Agricultural Research Service, which breeds rice and studies its grain quality, use the Waxy gene as one of the main markers for predicting how a new line will cook.

{{chain: The iodine test | add iodine to starch | amylose turns deep blue | amylopectin turns reddish-brown | >colour estimates amylose content}}
The oldest way to see this needs no sequencer. Iodine slips inside the helical coil of amylose and turns it a deep blue. Amylopectin, with its short branches, cannot hold iodine the same way, and stains a reddish-brown instead. Cut a grain of ordinary rice, add iodine, and it turns dark blue. Do the same with glutinous rice and it turns reddish-brown. Measured carefully, with a spectrophotometer reading the depth of the colour, this reaction becomes a standard laboratory method in which colour estimates amylose content, used by breeding programmes and grain-quality laboratories.

{{sources: Who measures rice starch | USDA Agricultural Research Service=rice breeding and grain quality | Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry=starch structure and stickiness | Royal Society of Chemistry=the chemistry of starch}}
Across those sources a consistent pattern appears. Varieties with relatively high amylose, such as many long-grain types, cook firmer and more separate. Varieties with lower amylose, such as many short-grain types, cook softer and stickier. Glutinous rice, with almost no amylose, sits at the extreme. Researchers publishing on starch structure, and bodies such as the Royal Society of Chemistry that cover the chemistry of starch, describe the same direction of effect, even where their exact measurements differ.

### Rice: Amylose holds grains apart as it cools

{{steps: Pasting test | heat a starch slurry while stirring | record viscosity as it thickens | cool it and record again | >setback tracks amylose}}
Why would amylose make grains less sticky? Part of the answer comes from viscosity instruments, such as the Rapid Visco Analyser, which heat a starch slurry while stirring and record viscosity as it thickens. The procedure is to heat a starch slurry while stirring, record viscosity as it thickens, then cool it and record again. On cooling, the paste firms up, a rise called setback, and setback tracks amylose. Linear amylose chains, freed from the granule, quickly find each other and pack back into ordered bundles, a process called retrogradation. That rapid re-ordering firms the grain and its surface, so the film between grains sets into something closer to a firm skin than a tacky paste.

{{text}}
Amylopectin behaves differently. Its bushy branches swell more freely, hold water and re-order only slowly, over days rather than hours. A grain rich in amylopectin stays soft, swollen and tacky at the surface, which is exactly the texture glutinous rice is prized for. The same retrogradation explains a familiar kitchen observation: day-old rice from the refrigerator is harder and less sticky than fresh rice, because its starch has partly re-ordered.

### Rice: The variety sets the range and the cook moves within it

{{contrast: long-grain | is=firmer and more separate | not=soft and tacky}}
Variety sets the range, but the method moves the result within it. Long-grain rice tends to cook firmer and more separate, while glutinous rice cooks soft and tacky, and no amount of technique fully swaps one for the other. More water lets granules swell further and release more starch. Stirring rubs grains together and scrapes starch off their surfaces, which is why risotto, made with constant stirring, turns creamy. Resting after cooking lets steam redistribute and the surface film set. Laboratories quantify the outcome with texture analysers, which press a probe into cooked rice and measure the force needed to pull it away, alongside trained tasting panels.

### Rice: What to notice in the edit

{{ambient}}
When the cross-section glows, watch the granules, not the grain. The grain barely changes shape, while its starch granules swell and spill. Every instrument in this episode, polarized light, X-ray diffraction, calorimetry, iodine and viscosity, is looking at that same small event from a different side.

### Rice: Evidence limit

{{checklist: What this evidence settles | +stickiness comes from starch | +amylose content predicts much of it | -one number that defines stickiness | ?how much rinsing changes it}}
This evidence establishes that stickiness comes from starch, and that amylose content predicts much of it. It does not give a single number that defines stickiness: instruments and tasting panels measure different things, and they do not always agree. Two varieties with similar amylose can still cook differently, because amylopectin chain lengths, protein and fat content also play roles that remain under study. How much rinsing changes the result remains open. And none of this evidence says anything about nutrition or health.

### Rice: Closing

{{text}}
A word that sounds like wheat turns out to describe a missing enzyme. The glue on your rice is starch that escaped its granule, and how much escapes was written, in large part, into a single gene.

### Meat: Why does meat need to rest after cooking?

Why does meat need to rest after cooking?

{{stat: 3 | minutes | Rest time paired with 145 degrees Fahrenheit for whole cuts | USDA}}
Take a roast off the heat, and its centre keeps getting hotter. Resting lets two physical processes finish after the heat is gone: heat keeps travelling inward from the hot outer layers, so the centre continues to cook, and the meat cools enough that less of its liquid runs out when it is cut. The USDA's temperature guidance for whole cuts of beef, pork, veal and lamb pairs 145 degrees Fahrenheit with a 3-minute rest, because the temperature holds or climbs during that time. That figure settles the heat question. It does not settle the question most people mean, which is about the puddle on the board.

### Meat: The heat is still moving when the pan goes cold

{{cross_section: a thick roast | hot outer layers, cooler centre}}
Start with what the pan or oven actually does. Heat enters at the surface. It travels inward by conduction, with each layer of meat warming the next. That process is slow, because meat is mostly water and protein, and neither moves heat quickly. So at any moment during cooking, a thick roast is not one temperature. It has hot outer layers, a cooler centre, and a slope of temperatures running between them. When you lift it out, nothing feeds the outside any more. The heat already stored in those outer layers does not vanish, though. Some of it escapes into the air. The rest keeps flowing the only other way it can, inward and down the slope toward the cooler centre. That is why a roast keeps cooking after it leaves the heat.

{{chain: Measuring carryover | probe thermometer in the centre | reading keeps rising off the heat | >heat still travelling inward}}
You do not have to take this on trust. The instrument is a probe thermometer in the centre, left in place after the meat comes off the heat. For a while, the reading keeps rising off the heat, with no flame and no oven involved. Then it levels off, and then it falls. Cooks call the rising part carryover cooking. The shape of that curve is the evidence. After the source is removed, the only heat that can warm the centre is heat still travelling inward from the outer layers. Cut a roast in half and a thermal camera shows the same pattern in colour: a hot rim fading into a cooler middle.

{{contrast: carryover | is=heat already inside moving inward | not=new heat from the pan}}
How much the centre rises is where the honest answer becomes a range with no fixed edges. It depends on the size of the cut, how hot the outside was, and how long it cooked. A large roast from a hot oven stores a great deal of heat in its outer layers and can rise noticeably. A thin steak stores little heat and cools from both faces almost at once. Estimates vary, and different figures get quoted for different cuts, so this script will not pick one. The mechanism, though, does not change. Carryover is heat already inside moving inward, not new heat from the pan.

{{define: rest time | the time after removal when the temperature remains constant or continues to rise | not a cooking instruction from this channel | USDA}}
Carryover is also the reason the USDA guidance includes a rest at all. The guidance describes its 3-minute rest as the time after removal when the temperature remains constant or continues to rise. That statement is about temperature and time, and this episode quotes it only to explain the physics. It is not a cooking instruction from this channel. The point here is narrower. An official body treats the minutes after cooking as part of the cooking, because the thermometer shows that they are.

### Meat: Cooking wrings water out of muscle fibres

{{steps: What heat does to muscle | proteins in the fibres contract | water is squeezed out | connective tissue shrinks | >liquid collects between the fibres}}
Now for the juice. Raw muscle is a bundle of long fibres, each one packed with protein filaments, and most of its weight is water held in and around them. As the meat heats, proteins in the fibres contract. They unfold, tangle and tighten, and the fibres shorten and narrow. Water is squeezed out, the way water leaves a sponge as you wring it. The connective tissue shrinks as well, wrapped around the bundles and tightening the squeeze. The released liquid collects between the fibres, in channels it did not fill before. Some of it reaches the surface and evaporates or drips. Some is still in the meat, loose, when you pick up the knife.

{{chain: Watching water move | low-field NMR | how freely that water moves | >heating shifts water toward the free fraction}}
You cannot see water inside a steak, so this has to be measured indirectly. One tool is low-field NMR, short for nuclear magnetic resonance. It uses the same physics as medical scanners, at a smaller scale. It listens to hydrogen in water molecules and reports how freely that water moves. Water bound tightly to protein, water trapped inside the fibres and water sitting loose between them each give a different signal. Meat scientists publishing in journals such as the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, from the American Chemical Society, have used it to follow cooking. As the meat heats, the signal from water inside the fibres shrinks and the signal from water between them grows. In other words, heating shifts water toward the free fraction, out of the fibres and into the gaps.

{{sources: Who studies this | USDA Agricultural Research Service=meat quality research | American Chemical Society=water in cooked meat | Institute of Food Technologists=heat and food}}
This is mainstream science. Researchers at the USDA Agricultural Research Service study meat quality, including how much water a cut holds and loses. The Institute of Food Technologists publishes widely on how heat changes food. Across that literature the direction is consistent: more heat means more contraction, and more contraction pushes more liquid loose. The old kitchen explanation gets that part right. It goes wrong about what happens next.

### Meat: Juice is not herded into the centre

{{contrast: the juice story | is=liquid squeezed loose throughout the meat | not=juice herded into the centre}}
The familiar version goes like this. A hot sear seals the surface, the heat drives the juice inward to the centre, and resting lets it spread back out to the edges so the slice stays moist. Each step sounds physical, but few of them survive measurement. A seared surface is not a seal. When meat seared first is weighed against meat that was not, the seared piece does not reliably lose less. You can hear the answer too. A searing steak keeps sizzling, and that sizzle is water reaching hot metal. There is also little direct evidence that juice gets herded into the centre. Squeezing happens wherever the meat is hot enough, which is everywhere, and it is strongest at the outside. The evidence supports liquid squeezed loose throughout the meat, not juice herded into the centre.

{{checklist: The popular story | -a sear seals the surface | ?juice is herded into the centre | +liquid is squeezed loose by heat}}
So the popular story scores unevenly. The claim that liquid is squeezed loose by heat is supported. The claim that a sear seals the surface is not. The claim that juice is herded into the centre and flows back is unproven at best, and nothing else in the explanation depends on it. That last point matters most. If the juice is not migrating, resting cannot be about letting it migrate home. Something else has to explain the real difference cooks see on the board.

### Meat: A scale tests what a story cannot

{{steps: Weighing the loss | weigh the raw meat | weigh it after cooking | weigh it after resting | weigh what runs onto the board}}
The least glamorous instrument in any lab settles most of this. It is a scale. Meat scientists call the result cooking loss, and the method fits in a breath. Weigh the raw meat. Weigh it after cooking. Weigh it after resting. Then cut it and weigh what runs onto the board. Do that for pieces sliced at once and pieces left to rest, and you have numbers instead of impressions. Everyone notices the liquid on the board. The earlier weighings tell you where the rest of the water went.

{{text}}
When cooks and researchers run comparisons like this, the results point one way. Meat sliced straight away sheds more onto the board than meat that has rested. That difference is real. Resting meat is not sealed either, though. While it sits, it stays hot, keeps steaming and drips a little onto the plate. Some of the water that would have reached the board goes into the air instead. As a result, the total loss of a rested piece is often closer to that of an unrested piece than the puddle suggests. How close depends on the cut and the conditions, and published comparisons do not agree on a single figure. Resting shrinks the puddle more clearly than it shrinks the total.

### Meat: The size of the cut decides how much any of this matters

{{chain: Why size matters | thick roast stores more heat | centre rises for longer | >resting changes a roast more than a thin steak}}
Put the two effects together and the role of size becomes clear. A thick roast stores more heat, so its centre rises for longer after it leaves the oven, and its outside and inside start further apart. Resting has more work to do on both the temperature and the liquid. A thin steak stores little heat, evens out quickly and cools fast whether you wait or not. For a thin steak, the gap between resting and not resting is small and hard to measure. Resting changes a roast more than a thin steak, which matches what the thermometer and the scale both report.

### Meat: Evidence limit

{{text}}
This evidence does not give a single correct resting time for every cut. The only time stated in this episode is the USDA's 3-minute rest, and that rest exists for temperature, not for juiciness. The evidence does not show that juice migrates to the centre. It also does not show that resting saves most of the liquid that cooking pushes loose, because much of that liquid is gone before the meat leaves the heat. It does not settle whether people can taste the difference in a blind comparison. Juiciness is partly a sensation, and sensory studies measure people, not water. Finally, the carryover rise has no fixed size. Mass, heat and time set its range.
