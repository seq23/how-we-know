### Butter: How does butter get made from cream?

How does butter get made from cream?

{{stat: 80 | percent milkfat | federal definition of butter, finished product | USDA}}
A liquid that is mostly water ends up as a solid that is mostly fat. Butter is made by agitating cream until its fat globules collide, break their membranes and clump together, turning a fat-in-water emulsion into a water-in-fat one. Under the federal definition of butter that the USDA's grade standards for butter rely on, the finished product must contain at least 80 percent milkfat by weight. That figure tells you where the process ends. It does not tell you how a liquid with fat scattered through water becomes a solid with water scattered through fat. That reversal is the real subject, and every step of it was worked out by looking inside the material.

### Butter: Cultured butter adds bacteria before the churn, on purpose

{{define: diacetyl | aroma compounds produced by certain strains | catalogued in PubChem | PubChem}}
Everything so far describes sweet cream butter, made from cream that has not been fermented. Cultured butter adds a step at the start. The cream is inoculated with lactic acid bacteria and allowed to ripen before churning. The bacteria convert some of the milk sugar into lactic acid, which sours the cream, and certain strains also produce aroma compounds. The best known is diacetyl. It is catalogued in PubChem, the chemical database run by the National Institutes of Health, where its structure and properties can be checked. Flavor chemists have long linked it to the characteristic aroma of cultured butter.

{{chain: Identifying aroma | gas chromatography | sniffing port | mass spectrometry | >aroma molecules in dairy products have been pinned down}}
Identifying which molecule smells of what took a particular pairing of instruments. Gas chromatography separates a sample's volatile compounds by how quickly they travel through a long, coated column. In gas chromatography olfactometry, the stream leaving the column is split: one part goes to a detector, the other to a sniffing port, where a trained person notes what they smell and when. Matching a buttery note to the moment a known compound emerges is how aroma molecules in dairy products have been pinned down. Mass spectrometry then confirms the identity from the compound's fragmentation pattern. Research of this kind appears in journals published by the American Chemical Society.

### Butter: Grading judges arrangement, not just amount

{{contrast: grading | is=how it is arranged | not=how much fat is present}}
Once butter is made, someone has to decide whether it is any good. In the United States, the USDA publishes voluntary grade standards for butter, and the top grade is Grade AA. Graders assess flavor, body, color and salt. Body is where the physical chemistry from earlier returns: a grader is judging texture, spreadability and whether water beads out of the cut surface, all consequences of crystal structure and droplet dispersion. The 80 percent milkfat requirement in the federal definition is a separate, compositional line. A product can meet it and still grade poorly, because composition says how much fat is present, not how it is arranged.

### Rice: Why does rice get sticky?

Why does rice get sticky?

{{cross_section: rice grain | starch granules}}
A grain sold as glutinous contains no gluten whatsoever. Rice gets sticky because cooking makes its starch granules swell and break down, and starch released at the grain surface acts as a glue between neighbouring grains. The word glutinous describes a texture, glue-like, not an ingredient. Gluten is a wheat protein, and rice stickiness is not a protein story at all. It is a starch story built from two molecules, which chemical databases such as PubChem list as separate entries: amylose and amylopectin. That much is settled. What it does not settle is why the same two molecules give you loose, separate grains of basmati in one pot and a single cohesive mass of sticky rice in another.

### Rice: Amylose holds grains apart as it cools

{{steps: Pasting test | heat a starch slurry while stirring | record viscosity as it thickens | cool it and record again | >setback tracks amylose}}
Why would amylose make grains less sticky? Part of the answer comes from viscosity instruments, such as the Rapid Visco Analyser, which heat a starch slurry while stirring and record viscosity as it thickens. The procedure is to heat a starch slurry while stirring, record viscosity as it thickens, then cool it and record again. On cooling, the paste firms up, a rise called setback, and setback tracks amylose. Linear amylose chains, freed from the granule, quickly find each other and pack back into ordered bundles, a process called retrogradation. That rapid re-ordering firms the grain and its surface, so the film between grains sets into something closer to a firm skin than a tacky paste.

{{text}}
Amylopectin behaves differently. Its bushy branches swell more freely, hold water and re-order only slowly, over days rather than hours. A grain rich in amylopectin stays soft, swollen and tacky at the surface, which is exactly the texture glutinous rice is prized for. The same retrogradation explains a familiar kitchen observation: day-old rice from the refrigerator is harder and less sticky than fresh rice, because its starch has partly re-ordered.

### Rice: The variety sets the range and the cook moves within it

{{contrast: long-grain | is=firmer and more separate | not=soft and tacky}}
Variety sets the range, but the method moves the result within it. Long-grain rice tends to cook firmer and more separate, while glutinous rice cooks soft and tacky, and no amount of technique fully swaps one for the other. More water lets granules swell further and release more starch. Stirring rubs grains together and scrapes starch off their surfaces, which is why risotto, made with constant stirring, turns creamy. Resting after cooking lets steam redistribute and the surface film set. Laboratories quantify the outcome with texture analysers, which press a probe into cooked rice and measure the force needed to pull it away, alongside trained tasting panels.

### Rice: Evidence limit

{{checklist: What this evidence settles | +stickiness comes from starch | +amylose content predicts much of it | -one number that defines stickiness | ?how much rinsing changes it}}
This evidence establishes that stickiness comes from starch, and that amylose content predicts much of it. It does not give a single number that defines stickiness: instruments and tasting panels measure different things, and they do not always agree. Two varieties with similar amylose can still cook differently, because amylopectin chain lengths, protein and fat content also play roles that remain under study. How much rinsing changes the result remains open. And none of this evidence says anything about nutrition or health.

### Rice: Closing

{{text}}
A word that sounds like wheat turns out to describe a missing enzyme. The glue on your rice is starch that escaped its granule, and how much escapes was written, in large part, into a single gene.

### Meat: Why does meat need to rest after cooking?

Why does meat need to rest after cooking?

{{stat: 3 | minutes | Rest time paired with 145 degrees Fahrenheit for whole cuts | USDA}}
Take a roast off the heat, and its centre keeps getting hotter. Resting lets two physical processes finish after the heat is gone: heat keeps travelling inward from the hot outer layers, so the centre continues to cook, and the meat cools enough that less of its liquid runs out when it is cut. The USDA's temperature guidance for whole cuts of beef, pork, veal and lamb pairs 145 degrees Fahrenheit with a 3-minute rest, because the temperature holds or climbs during that time. That figure settles the heat question. It does not settle the question most people mean, which is about the puddle on the board.

### Meat: The size of the cut decides how much any of this matters

{{chain: Why size matters | thick roast stores more heat | centre rises for longer | >resting changes a roast more than a thin steak}}
Put the two effects together and the role of size becomes clear. A thick roast stores more heat, so its centre rises for longer after it leaves the oven, and its outside and inside start further apart. Resting has more work to do on both the temperature and the liquid. A thin steak stores little heat, evens out quickly and cools fast whether you wait or not. For a thin steak, the gap between resting and not resting is small and hard to measure. Resting changes a roast more than a thin steak, which matches what the thermometer and the scale both report.

### Meat: Evidence limit

{{text}}
This evidence does not give a single correct resting time for every cut. The only time stated in this episode is the USDA's 3-minute rest, and that rest exists for temperature, not for juiciness. The evidence does not show that juice migrates to the centre. It also does not show that resting saves most of the liquid that cooking pushes loose, because much of that liquid is gone before the meat leaves the heat. It does not settle whether people can taste the difference in a blind comparison. Juiciness is partly a sensation, and sensory studies measure people, not water. Finally, the carryover rise has no fixed size. Mass, heat and time set its range.

### Meat: Closing

{{ambient}}
So the minutes after cooking are not empty. Heat is still crossing the meat from the outside in, and the loose liquid inside it is cooling and thickening. Resting is the name for letting both of those finish before the knife decides where the water goes.
