## YouTube title

Diamond is not tougher than steel

## Direct-answer lock

Diamond is harder than any metal, but it is not tougher. It resists scratching and indentation better than any steel. Yet it is brittle: an impact that would merely dent a steel bar can cleave or shatter a diamond. Whether diamond counts as "stronger" depends on which property you mean, whether that is hardness, strength or toughness, and each one is measured by a different test.

A gem no file can scratch can still chip against a doorframe. Diamond is harder than any metal, but it is not tougher. That figure answers one question cleanly. It does not answer the one most people mean when they say stronger. A 10 tells you what diamond can scratch. It says nothing about what happens when you drop it, bend it, or hit it. For that you need a different word and a different test, and the answer reverses.

### Stronger hides three different properties

{{define: Hardness | resistance to scratching and indentation | says nothing about impact | ASM International}}
Start with the word itself. In engineering, stronger is not one property. It is at least three. ASM International, the professional society for materials engineers, treats them as separate properties for good reason. Hardness is resistance to scratching and indentation, meaning how well a surface stops something sharper from digging in. It says nothing about impact. Strength is the stress a material can carry before it permanently deforms or breaks. Toughness is the energy a material can absorb while a crack is trying to grow through it.

{{contrast: Toughness | is=energy absorbed as a crack grows | not=resistance to scratching}}
Those three can point in opposite directions. Window glass resists scratching well, and you know what happens when you drop a glass. A rubber band is barely hard at all, yet it survives being stretched again and again. Diamond and steel are the sharpest example of the split. On hardness, diamond wins outright. On toughness, ordinary steel wins outright. Strength sits in between, and it depends on whether you push, pull or bend.

### The Mohs scale ranks, it does not measure

{{steps: Mohs scratch test | press a sharp edge | look for a groove | >The harder mineral leaves the mark}}
The 10 comes from a test you could run on a kitchen table. In the early nineteenth century, the German mineralogist Friedrich Mohs arranged ten reference minerals in order, each able to scratch the ones below it. You press a sharp edge of one mineral across another and look for a groove. The harder mineral leaves the mark. Talc sits at 1. Corundum, the mineral behind ruby and sapphire, sits at 9. Diamond sits at 10, and nothing natural scratches it.

{{text}}
But the scale is a ranking, not a ruler. It tells you order, not distance. The step from 9 to 10 looks the same on paper as the step from 1 to 2, and it is not. On instrumented hardness tests, the jump from corundum to diamond is far larger than any other step on the scale. Published comparisons put the size of that gap in different places, but none of them make it small. Steels, meanwhile, land somewhere in the middle of the ranking. Where exactly depends on the alloy and how it was heat treated. That spread is itself a clue: metal is not one material.

{{contrast: Pure carbon | is=diamond, a rigid three-dimensional network | not=graphite, flat sheets that slide}}
Why is diamond at the top at all? Each carbon atom shares electrons with four neighbours, building a rigid three-dimensional network of short, strong bonds. To scratch diamond, you have to break them. Graphite is also pure carbon, but its atoms sit in flat sheets that slide past each other, which is why pencil lead is soft. Same element, different arrangement, opposite hardness. The arrangement, not the element, decides.

### Indentation turns hardness into a number, and diamond has to test itself

{{chain: Indentation test | diamond pyramid | known load | measure the impression | >hardness number}}
To get a number rather than an order, laboratories press something into the surface. In the Vickers and Knoop tests, a tiny diamond pyramid is driven into the sample under a known load, held, then lifted away. Under a microscope, you measure the impression it left. A small mark under a large load means a hard material. Divide the load by the area of the mark and you have a hardness number. ASTM International publishes the standard methods, so a result from one laboratory can be compared with another. NIST works on the calibration that keeps those comparisons honest.

{{checklist: Measuring diamond with diamond | +the method works for steel | -the indenter is as hard as the sample | ?depends on crystal orientation}}
Notice the problem. The indenter is diamond. The method works for steel, because the tool is far harder than the sample, so only the sample deforms. For diamond, the indenter is as hard as the sample. It can blunt or crack during the test. The measured value also depends on crystal orientation, because diamond is harder along some directions than others. So diamond's hardness is measured with real care and real disagreement. The ranking is certain. The exact figure is not, which is why you will not hear one here.

### Toughness is where steel wins

{{steps: Fracture toughness test | grow a sharp crack | load the sample | record when the crack runs | >resistance to crack growth}}
Hardness asks what can scratch a material. Toughness asks a harder question: what happens once a crack already exists. In a fracture toughness test, engineers cut or grow a sharp crack into a sample, load the sample, and record when the crack runs. In a Charpy impact test, a weighted pendulum swings down and snaps a notched bar. The height the pendulum loses tells you how much energy the bar absorbed. Both kinds of test are standardized by ASTM International, and both reward the same thing: resistance to crack growth.

{{contrast: Steel under impact | is=dents and bends | not=shatters}}
Here steel and diamond swap places. In a metal, the bonding lets whole planes of atoms slip past one another along defects called dislocations. When a crack tip pushes forward, the metal around it flows, the tip blunts, and energy is soaked up as bending rather than breaking. That is why a steel bar dents and bends. In diamond, every carbon atom is locked to its neighbours by strong, directional covalent bonds. At room temperature there is almost no slip available. The energy has nowhere to go except into the crack, so the crack runs, and the stone shatters.

### Diamond breaks along its own geometry

{{ambient}}
Diamond's crystal structure adds a second weakness. Its atoms sit in a repeating lattice, and some planes through that lattice are easier to separate than others. These are cleavage planes. A sharp blow in the right direction can split a diamond cleanly along one of them. Diamond cutters exploited this for centuries, marking a rough stone and cleaving it with a single strike of a blade. Today sawing and lasers do much of that work, but the planes are still there in every finished gem.

{{text}}
That is why a diamond ring can chip. A knock against a doorframe at the wrong angle, on a thin edge, can take a flake off a stone that no file could ever scratch. Gemologists inspect the thin outer edge of a cut diamond for exactly this kind of damage. The hardest natural mineral can also be broken by an ordinary accident. Both facts are measured, and both are true.

### In a machine shop, iron dissolves diamond

{{chain: Cutting steel with diamond | the cutting edge gets hot | carbon atoms diffuse into the iron | >The tool wears away chemically}}
If diamond is the hardest material, you might expect every steel-cutting tool to be tipped with it. Plenty of tools are tipped with diamond, but not for steel. Diamond tooling excels at cutting stone, glass, ceramics and non-ferrous metals such as aluminium. Against steel at machining speeds, the cutting edge gets hot, and at those temperatures carbon atoms diffuse into the iron. The tool wears away chemically, not mechanically. The hardness contest is never even decided.

{{define: Cubic boron nitride | a synthetic material made from boron and nitrogen | used because hot iron attacks diamond | ASM International}}
For hardened steel, shops usually reach for cubic boron nitride, a synthetic material made from boron and nitrogen. It is used because hot iron attacks diamond. Heat matters in open air too. Diamond is pure carbon, and heated strongly in oxygen it will burn. Jewellers repairing a setting with a torch take this seriously. So even the claim that diamond beats steel at hardness carries a condition: room temperature, a scratch, no chemistry involved. Change the temperature or the chemistry, and the contest changes.

### Squeezed, diamond is formidable; small, it bends

{{contrast: Diamond under compression | is=formidable | not=the same as under tension}}
Strength, the third word, depends on direction. Squeeze diamond and it is formidable. Physicists build diamond anvil cells, two gem-quality diamonds pressed tip to tip, to crush tiny samples to pressures found deep inside planets. Compression pushes cracks closed. Pull or bend the same diamond and cracks are pulled open, and any tiny flaw becomes the starting point of failure. Diamond's behaviour under compression is not the same as under tension, and a single word cannot hold both.

{{sources: Who measures what | Smithsonian=the hardest natural mineral | ASTM International=the standard methods | MIT=nanoscale diamond needles | Science=bent far more than bulk diamond}}
Size changes the picture too. A research team that included engineers at MIT reported in the journal Science that nanoscale diamond needles bent far more than bulk diamond before snapping, then sprang back. The likely reason is flaws. A large crystal almost always contains some defect where a crack can start. A needle that small may contain none. Strength, in other words, belongs to an object as much as to a material. A flawless sliver of diamond and a gem with a hidden inclusion are the same substance with very different breaking points.

### What to notice in the edit

{{checklist: Before you accept stronger | +which property | +which test | ?which material}}
When you see a claim that one material is stronger than another, watch for the swap. A sentence will open with hardness and close with toughness, as if they were one quantity. Ask three things: which property, which test, and which material. Ask which property, because hardness, strength and toughness can rank the same pair in opposite orders. Ask which test, because a scratch ranking and an indentation number are different kinds of evidence. Ask which material, because steel alone covers a huge range, and metal covers far more.

### Evidence limit

{{text}}
This comparison does not produce a single verdict, and the evidence does not support one. There is no agreed single hardness figure for diamond. The measurement uses diamond to test diamond, and the result shifts with crystal direction. Natural single crystals, synthetic diamond grown in a lab, and polycrystalline diamond made of many fused grains behave differently, especially in toughness. Results for one do not transfer cleanly to another. The nanoscale bending results describe needles far smaller than any gem, not rings or drill bits. And metal is a category, not a material: a soft pure metal and a hardened tool steel sit far apart on every one of these tests.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So diamond is harder than any metal, and steel is tougher than diamond. Both statements come from instruments: a scratch, a pyramid pressed into a surface, a pendulum swinging through a notched bar. The word stronger was never going to carry all three.
