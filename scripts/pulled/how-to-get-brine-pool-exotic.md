Ordinary seawater averages about 35 parts per thousand salt, according to NOAA. Brine pools are several times saltier, and the exact figure differs from pool to pool. That difference is the whole story, but a number alone settles very little. It does not tell you where the salt came from, why it stays put, or why life crowds the rim instead of fleeing it.

### The salt was laid down by seas that dried out

{{chain: Where the salt comes from | ancient seas evaporate | the salt is buried | the salt rises | >seawater dissolves it into brine}}
Start with the salt, because a brine pool is really a salt deposit being slowly unpacked. The sequence runs like this. First, ancient seas evaporate faster than fresh seawater can refill them, and they leave behind thick beds of rock salt. Next, the salt is buried, as mud and sand pile on top of it over immense spans of time. Then the salt rises. Rock salt is lighter than the sediment around it, and under enough weight it creeps like an extremely stiff fluid, pushing upward in domes and sheets. Finally, where that salt comes close to the seafloor, seawater dissolves it into brine, and the brine leaks out.

{{contrast: brine | is=water heavy with dissolved salt | not=a separate kind of liquid}}
It is worth being exact about what brine is. It is still water. Brine is water heavy with dissolved salt, not a separate kind of liquid. What changes is its density. Water loaded with salt weighs more, volume for volume, than ordinary seawater, and that single property drives almost everything else in this story. Geologists know the salt is there because seismic surveys, which bounce sound off buried layers, reveal the shapes of salt bodies beneath the Gulf, and because drilling has sampled them directly. The brine on the seafloor is the visible end of that buried structure.

### Density, not walls, holds the pool together

{{text}}
A brine pool has no container in the usual sense. Nothing walls it in except gravity. The heavy brine flows downhill across the seabed and settles into whatever low ground it finds: a hollow, a crater, a fold in the mud. Because it is denser, it does not stir into the seawater above. It sits beneath it, the way oil sits on top of water, only reversed.

{{steps: Reading the interface | the surface is sharp | the surface carries waves | the surface reflects light | >the boundary is density, not a membrane}}
Footage from remotely operated vehicles shows this in three ways. First, the surface is sharp. Cameras show a clear line where seawater ends and brine begins, often with a faint haze where the two touch. Second, the surface carries waves. When a vehicle or a sinking object disturbs it, ripples roll across the pool and lap against its edge, which is why researchers speak of shorelines. Third, the surface reflects light from the vehicle's lamps, so the pool can look like a mirror lying on the seabed. None of this means the boundary is solid. The boundary is density, not a membrane. Push through it and you are simply in heavier water.

{{text}}
A pool also has to be fed. Brine at its surface slowly mixes with seawater and is lost, so a pool that lasts must be topped up from below, by brine still seeping out of the sediment. Where the supply is strong, the brine can spill over the lip of its hollow and run downslope in narrow channels, like a stream on land. Where the supply fails, the pool should slowly thin and fade. A brine pool is therefore not a fixed object but a balance between what leaks in and what mixes away.

### Low oxygen turns the shoreline into a trap

{{checklist: What the brine lacks and carries | -oxygen | +methane | +hydrogen sulfide | ?the exact mix in each pool}}
The brine is usually starved of oxygen. Water that sits still does not exchange gases with the water above it the way moving seawater does, and microbes inside it use up what little oxygen arrives. Many pools also carry dissolved methane and hydrogen sulfide, gases that rise with fluids from the buried sediment. The exact mix in each pool is not the same, and it has to be measured pool by pool rather than assumed.

{{ambient}}
For an animal, that combination is dangerous. A fish or a crab that wanders over the edge enters water with almost no oxygen, and it can be stunned or killed. Remotely operated vehicles have filmed animals lying motionless inside brine pools, and the brine can preserve their bodies, because the scavengers and much of the decay that would normally clear them away cannot work there. So brine pools kill what swims into them, even when they look perfectly calm.

### The edge is crowded because the chemistry is rich

{{anatomy: A brine pool's rim | brine@0.5,0.75 | mussel bed@0.25,0.5 | seawater@0.5,0.2}}
And yet the rim is often the busiest place in sight. Around some pools in the Gulf of Mexico, a dense mussel bed forms a ring right at the shoreline, sometimes so neatly that the pool looks framed. The reason is chemistry. These mussels host bacteria inside their gills that can use methane as a source of energy. The animals sit where seawater, with its oxygen, meets brine, with its methane. They need both, and the edge is the only place both are available at once.

{{define: chemosynthesis | making food from chemical energy rather than sunlight | depends on chemicals reaching the animal, not on light | Smithsonian}}
This is chemosynthesis: making food from chemical energy rather than sunlight. The Smithsonian describes deep-sea communities built on it at vents and seeps, and its boundary matters here. It depends on chemicals reaching the animal, not on light. That is why a brine pool can host a thriving ring of life in total darkness. Researchers know the bacteria do the work because they have examined the mussels' gill tissue under microscopes, identified the bacteria from their genetic material, and traced the carbon in the mussels' flesh back to methane through its chemical signature. Several separate lines of evidence point the same way.

{{text}}
Inside the brine, the picture is different but not empty. Microbes live there, single-celled organisms adapted to salt levels that would pull the water out of most cells. Researchers find them by filtering brine samples and reading the genetic material they contain, then growing what they can in the laboratory. That method is good at finding what is there. It is weaker at proving how active a microbe was in the pool, which is why claims about life inside the brine are made carefully.

### Not every brine pool forms the same way

{{contrast: brine pool | is=a pool of dense, salty water | not=one single kind of place}}
The Gulf of Mexico is the best-known example, but it is not the template for every pool. In the Red Sea, where the seafloor is being pulled apart, some deep basins hold brines that are hot as well as salty, heated by the same volcanic activity that is opening the sea. In the deep Mediterranean, researchers have found basins filled with brine where buried salt from an ancient episode of evaporation has dissolved. So a brine pool is a pool of dense, salty water, not one single kind of place. The salt can come from dissolving old rock salt, from hot fluids rising through new crust, or from both, and each route gives the pool a different temperature and chemistry.

### A brine pool is confirmed by visiting it, not by mapping it

{{sources: Who goes and looks | Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution=operates the submersible Alvin | MBARI=runs remotely operated vehicles | NOAA Ocean Exploration=publishes expedition findings}}
Finding a candidate is the easy part. Ships map the seafloor with sonar, and a depression or an unusually smooth patch can hint that something dense is sitting in it. But a map cannot tell brine from mud. Confirmation means going there. Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution operates the submersible Alvin, the kind of vehicle that carries scientists down to look at seafloor features like these. MBARI runs remotely operated vehicles that work the deep seafloor on tethers from a ship. NOAA Ocean Exploration publishes expedition findings, including footage, from its own dives.

{{steps: Confirming a pool | map the seafloor | dive to the site | measure the water | sample the brine | >confirmation needs a visit}}
The working method follows a fixed order. Teams map the seafloor, dive to the site, measure the water with probes for salinity, temperature and oxygen, and sample the brine in sealed bottles to bring back for analysis. Only the last steps confirm a pool, because confirmation needs a visit. Each dive is limited by batteries, weather and ship time, which is one reason the number of brine pools studied in detail remains small.

### What to notice in the edit

{{ambient}}
When you watch footage of a brine pool, watch the edge, not the middle. The interesting physics and the interesting biology are both at the boundary. Look for the ripple when a vehicle's thrusters disturb the surface, and for the haze where the two waters meet. Notice too that the lights make the scene. Down there it is dark. Every colour you see was carried down on the vehicle, and the shine on the pool is the vehicle's own lamps reflected back.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +brine is denser and sinks | +most pools lack oxygen | ?whether any animal lives inside | ?how typical the studied pools are}}
The probes, cameras and sample bottles leave two questions hanging over the brine itself. It is solid that brine is denser and sinks, and that most pools lack oxygen. It is not settled whether any animal lives inside the brine itself rather than visiting its edge. Researchers working in the deep Mediterranean once reported tiny animals that appeared to live permanently without oxygen in a brine basin. Other scientists have questioned whether those animals were living there or had drifted in after death, and the question remains open. The second gap is sampling. Only a handful of pools have been studied up close, mostly in a few well-surveyed regions, so we cannot yet say how typical the studied pools are. Salinity figures in particular should be read as belonging to a specific pool on a specific dive, not to brine pools in general.

### Closing

{{text}}
So the strangeness of a brine pool is not mysterious. It is salt from old seas, gravity, and a shortage of oxygen, arranged so that one narrow edge holds everything life needs. The lake is lethal in the middle and generous at the shore, and the boundary is where you learn the most.
