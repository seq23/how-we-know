## YouTube title

## Direct-answer lock

Rice gets sticky because cooking makes its starch granules swell and break down, and starch released at the grain surface acts as a glue between neighbouring grains. How sticky a variety becomes depends mostly on the balance of rice's two starch molecules. Amylose keeps grains firmer and more separate. Amylopectin dominates the soft, cohesive, glutinous varieties.

A grain sold as glutinous contains no gluten whatsoever. Rice gets sticky because cooking makes its starch granules swell and break down, and starch released at the grain surface acts as a glue between neighbouring grains. The word glutinous describes a texture, glue-like, not an ingredient. Gluten is a wheat protein, and rice stickiness is not a protein story at all. It is a starch story built from two molecules, which chemical databases such as PubChem list as separate entries: amylose and amylopectin. That much is settled. What it does not settle is why the same two molecules give you loose, separate grains of basmati in one pot and a single cohesive mass of sticky rice in another.

### Stickiness lives in starch, not in protein

{{contrast: glutinous | is=glue-like | not=gluten}}
Start with what is inside a grain of milled white rice. Most of it is endosperm, the stored food the seed would have used to grow, and most of that endosperm is starch. Proteins are present, but they are minor partners here. Wheat dough becomes elastic because two families of wheat proteins link into a stretchy network when the flour is wetted and worked. Rice has no comparable network. So when rice clumps, the glue has to be coming from somewhere else, and every line of evidence points to the starch.

{{define: amylose | a mostly linear chain of glucose | amylopectin is the heavily branched form | PubChem}}
Starch is not one substance. It is glucose, linked into long chains, and those chains come in two architectures. Amylose is mostly linear: a long chain that can coil into a helix. Amylopectin is heavily branched, a bushy molecule with many short chains hanging off a backbone, and it is far larger than amylose. Both are catalogued by PubChem, the chemical database run under the National Institutes of Health. Inside the grain, these molecules are packed into tiny starch granules, and rice granules are among the smallest of the cereal starches. Within each granule, the branches of amylopectin line up side by side into ordered, crystalline layers, with amylose threaded through the less ordered regions.

{{checklist: What the uncooked granule is | +packed with amylose and amylopectin | +partly crystalline | -swollen | -sticky}}
That ordered packing is the key to everything that follows. A raw granule is dense, partly crystalline and resistant to water. Uncooked rice is hard and not sticky, because its starch is locked away in that structure. Stickiness only appears when that structure is taken apart, and the methods that show it happening are worth looking at one by one.

### Heat and water take the granule apart below boiling

{{chain: Seeing the granule change | polarized light | X-ray diffraction | differential scanning calorimetry | >the crystalline order is lost}}
The process is called gelatinization, and it was not inferred from texture alone. Three instruments show it from different angles. Under polarized light, a raw starch granule glows with a dark cross across it, called a Maltese cross, because its ordered layers bend light. As the granule is heated in water, the cross fades and disappears. X-ray diffraction gives the same verdict in another language: raw cereal starch scatters X-rays into a characteristic pattern of sharp rings, and after heating in water that pattern collapses into a diffuse blur. Differential scanning calorimetry measures heat flow, and it records a distinct absorption of energy at the moment the crystals melt. Three independent signals, one conclusion: the crystalline order is lost.

{{kitchen: 100 | boiling point}}
Here is a detail that surprises many cooks. Rice starch gelatinizes well below the boiling point. Water at sea level boils at 100 degrees Celsius, and a pot of simmering rice sits close to that, but the granules begin swelling and losing their order at noticeably lower temperatures. Different varieties gelatinize at different temperatures, and rice breeders classify grains as low, intermediate or high on that scale. The published values vary with variety and method, so this script will not quote a single figure. What matters is that by the time the pot is simmering, gelatinization is well under way.

### What leaks out of the grain becomes the glue

{{steps: From granule to glue | granules swell | starch leaches out | the surface film dries | >grains bond where films touch}}
Once the crystals melt, water floods in and the granules swell, many times their original volume. Swelling is not tidy. As the granules swell, some starch leaches out, both from granules at the surface of each grain and from the fine starch dust left behind by milling. That released starch forms a thin, viscous film on the outside of every grain. When cooking stops and steam escapes, the surface film dries and thickens. Where two grains touch, their films merge, and the grains bond where films touch. Electron microscopes show this directly: images of cooked rice surfaces reveal a layer of smeared, gelled material that is absent on raw grains. Food chemists publishing in journals such as the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, from the American Chemical Society, have analysed that surface material and linked its quantity and composition to measured stickiness.

{{contrast: rinsing | is=removing surface starch | not=changing the starch inside}}
This is also why rinsing rice before cooking can change the result. Rinsing washes away loose starch dust from the outside of milled grains, so less material is available to form that first surface film. It does not alter the starch locked inside each grain, which still gelatinizes and still releases some starch during cooking. Studies of rinsing report effects that differ by variety and by how thoroughly the rice is washed, so the honest summary is modest. Rinsing reduces one source of glue. It does not remove the mechanism.

### One gene sets most of the ratio

{{define: Waxy gene | encodes granule-bound starch synthase | without it, the grain makes almost no amylose | USDA Agricultural Research Service}}
So why is glutinous rice so much stickier than basmati, if both gelatinize and both release starch? The answer lies in the ratio of the two molecules, and much of that ratio is controlled by a single gene. It is called the Waxy gene, and it encodes an enzyme called granule-bound starch synthase, which builds amylose inside the granule. When that enzyme is broken, without it, the grain makes almost no amylose. Its starch is almost entirely amylopectin. Glutinous rice varieties carry broken versions of this gene. Rice geneticists, including those at the USDA Agricultural Research Service, which breeds rice and studies its grain quality, use the Waxy gene as one of the main markers for predicting how a new line will cook.

{{chain: The iodine test | add iodine to starch | amylose turns deep blue | amylopectin turns reddish-brown | >colour estimates amylose content}}
The oldest way to see this needs no sequencer. Iodine slips inside the helical coil of amylose and turns it a deep blue. Amylopectin, with its short branches, cannot hold iodine the same way, and stains a reddish-brown instead. Cut a grain of ordinary rice, add iodine, and it turns dark blue. Do the same with glutinous rice and it turns reddish-brown. Measured carefully, with a spectrophotometer reading the depth of the colour, this reaction becomes a standard laboratory method in which colour estimates amylose content, used by breeding programmes and grain-quality laboratories.

{{sources: Who measures rice starch | USDA Agricultural Research Service=rice breeding and grain quality | Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry=starch structure and stickiness | Royal Society of Chemistry=the chemistry of starch}}
Across those sources a consistent pattern appears. Varieties with relatively high amylose, such as many long-grain types, cook firmer and more separate. Varieties with lower amylose, such as many short-grain types, cook softer and stickier. Glutinous rice, with almost no amylose, sits at the extreme. Researchers publishing on starch structure, and bodies such as the Royal Society of Chemistry that cover the chemistry of starch, describe the same direction of effect, even where their exact measurements differ.

### Amylose holds grains apart as it cools

{{steps: Pasting test | heat a starch slurry while stirring | record viscosity as it thickens | cool it and record again | >setback tracks amylose}}
Why would amylose make grains less sticky? Part of the answer comes from viscosity instruments, such as the Rapid Visco Analyser, which heat a starch slurry while stirring and record viscosity as it thickens. The procedure is to heat a starch slurry while stirring, record viscosity as it thickens, then cool it and record again. On cooling, the paste firms up, a rise called setback, and setback tracks amylose. Linear amylose chains, freed from the granule, quickly find each other and pack back into ordered bundles, a process called retrogradation. That rapid re-ordering firms the grain and its surface, so the film between grains sets into something closer to a firm skin than a tacky paste.

{{text}}
Amylopectin behaves differently. Its bushy branches swell more freely, hold water and re-order only slowly, over days rather than hours. A grain rich in amylopectin stays soft, swollen and tacky at the surface, which is exactly the texture glutinous rice is prized for. The same retrogradation explains a familiar kitchen observation: day-old rice from the refrigerator is harder and less sticky than fresh rice, because its starch has partly re-ordered.

### The variety sets the range and the cook moves within it

{{contrast: long-grain | is=firmer and more separate | not=soft and tacky}}
Variety sets the range, but the method moves the result within it. Long-grain rice tends to cook firmer and more separate, while glutinous rice cooks soft and tacky, and no amount of technique fully swaps one for the other. More water lets granules swell further and release more starch. Stirring rubs grains together and scrapes starch off their surfaces, which is why risotto, made with constant stirring, turns creamy. Resting after cooking lets steam redistribute and the surface film set. Laboratories quantify the outcome with texture analysers, which press a probe into cooked rice and measure the force needed to pull it away, alongside trained tasting panels.

### What to notice in the edit

When the cross-section glows, watch the granules, not the grain. The grain barely changes shape, while its starch granules swell and spill. ### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What this evidence settles | +stickiness comes from starch | +amylose content predicts much of it | -one number that defines stickiness | ?how much rinsing changes it}}
This evidence establishes that stickiness comes from starch, and that amylose content predicts much of it. It does not give a single number that defines stickiness: instruments and tasting panels measure different things, and they do not always agree. Two varieties with similar amylose can still cook differently, because amylopectin chain lengths, protein and fat content also play roles that remain under study. How much rinsing changes the result remains open. And none of this evidence says anything about nutrition or health.

### Closing

{{text}}
A word that sounds like wheat turns out to describe a missing enzyme. The glue on your rice is starch that escaped its granule, and how much escapes was written, in large part, into a single gene.
