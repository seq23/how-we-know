## Direct-answer lock

Yes: sea pigs are real deep-sea animals, sea cucumbers in the genus Scotoplanes, and the name is a nickname, not a relationship to pigs. They are echinoderms, relatives of sea stars and sea urchins, that walk across the deep seafloor on water-filled tube feet and eat organic material from the sediment. ### Title card

Are sea pigs real?

### The name is a nickname; the animal is an echinoderm

{{define: sea pig | a sea cucumber in the genus Scotoplanes | not a pig | MBARI}}
Start with the name, because it causes most of the confusion. "Sea pig" is a common name, the kind people give an animal because of how it looks. It is not a scientific claim. The scientific identity is this: a sea pig is a sea cucumber in the genus Scotoplanes. Sea cucumbers are echinoderms, the same group of animals as sea stars, brittle stars and sea urchins. They have no relationship to pigs, or to any mammal. The resemblance is all surface. A sea pig is plump, pinkish and often translucent, with a row of stubby legs underneath and a few soft projections on its back that can look like ears or horns. On a dim screen, that shape reads as a small pink pig. That is the whole story behind the name, and it is a story about appearance, not kinship. A sea pig is not a pig, in the same way a sea star is not a star.

{{ambient}}
So the real question is not whether something pig-like lives in the deep ocean. It is whether this particular sea cucumber exists the way the pictures show it. And that turns into a question about evidence. Who saw it? With what instrument? Has anyone actually held one, measured it and put it in a jar?

### Looking unreal is not the same as being unreal

{{text}}
Part of the doubt comes from a reasonable instinct. Sea pigs look like a cartoon, and cartoons are invented. But the deep sea is full of bodies that look odd to a land animal like you, because the conditions shaping them are unlike anything at the surface. There is no sunlight. The water is cold. Food is scarce. And the weight of the water column above presses on everything. A soft, water-filled body with no hard skeleton to protect is not a disadvantage in a place like that. Strange means unfamiliar. It does not mean fake. The way to tell the two apart is not to judge the picture by how believable it feels. It is to ask where the picture came from, and whether anyone else, using different equipment on a different day, has seen the same thing.

### Footage from the seafloor is the first line of evidence

{{chain: Seeing it alive | remotely operated vehicle | camera | video archive | >filmed alive}}
Most people who doubt sea pigs have seen only a still image, often a heavily shared one, and sometimes a soft toy. The stronger evidence is moving footage. Institutions such as MBARI send remotely operated vehicles, robots tethered to a ship by a cable, down to the deep seafloor. Each remotely operated vehicle carries lights and a camera, and pilots on the ship steer it while scientists watch the live feed. When a sea pig drifts into frame, the encounter is recorded and logged with the depth and location reported by the vehicle's own instruments. That footage goes into a video archive that researchers return to for years, annotating what appears in each frame.

{{sources: Who filmed them | MBARI=filmed sea pigs alive | NOAA Ocean Exploration=film deep-sea sea cucumbers}}
This matters because a single photograph can be staged, mislabeled or misunderstood. A video archive is much harder to fake in that way. It holds many encounters, from different dives, with the same kind of animal behaving the same way: walking slowly across soft sediment, its body flexing as it moves. When the same thing keeps turning up, the simplest explanation is that the thing is there. MBARI has filmed sea pigs alive in the deep sea. Exploration programs such as NOAA Ocean Exploration film deep-sea sea cucumbers of many kinds on their dives and share that footage publicly. No single clip carries the claim. The accumulation does.

### Specimens settle what cameras cannot

{{steps: From footage to species | collect a specimen | preserve it | examine the ossicles | compare against described species | >a name tied to a physical type}}
Footage shows that an animal exists. It does not, on its own, tell you exactly what the animal is. For that, scientists need specimens. A remotely operated vehicle can collect a specimen with a suction sampler or a robotic arm, and older expeditions dredged animals up from the bottom with nets dragged along the seafloor. Once the animal is on deck, it is photographed, and then the team must preserve it so it can be studied in a laboratory and kept in a museum collection, where it can be checked again long after the voyage ends.

{{text}}
Sea cucumber identification leans on details that no camera captures well. Many sea cucumbers have ossicles, tiny pieces of mineral embedded in the skin, and their shapes differ between groups. Taxonomists examine the ossicles under a microscope, describe the tube feet and other structures, and compare against described species, each of which is anchored to a physical type specimen held in a collection. Increasingly, they sequence DNA as well. In other words, a name tied to a physical type, not to a picture. That is why the genus Scotoplanes is a defined thing rather than a loose label. There are preserved animals in collections that anyone with the right training can re-examine and argue about.

### The legs run on water pressure, not on bones

{{contrast: tube feet | is=water-filled tube feet | not=jointed legs}}
Once you accept that sea pigs exist, the next fair question is how they walk, because the legs are both the most pig-like part and the least intuitive. They are not legs in the sense that your legs are legs. They are not jointed legs with bones inside. They are water-filled tube feet, part of a system that echinoderms share, called the water vascular system. It is a network of internal canals filled with fluid. By shifting that fluid and contracting muscles, the animal can extend or shorten each tube foot, a little like pressing on one end of a water balloon to push out the other.

{{text}}
In most sea cucumbers, the tube feet are small and numerous, used for gripping surfaces. In sea pigs, some of them are enlarged into stubby walking feet along the underside, and the soft projections on the back are modified tube feet too. How is it known that this is hydraulic, and not simply muscle? Because the anatomy of dissected specimens shows the canals connecting to the tube feet, the same plan found across echinoderms, from sea stars to urchins. The walking is visible in footage. The mechanism is visible on the dissecting table. Two different kinds of evidence, gathered in two different places, describe the same animal.

### Sea pigs live on food that falls from far above

{{zones: abyssal zone}}
To understand what sea pigs eat, you need a sense of where they live. They have been recorded across a wide band of deep-sea depths, from continental slopes down to abyssal plains, and the published ranges vary between species and between surveys. NOAA's layers of the ocean place the abyssal zone beginning at 4,000 meters. Sunlight does not reach anywhere near that far. No plants grow there. Almost all food arrives from above, as a slow fall of dead plankton, waste and other organic particles that scientists call marine snow. It settles onto the seafloor as a thin, uneven film.

{{ambient}}
Sea pigs are deposit feeders. They use tentacles around the mouth to gather the surface layer of sediment, and their gut extracts the organic material from it. Studies of deep-sea sea cucumbers suggest that many of them favor the freshest material, the part of marine snow that has only recently settled. This is one reason ecologists pay attention to them. Animals like sea pigs work through the seafloor's surface, and they are among the animals that use food arriving from the sunlit ocean far above. Seafloor surveys have found that sea cucumbers can make up a large share of the visible animal life on parts of the deep floor. Exactly how large varies from place to place, and from one survey method to another.

### Herds are observed; their purpose is not settled

{{checklist: What the footage shows | +seen in groups | +facing the same direction | ?why they gather | -how many exist}}
One of the striking things in seafloor footage is that sea pigs are often seen in groups, sometimes many in view at once. In some recordings, many of them appear to be facing the same direction. Researchers have suggested that facing into the current might help them detect food arriving from upstream, the way a scent carries on a breeze. That is a reasonable hypothesis. It is not a demonstrated fact. Footage records position. It does not record purpose, and a group in one frame may have formed around a patch of food, in response to the current, or for a reason no one has yet considered. So the checklist is uneven. Seen in groups: yes. Facing the same direction: in some footage. Why they gather: open. How many exist: unknown.

{{text}}
Something similar applies to the small animals that have been seen with them. Footage has shown small crabs sheltering under or beside sea pigs. That association is real in the sense that it was recorded. Whether it helps the crab, harms the sea pig, or makes no difference to either is a separate question, and one that a camera alone cannot answer.

### What to notice in the edit

As you watch, notice which images are footage and which are stills. Notice which shots come from remotely operated vehicles, with the lights casting a cone onto soft sediment, and which are illustrations made to show you a shape. The claim that sea pigs are real rests on footage and specimens, not a single photograph. When a drawing appears, it is there to show the outline people recognize, not to stand in as evidence for it.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What this evidence settles | +sea pigs exist | +what group they belong to | -how many there are | ?how long they live}}
This evidence establishes that sea pigs exist, what group they belong to, how they move and broadly what they eat. It does not establish how many there are. The deep seafloor is vast and only a small fraction of it has ever been seen by cameras, so any population estimate would be an extrapolation from a limited number of sites. There is no reliable figure for how long they live. How many species belong in the genus Scotoplanes, and how they are related to each other, depends on how specimens are classified and on genetic work that is still limited. And the reasons sea pigs gather remain a hypothesis. When you meet confident numbers about sea pigs, ask where they came from.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So yes, they are real. Not a pig, and not a hoax, but a sea cucumber walking on water-filled feet across the deep seafloor, filmed alive and kept in collections, with most of its life still out of view.
