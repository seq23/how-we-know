### The word largest needs a measuring rule

{{sources: TWO DIFFERENT KINDS OF RECORD | Giant squid=greater total length, because the feeding tentacles are extremely long | Colossal squid=more compact and much heavier}}
Colossal squid and giant squid are often placed in the same headline, but they hold different kinds of records. Giant squid can reach greater total length because their feeding tentacles are extremely long. Colossal squid are more compact and much heavier.

{{contrast: THE TE PAPA SPECIMEN | is=The heaviest known invertebrate specimen | not=Necessarily the longest cephalopod}}
The best-known preserved female at Te Papa in New Zealand weighs 495 kilograms and measures about 4.2 meters. That makes it the heaviest known invertebrate specimen, not necessarily the longest cephalopod. The distinction matters because drawings often stretch a colossal squid to giant-squid proportions, while headlines switch between length and mass without warning.

{{checklist: WHY SPECIMENS ARE HARD TO COMPARE | ?Specimens change after capture and preservation | ?Tentacles can be missing | ?Tissue contracts | >Measurements from an intact animal beat estimates reconstructed from a beak}}
Specimens also change after capture and preservation. Tentacles can be missing. Tissue contracts. Measurements taken from an intact animal are more useful than estimates reconstructed from a beak. The first open loop is visual: why would an animal living in deep Antarctic water invest so much biological material in eyes that can measure roughly 27 centimeters across?

### Eyes built to detect a large moving signal

{{stat: 27 | CENTIMETRES | Estimated eye diameter in a living animal | Te Papa}}
Te Papa's anatomical work describes colossal squid eyes as the largest animal eyes ever studied. In a living animal, the diameter is estimated around 27 centimeters, with pupils several centimeters wide. Large eyes collect more light, but simple brightness is not the whole explanation. Modeling work has suggested that very large deep-sea eyes may be especially useful for detecting the broad bioluminescent disturbance made when a sperm whale moves through surrounding plankton. The specimen evidence proves the eye is enormous. The exact ecological advantage is still tested through models and comparative anatomy because healthy colossal squid are not routinely observed in the wild. The eyes also carry light organs near them, an unusual arrangement whose behavior is not fully documented.

{{steps: THE EVIDENCE LAYERS | Direct measurement=of the organ | Strong optical principles | A more tentative hypothesis=about the signal it detects}}
This is a useful example of evidence layers: direct measurement of the organ, strong optical principles, and a more tentative hypothesis about the signal it detects. The next question is what the squid does when the target is close enough to strike.

### Rotating hooks, a beak, and very little live footage

{{contrast: WHAT THE ANATOMY PROVIDES | is=Swiveling hooks on the tentacle clubs | is=Suckers and smaller hooks on the arms | is=A robust beak | not=A complete hunting sequence from a healthy adult at depth}}
The colossal squid's tentacle clubs carry swiveling hooks. Some can rotate in their sockets, while others are fixed. The arms carry suckers and smaller hooks. The beak is robust, as expected for a large predatory cephalopod. These structures are available for direct study because specimens have been recovered from Antarctic fisheries. What is missing is a complete hunting sequence from a healthy adult at depth. It is tempting to animate the hooks like grappling equipment and assign a dramatic attack speed, but the anatomy does not provide a full behavior script. Stomach contents, predator remains, and distribution records suggest prey such as Antarctic fishes and interactions with sperm whales. Scars on whales show that large squids are not passive prey.

{{checklist: NOT RESOLVED BY ONE BODY IN A MUSEUM TANK | ?Ambush | ?Active pursuit | ?Scavenging}}
Still, the balance between ambush, active pursuit, and scavenging is not resolved by one body in a museum tank. The open loop becomes a scientific one: how do you describe an animal accurately when nearly every close look begins after it has left its normal environment?

### A specimen is a library, not a movie

{{sources: EACH METHOD ANSWERS A DIFFERENT QUESTION | The preserved specimen=eyes, hooks, beak, tissue and internal anatomy, revisited as methods improve | Fisheries records=where encounters occur | Beaks in sperm-whale stomachs=predator-prey relationships, after soft tissue has vanished}}
Te Papa's preserved colossal squid has supported repeated examinations of eyes, hooks, beak, tissue, and internal anatomy. Samples can be revisited as methods improve. That makes the specimen a durable evidence object. It does not show migration routes, courtship, hunting posture, or lifespan. Fisheries records tell researchers where encounters occur, but encounter location is not always the animal's preferred depth. Beaks in sperm-whale stomachs reveal predator-prey relationships, but soft tissue has already vanished. Each method answers a different question.

{{contrast: WHAT WE KNOW | is=Its mass can exceed any other confirmed invertebrate | is=Its eyes are extraordinary | is=Its tentacles carry rotating hooks | not=Migration routes, courtship, hunting posture, or lifespan}}
The right conclusion is not that the colossal squid is unknowable. We know its mass can exceed any other confirmed invertebrate, its eyes are extraordinary, and its tentacles carry rotating hooks. We also know the viral life story is much more complete than the evidence. A good episode preserves that gap. ### Measure the specimen before imagining the monster

{{stat: 4.2 | METRES | A measured specimen, not a projected maximum | Te Papa}}
Colossal squid and giant squid are different animals. Colossal squid have a heavier body, rotating hooks on parts of the arms and tentacles, a beak, and extremely large eyes. But live behavior remains poorly documented.

{{sources: TWO CATEGORIES OF NUMBER | Museums=measure a specimen's mass, mantle, arms, tentacles, beak and eyes | A theoretical maximum=uses incomplete specimens, beaks from predator stomachs, and assumptions about body proportions}}
Museums can measure a specimen's mass, mantle, arms, tentacles, beak, and eyes. Estimates of a theoretical maximum use incomplete specimens, beaks found in predator stomachs, and assumptions about body proportions. Those projections may be useful, but they should not be narrated as if a complete animal was placed on a scale.

### The record belongs to a specimen, not a silhouette

{{checklist: WHAT STILL NEEDS CONTEXT | ?The animal was frozen, thawed, unfolded and preserved | ?Tentacles can contract or be damaged | ?Water and ice can affect mass | >Total length, mantle length and mass answer different size questions}}
Te Papa's displayed specimen is useful precisely because it can be measured, re-examined, and compared with other material. Its reported mass and total length are real specimen values, but even they require context. The animal was frozen, thawed, unfolded, and preserved. Tentacles can contract or be damaged. Water and ice can affect mass. Total length, mantle length, and mass therefore answer different size questions.

{{ambient}}
Projected maxima are a separate category. Larger beaks recovered from sperm whale stomachs suggest that larger colossal squid exist, but the relationship between beak size and total body mass is not yet supported by a large sample of complete specimens.

{{checklist: THREE LABELS THAT MUST LOOK DIFFERENT ON SCREEN | +Measured | ?Estimated | ?Possible}}
The video should never place a projected giant beside the measured specimen without a label. 'Measured,' 'estimated,' and 'possible' must look different on screen. That visual honesty lets the animal remain extraordinary without borrowing certainty from an incomplete record.

### Evidence limit

{{contrast: THE 495-KILOGRAM ANIMAL | is=A measured specimen associated with Te Papa | not=Proof of a species maximum | not=A universal exact record without a current source}}
Size records depend on whether the claim refers to mass, total length, mantle length, or eye diameter. Damaged and contracted specimens complicate length. The 495-kilogram, roughly 4.2-meter animal is a measured specimen associated with Te Papa; it does not prove a species maximum. Claims that colossal squid have the largest eyes should remain tied to measured or documented specimens and not become a universal exact record without a current source.
