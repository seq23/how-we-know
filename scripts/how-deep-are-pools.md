Pools whose floor is often not known

## Direct-answer lock

Deep-sea brine pools sit on the seafloor from several hundred meters down to more than three thousand meters below the surface. How thick the brine inside each pool is, from its surface to its floor, is a separate measurement. It varies widely from one pool to the next and has been made for far fewer pools than the first.

## Narration

### Cold open

{{text}}
On the seafloor, there are lakes with shorelines, ripples, and water that kills. Deep-sea brine pools sit on the seafloor from several hundred meters down to more than three thousand meters below the surface. NOAA Ocean Exploration has mapped and filmed pools like these in the Gulf of Mexico, and others lie on the floor of the Mediterranean and the Red Sea. That range tells you where the pools are. It does not tell you how much brine lies inside them. For most pools, that second number is only half measured: the surface is known and the floor is not.

### Title card

How deep are pools?

### "Deep" is doing two jobs, and only one of them is easy

{{contrast: deep | is=how far the pool sits below the sea surface | not=how thick the brine inside it is}}
When someone asks how deep these pools are, they usually mean one of two things. The first is how far the pool sits below the sea surface. That is a question about location, and it is answered the way any seafloor depth is answered. The second is how thick the brine inside it is, from its glassy upper surface down to the sediment at its floor. That is a question about the pool as an object, and it is much harder to answer. Most of the confusion about brine pools comes from blending these two measurements into one word. In this episode they stay apart. Wherever you hear a depth, notice which of the two it is.

{{define: brine pool | a body of dense, very salty water resting in a seafloor depression | it is water inside water, not a hole filled with air | NOAA}}
First, a brine pool needs defining. In plain terms, and consistent with how NOAA describes them, a brine pool is a body of dense, very salty water resting in a seafloor depression. The important word is dense. Ordinary seawater already carries dissolved salt, but brine carries much more. That extra salt makes it heavier than the seawater around it, so it does not mix in and drift away. It sinks and settles in low ground the way spilled syrup settles in the bottom of a glass of water. So a brine pool is water inside water, not a hole filled with air. It has no lid. The only thing holding it in place is the difference in density between two liquids.

### The salt comes from ancient seas that are now buried rock

{{chain: Where the brine comes from | buried salt layers | seawater dissolves the salt | dense brine seeps out | >the brine collects in low ground}}
The next question is where that much salt comes from. In the places where brine pools are best studied, the answer lies underneath the seafloor. Long ago, parts of these basins were shallow seas that evaporated and left thick deposits of salt behind. Over geological time, those deposits were buried under sediment. Today the buried salt layers sit beneath the seafloor. Seawater works its way down through cracks and pores, and seawater dissolves the salt. The result is heavier than anything around it. Dense brine seeps out at the seafloor, flows downhill as a thin, heavy current, and the brine collects in low ground. A pool is what you get when that low ground has an edge high enough to hold the brine in. The same chain can carry gas. In the Gulf of Mexico, many pools sit where methane also escapes from below, which is part of why their rims attract life.

{{ambient}}
That life stops sharply at the shoreline. Brine pools are generally very low in oxygen, and some carry dissolved gases that are toxic to most animals. Footage from the Gulf of Mexico shows beds of mussels crowding the rim of a pool. These mussels host bacteria that can use the chemicals seeping up from below. A short distance away, animals that stray into the brine itself can be overcome and die there. The pool is a boundary in the most literal sense. On one side there is a food supply. On the other there is water that most animals cannot survive. That boundary is also what lets researchers find the pool at all.

### A pool is found by its surface, not its floor

{{steps: Finding a pool | sonar maps the seafloor | a vehicle descends | sensors detect the salinity jump | >the surface is located long before the floor}}
Here is how a brine pool is usually located. Sonar maps the seafloor from a ship, building up depth from the time sound takes to travel down and back. That gives the shape of the ground and shows where hollows lie. Then a vehicle descends, usually a remotely operated vehicle carrying cameras and instruments. As it nears the pool, its sensors detect the salinity jump: the water reading changes abruptly from ordinary seawater to brine. On camera the same boundary looks like a shimmering, mirror-like surface. When the vehicle's thrusters disturb it, the surface can ripple and send slow waves rolling across it. Notice the order of events. The surface is located long before the floor. In many pools, the floor is rarely reached, if it is reached at all.

{{checklist: What a survey usually confirms | +the depth of the brine surface | +the outline of the shoreline | -the depth of the brine floor | ?how the pool changes over time}}
So when an expedition reports a brine pool, check what it actually confirms. It almost always confirms the depth of the brine surface, because the vehicle was right there and its instruments recorded it. It often confirms the outline of the shoreline, because cameras can follow the edge where life stops. It frequently does not confirm the depth of the brine floor, because reaching that floor means pushing an instrument down through dense, dark, chemically hostile fluid. Then there is a question most single visits cannot address at all: how the pool changes over time. Does the brine level rise and fall? Does the shoreline creep? Answering that takes repeat visits to the same spot. Repeat visits are rare in a place this hard to reach.

### Depth below the sea surface is the well-measured number

{{text}}
Start with what is known well. The depth of a pool below the sea surface rests on two independent methods that check each other. The first is ship sonar, the same mapping used by projects like GEBCO to chart the global seafloor. The second is the vehicle itself. An underwater vehicle does not carry a tape measure. It carries a pressure sensor. NOAA notes that water pressure rises by about one atmosphere for every ten meters of depth. So a measured pressure converts directly into a depth. At more than three thousand meters, the deepest of these pools sit under more than three hundred atmospheres of water. When the sonar depth and the pressure depth agree, you can trust where the pool's surface is.

{{contrast: depth sensor | is=a pressure reading converted to meters | not=a tape measure}}
That is why the range you heard at the start is solid even though it is wide. Several hundred meters down to more than three thousand is not one uncertain measurement. It is the spread of many different pools in many different basins, each measured fairly precisely. The width comes from geography, not doubt. A pool in the Gulf of Mexico and a basin in the eastern Mediterranean are simply in different places on very different seafloors. A depth sensor is a pressure reading converted to meters, and the conversion is well understood. The weak point is not the sensor. It is what the sensor is pointed at.

### The thickness of the brine is the hard number

{{chain: Measuring the brine itself | lower a probe through the surface | record salinity and temperature with depth | find where the sediment begins | >one thickness, at one spot}}
Now the hard part: the brine itself. The most direct way to measure its thickness is to lower a probe through the surface and record salinity and temperature with depth. You keep going until you find where the sediment begins. That sounds simple, and in principle it is. In practice the brine is often murky. It can damage equipment, and it resists anything trying to sink into it because it is so dense. Sonar is less help than you might expect. The sharp boundary at the brine surface reflects sound strongly, so the seafloor below can be harder to read through it. And even a clean measurement gives you one thickness, at one spot. A pool's floor need not be flat, so a single reading does not describe the whole pool.

{{define: halocline | a layer where salinity changes sharply with depth | it marks the brine surface, not the pool floor | Woods Hole}}
The edge that makes pools findable has a name: the halocline, a layer where salinity changes sharply with depth. Researchers at Woods Hole and elsewhere use the term for salt boundaries across the ocean. In a brine pool the halocline is unusually abrupt. That is why it can look like a surface you could stand on. It is also a source of easy mistakes. The halocline marks the brine surface, not the pool floor. A report that a vehicle "found the bottom of the pool" may mean it found the bottom of the ordinary seawater, which is the top of the brine. In some Mediterranean basins the brine is thick enough to fill a large depression. In some Gulf of Mexico pools it is shallow enough for vehicles to observe the floor. Published thicknesses vary widely, and no single figure describes them all.

### Pools in different seas are different kinds of place

{{checklist: What differs between basins | +depth below the surface | +how the salt reached the seafloor | -one shared thickness | ?how long each pool has existed}}
Compare basins and the variety becomes clearer. Pools differ in depth below the surface, as you have seen. They differ in how the salt reached the seafloor. Some sit over buried salt that dissolves slowly. In the Red Sea, the brine basins are linked to the spreading of the seafloor itself, and some of that brine is hot. What they do not share is one shared thickness. Each pool fills the hollow it happens to occupy, so its thickness depends on that hollow's shape. One question stays open for most of them: how long each pool has existed. A pool could be filling, draining or holding steady. Without measurements repeated over time, the rate is not known.

{{text}}
This is why the Smithsonian and other deep-sea educators tend to describe brine pools by what they do rather than by a single size. They concentrate salt. They exclude most animals. They hold a sharp boundary that life gathers around. Those properties are shared. Dimensions are not. Answering "how deep is a brine pool" with one number would be like answering "how deep is a lake" with one number. You could do it, but the number would describe one lake, not lakes.

### What to notice in the edit

{{contrast: the shimmering surface | is=the top of the brine | not=the bottom of the pool}}
When you watch footage of a brine pool, keep track of what the camera is actually showing. The shimmering surface is the top of the brine, not the bottom of the pool. The rippling you see is a boundary between two liquids, moved by the vehicle's own thrusters. When a depth appears on screen, it is almost always the depth of the vehicle, which means the depth of the surface it is hovering over. Shots that show a probe going in, or sediment beneath clear brine, are rarer and worth noticing. They are the moments when the second measurement, the hard one, is actually being made.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What this evidence does not establish | -the thickness of most pools | -whether any pool is growing or shrinking | -how many pools exist | +where the surveyed pools sit}}
Below the shimmering surface, the brine still keeps most of its answers. The evidence does not give the thickness of most pools. Thickness has been measured at particular spots in particular pools, and those spots may not represent the whole pool. It does not show whether any pool is growing or shrinking, because few have been revisited often enough. It does not tell us how many pools exist. Only a small fraction of the deep seafloor has been examined closely, so the pools we know are the pools in places someone looked. What it does establish, firmly, is where the surveyed pools sit. Their depth below the sea surface is measured twice over, by sonar and by pressure, and the two agree. Settling the rest would take repeated visits, probes that reach the floor in several places, and wider surveys of basins nobody has yet looked at closely.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So brine pools are deep in one sense and often uncertain in the other. Their surfaces lie from several hundred meters down to more than three thousand meters below the sea surface, and that much is well measured. Below each surface sits a layer of brine whose full depth, for most pools, is still being measured.
