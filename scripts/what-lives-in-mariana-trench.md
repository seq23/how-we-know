### The deepest floor belongs to crustaceans

{{zones: hadal}}
Start with the vocabulary, because the trench has its own. NOAA divides the ocean into zones by depth, and the deepest of them is the hadal zone, the ocean deeper than 6,000 metres. It is found almost entirely in trenches, which are long cracks where one tectonic plate dives beneath another. The Mariana Trench is the deepest of these. It lies in the western Pacific, and everything in this episode lives inside that hadal band or near its upper edge.

{{contrast: amphipods | is=scavenging crustaceans | not=shrimp}}
The animals you would meet first at the bottom are amphipods. They are scavenging crustaceans, not shrimp, though at first glance they look a little like pale, curled shrimp. In the trench they gather in swarms around anything edible that reaches the floor. Some hadal species grow far larger than the amphipods you might find under seaweed on a beach. One of them, Hirondellea gigas, has been collected from the Challenger Deep itself, the deepest known point of the trench. Amphipods are the reason we can say with confidence that animal life reaches the very bottom. They have been caught there repeatedly and brought to the surface.

{{stat: 1,000 | times | the pressure at the surface | NOAA}}
They live under more than 1,000 times the pressure at the surface, a figure NOAA uses to describe the trench floor. That number sounds as if it should crush anything soft. It does not, and the reason matters for the rest of this story. For an animal whose body is mostly water, pressure is not mainly a crushing force, because water barely compresses. The damage pressure does is subtler. It distorts proteins and cell membranes, the molecular machinery that has to fold and flex in order to work. Life at the bottom of the trench is a problem of chemistry, not of armour.

### Almost nobody has seen it; machines have waited

{{chain: How a lander sees the floor | bait | camera | trap | >what came to the bait}}
Here is how most of this is known. Very few people have ever been to the bottom of the Mariana Trench, and a crewed dive covers a tiny patch of seabed for a few hours. The workhorse is the lander, an unpowered frame dropped from a ship. It carries bait, a camera, and often a trap. It falls freely to the floor, sits there, and records what came to the bait. Then it releases its ballast weights and floats back up. The haul is hours of footage, a trap of animals, and sometimes a water sample. That is the raw evidence behind most of the species lists you will read.

{{checklist: What a lander can show | +what came to the bait | -what avoided the bait | ?how many live there}}
Be clear about what that method can and cannot show. A baited camera is excellent at revealing what came to the bait: scavengers with a good sense of smell that can swim or crawl toward it. It is poor at revealing what avoided the bait, or what was never interested in it, such as animals that filter food from the water or graze the sediment. A swarm around a carcass also tells you very little about how many live there across the whole floor. So the trench's best-known residents are partly a portrait of who answers a dinner bell.

### Fish live in the trench, but stop short of the floor

{{text}}
Fish are the clearest case of a limit. The Mariana Trench does have fish. The best known is the Mariana snailfish, a small, pale, almost translucent animal with a soft, gelatinous body. It has been filmed and collected in the trench's upper hadal reaches. Snailfish are the dominant fish at these depths in trenches around the Pacific. They are not the eel-like monsters of deep-sea illustration. They look more like a tadpole made of frosted glass, and on camera they drift toward bait with a slow, unhurried flutter.

{{chain: Why fish stop | TMAO | rises with depth | >a limit above the floor}}
No fish has been recorded anywhere near the trench's deepest floor, though, and there is a chemical explanation for why. Many deep-sea fish carry a molecule in their tissues called TMAO, which helps proteins hold their shape under pressure. Researchers who sampled fish from progressively deeper water found that its concentration rises with depth. That trend cannot continue forever. Past a certain point, the dissolved molecules inside the fish would outnumber those in the surrounding seawater, and water would flow into its tissues faster than it could cope. Extend the trend downward and it predicts a limit above the floor of the deepest trenches, a few kilometres short of the bottom. The deepest fish ever filmed sit close to that predicted line.

{{contrast: the fish limit | is=a prediction that fits the observations | not=a proven law}}
Notice the shape of that argument. It is a prediction that fits the observations, not a proven law. It rests on a trend measured in a limited number of species, extended beyond the depths where it was measured, and then checked against where fish have actually been seen. So far the check has held. Years of landers dropped into the deepest trenches have filmed amphipods at the bottom and no fish. A lack of sightings is weaker evidence than a sighting. Still, many drops that all come back the same way add up to something you can lean on.

### Some of the largest single cells on Earth live there

{{define: xenophyophore | a giant single-celled organism | one cell, not an animal | Scripps}}
Lower than the fish, there is life of a stranger kind. Xenophyophores are giant single-celled organisms, relatives of the shelled amoebae known as foraminifera. A single xenophyophore can grow to the size of your palm or larger, building a fragile, sponge-like case out of sediment grains and debris. It is one cell, not an animal. In 2011, an expedition led by researchers from Scripps, working with National Geographic, used free-falling cameras to film xenophyophores deep in the Mariana Trench, below the depths where they had previously been recorded.

{{ambient}}
Xenophyophores matter beyond their oddness. Their cases trap particles, and other small organisms live in and around them. On parts of the deep seafloor they add structure to an otherwise flat, soft landscape. Whether they play that same role at the deepest points of the trench is not well established. They have been seen there, but they have not been studied there the way a reef is studied.

### The trench lives on what falls into it

{{light}}
No sunlight reaches the trench. Light fades fast with depth, the reds first and the blues last, and long before the hadal zone there is nothing left for photosynthesis. Nothing at the bottom can grow its food from sunlight. Almost everything the trench eats arrives from above. Most of it is a slow drift of dead plankton, faecal pellets and organic debris that oceanographers call marine snow. Occasionally there is a larger windfall, such as a dead fish sinking from shallower water.

{{chain: Why the trench is fed | marine snow | trench walls | sediment slides | >food collects at the bottom}}
The trench's shape changes what reaches the floor. A trench is a long, steep-sided valley. Material that settles on the trench walls does not stay put, because sediment slides carry it downslope, and the axis of the trench gathers organic matter from a much wider area than the patch of sea directly above it. Researchers have measured how quickly microbes in Challenger Deep sediment use oxygen. They found more activity than on the shallower abyssal plain nearby. The simplest reading is that the trench works as a funnel, so food collects at the bottom.

{{text}}
That is why the microbes may be the trench's most important residents, even though they are the least visible. Bacteria and archaea live throughout the sediment, breaking down what falls, and everything else depends on their work. You will never see them on a lander's camera. They are known from sediment cores and water samples, which are read in the laboratory through their genes and their chemistry.

{{ambient}}
Not everything at the bottom chases bait. Sea cucumbers, soft-bodied relatives of starfish, plough slowly across the sediment, swallowing it and digesting the organic matter it holds. They are among the commonest large animals on soft deep-sea floors, and cameras have recorded them deep in the Mariana Trench. Because they feed on sediment rather than carcasses, they are exactly the kind of animal a baited trap tends to miss. That is a reminder that the trench's species list is shaped by the tools used to compile it.

### The first sighting is still argued over

{{timeline: 1960=Trieste | 2012=James Cameron}}
People have reached the floor too, and their reports are part of the record, with all the strengths and weaknesses of eyewitness evidence. In 1960, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh descended into the Challenger Deep in the bathyscaphe Trieste. Piccard later described seeing a flatfish on the bottom. In 2012, James Cameron made a solo dive to the Challenger Deep in a purpose-built submersible, in a project supported by National Geographic. He described a pale, featureless floor with small amphipods moving across it.

{{checklist: The flatfish report | +an eyewitness account | -a photograph | ?what was really seen}}
The flatfish is this episode's best lesson in weighing evidence. It was an eyewitness account from a trained observer, made under enormous strain, through a small window, in poor light. There is no photograph. It also conflicts with everything since: the chemical argument for a fish depth limit, and years of camera drops that have never shown a fish at that depth. Most deep-sea biologists now think Piccard saw something else, perhaps a sea cucumber, though we cannot know for certain what was really seen. The report is not dismissed because it is inconvenient. It is outweighed.

### What to notice in the edit

{{sources: Who reported it | NOAA=the depth NOAA gives for its deepest point | Scripps=researchers from Scripps}}
As you watch, notice which kind of evidence sits behind each claim. The depth of the floor is a survey measurement, and NOAA is the source for the figure in this episode. Amphipods at the bottom are physical specimens, caught and brought up. The xenophyophores were filmed by researchers from Scripps. The fish limit is an argument from chemistry, checked against footage. The flatfish is a single eyewitness report. These are different grades of evidence, and the edit should not let them blur into one confident list.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What we can say | +life reaches the bottom | -a full species list | ?how long they live}}
This evidence establishes that life reaches the bottom of the Mariana Trench, and it names some of what lives there. It does not provide a full species list. The trench is long, and only a small fraction of its floor has ever been sampled or filmed. Landers favour scavengers. Many specimens collected from hadal depths are still waiting to be formally described. Microbial diversity is known mainly through genetic surveys, which detect far more than has been named. The evidence also does not tell us how many animals live there, how long they live, or how stable these communities are over time. What we have is a sketch drawn through a handful of windows, not a census.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
At the bottom of the deepest trench, in total darkness and under more than 1,000 times the pressure at the surface, small crustaceans gather at a fallen meal. A single cell builds its fragile house from mud. Microbes work through what drifts down from the sunlit sea far above. The trench is not empty. It is simply very hard to see.
