## Direct-answer lock

A frilled shark is a rare deep-water shark with an eel-like body, six pairs of frilled gill slits, and flexible jaws. Calling it unchanged for 80 million years is misleading.

### The shark that breaks the familiar outline

{{stat: 6 | PAIRS | Six pairs of gill slits; the first joins beneath the throat}}
Most shark silhouettes are easy to recognize: a rigid torpedo body, a tall dorsal fin, and a mouth set under the snout. The frilled shark rearranges that picture. Its body is long and flexible. The mouth opens at the end of the head. Six pairs of gill slits sit behind it, and the first pair joins beneath the throat to form the frilled collar that gives the animal its name. Rows of narrow, three-cusped teeth angle inward. Smithsonian descriptions compare the animal to an eel because that is the fastest visual reference, not because it is closely related to eels. It remains a shark, with cartilaginous skeleton, shark skin, and a lineage within the hexanchiform sharks. Rare encounters usually happen when an animal is caught or recorded near continental slopes. That scarcity creates the first open loop: if the body looks so unlike a modern shark, why do people keep calling it a survivor from prehistory?

### Why living fossil is a dangerous shortcut

{{define: Living fossil | Used for organisms whose visible anatomy resembles forms known from older branches of the fossil record | It does not mean a living species stopped evolving}}
The phrase living fossil is memorable and scientifically slippery. It is often used for organisms whose visible anatomy resembles forms known from older branches of the fossil record. The phrase does not mean a living species stopped evolving.

{{checklist: WHAT CONTINUES REGARDLESS | +DNA changes | +Populations split | +Environments shift | +Selection continues}}
DNA changes, populations split, environments shift, and selection continues. A frilled shark alive now is not an eighty-million-year-old individual, and its genome is not a museum cast.

The more careful statement is that frilled sharks preserve a combination of traits considered primitive within living sharks, including six gill pairs and an elongated body plan. That is interesting without pretending time froze. The popular title also collapses multiple species and uncertain fossil comparisons into one claim.

### Anatomy suggests a feeding strategy, but cameras are scarce

{{anatomy: TEETH THAT HOLD SLIPPERY PREY | wide jaws@0.12,0.52 | three-cusped teeth@0.17,0.58 | frilled collar@0.30,0.48}}
The frilled shark's jaws can open wide, and the teeth form repeating white traps that point inward. Squid, fishes, and other sharks have been reported from stomach analyses, supporting the idea that the animal seizes slippery prey. Yet direct wild feeding observations remain rare. Some accounts describe a snake-like strike, but anatomy alone cannot prove the exact motion or speed. Deep-water capture can also distort behavior. An exhausted shark at the surface is not a clean demonstration of how it moves at depth. This distinction matters because every unusual feature attracts a cinematic explanation. Flexible jaws may allow large prey. A long body may permit a different approach angle. Those are testable ideas, not license to invent a filmed ambush. The correct documentary move is to show what the teeth look like, explain what prey-retention teeth generally do, and label the unobserved part as inference. The unresolved loop is why an animal with such a broad popular reputation remains so poorly documented in its normal habitat.

### What a fossil resemblance can and cannot prove

{{contrast: THE SAFER CLAIM | is=Its lineage has ancient roots | is=Its body retains a combination of traits viewers find unfamiliar | not=That the exact modern species has remained unchanged for a stated number of millions of years}}
A modern animal can retain features that resemble ancient relatives while still evolving continuously. Genes change. Populations adapt. Environments shift. The phrase 'living fossil' compresses that history into a misleading image of an organism frozen in time. For the frilled shark, the safer claim is that its lineage has ancient roots and its body retains a combination of traits viewers find unfamiliar—not that the exact modern species has remained unchanged for a stated number of millions of years.
