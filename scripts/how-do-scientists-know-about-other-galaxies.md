## Direct-answer lock

Scientists know about other galaxies through light that has traveled across space for millions or billions of years. Telescopes collect that light, and spectroscopy reveals what elements are present, how fast the galaxy is moving, and how far away it is. The evidence is electromagnetic radiation analyzed by instruments, not guesswork.

### Light carries information across space that instruments can decode

{{contrast: light | is=electromagnetic radiation with measurable properties | not=a carrier of subjective impressions}}
When you look at a galaxy, you are not seeing the galaxy as it is now. You are seeing light that left that galaxy millions or billions of years ago and has been traveling through space ever since. Light is electromagnetic radiation, and it carries information in its wavelength, intensity, and spectrum. A telescope collects that light. A spectrograph splits it into its component wavelengths, producing a spectrum. That spectrum contains absorption and emission lines corresponding to specific elements. Hydrogen produces a characteristic pattern. So does helium, oxygen, and iron. The pattern is a fingerprint. If you see hydrogen's lines in a galaxy's spectrum, hydrogen is present in that galaxy. The method is called spectroscopy, and it has been used since the mid-1800s.

{{chain: SPECTROSCOPY | light collected | split into wavelengths | lines matched to elements | >composition determined}}
The reason this works is that every element absorbs and emits light at specific wavelengths, determined by the energy levels of its electrons. Those wavelengths do not change. Hydrogen in a laboratory on Earth produces the same spectral lines as hydrogen in a galaxy 10 million light-years away. The lines may be shifted in wavelength due to the galaxy's motion, but the pattern remains recognizable. This is not interpretation. It is measurement. The spectrum is a physical record, and the lines either match known elements or they do not.

### Distance is measured using objects with known intrinsic brightness

{{ladder: Cepheid variable=30000 | Type Ia supernova=10000000000 | Earth=12742}}
Knowing a galaxy exists is not the same as knowing how far away it is. Distance measurements rely on standard candles, objects whose intrinsic brightness is known or can be calculated. Cepheid variables are one type. These are stars whose brightness oscillates over a period of days or weeks. In 1908, Henrietta Swan Leavitt, working at Harvard College Observatory, discovered that the period of a Cepheid's pulsation is directly related to its luminosity. A Cepheid that pulses every 10 days is intrinsically brighter than one that pulses every 3 days. If you measure the period, you know the luminosity. If you know the luminosity and measure the apparent brightness from Earth, you can calculate the distance.

{{stat: 2.5 | million light-years | Andromeda Galaxy distance via Cepheids | NASA}}
This is how Edwin Hubble, using the 100-inch Hooker Telescope at Mount Wilson Observatory, determined in 1923 that Andromeda was far outside our own Milky Way. He identified Cepheids in Andromeda, measured their periods, calculated their luminosities, and derived a distance of approximately 900,000 light-years. That figure was later revised upward as the Cepheid calibration improved. The current accepted distance is approximately 2.5 million light-years. The method has not changed. The calibration has become more precise.

{{text}}
Cepheids are visible only out to a certain distance, roughly 10 million to 100 million light-years depending on the telescope. Beyond that, they are too faint. For greater distances, astronomers use Type Ia supernovae. These are thermonuclear explosions of white dwarf stars that reach a consistent peak brightness. The mechanism is well understood. A white dwarf in a binary system accretes matter from a companion star until it reaches approximately 1.4 solar masses, the Chandrasekhar limit. At that mass, carbon fusion ignites throughout the star nearly simultaneously, and the star explodes. Because the mass at detonation is always near the same limit, the peak brightness is always near the same value. Type Ia supernovae have been detected in galaxies billions of light-years away.

### Redshift reveals how fast a galaxy is moving away from Earth

{{define: redshift | wavelength stretched by recession | measured in spectrum | Doppler effect and expansion}}
When a galaxy is moving away from Earth, the wavelengths of light it emits are stretched. This is called redshift. The faster the recession, the greater the stretch. Redshift is measured by comparing the observed wavelength of a spectral line to the wavelength that line has in a laboratory. If hydrogen's red line, normally at 656.3 nanometers, appears at 660 nanometers in a galaxy's spectrum, that galaxy is receding. The amount of shift tells you the velocity. This is the Doppler effect for light, analogous to the change in pitch of a siren as it moves away from you.

{{timeline: 1929=Hubble publishes velocity-distance relation | 1998=accelerating expansion discovered | 2003=WMAP measures cosmological parameters}}
In 1929, Edwin Hubble published a paper showing that more distant galaxies have greater redshifts. The relationship is linear: velocity increases with distance. This is evidence that the universe is expanding. The slope of that relationship is called the Hubble constant, and its value has been refined over decades. The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, has measured Cepheids in galaxies up to 60 million light-years away, improving the calibration. The Planck satellite, which observed the cosmic microwave background from 2009 to 2013, provided an independent measurement of the expansion rate. The two methods give slightly different values, a tension that remains unresolved, but both confirm that distant galaxies are receding and that the universe is expanding.

### Galaxies are not just points of light but resolved structures

{{anatomy: SPIRAL GALAXY | nucleus@0.5,0.5 | spiral arms@0.3,0.7 | dust lanes@0.6,0.3}}
Early telescopes showed galaxies as fuzzy patches. William Herschel, in the late 1700s, cataloged thousands of nebulae, but he could not determine whether they were clouds within the Milky Way or separate systems. The resolution was insufficient. By the early 1900s, larger telescopes revealed structure. Spiral arms, central bulges, and dust lanes became visible. The 100-inch Hooker Telescope, completed in 1917, was the instrument that allowed Hubble to resolve individual stars in Andromeda. Once individual stars were visible, Cepheids could be identified, and distance could be measured.

{{sources: RESOLVED STRUCTURES | Hubble Space Telescope=images of distant galaxies | Sloan Digital Sky Survey=spectroscopic data for millions of galaxies}}
Modern telescopes resolve galaxies in extraordinary detail. The Hubble Space Telescope has imaged galaxies billions of light-years away, showing their shapes, star-forming regions, and interactions with neighboring galaxies. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey, which began in 2000, has obtained spectra for millions of galaxies, mapping their distances and velocities. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, observes in infrared, penetrating dust and detecting galaxies formed within a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. These are not artist's impressions. They are photographs, built from photons collected over hours or days of exposure.

### The cosmic distance ladder is a chain of overlapping methods

{{steps: DISTANCE LADDER | parallax for nearby stars | Cepheids for nearby galaxies | Type Ia supernovae for distant galaxies | >each step calibrates the next}}
The cosmic distance ladder is a sequence of techniques, each building on the one before. Parallax, the apparent shift in a star's position as Earth orbits the Sun, works for stars within a few hundred light-years. Cepheids extend the ladder to tens of millions of light-years. Type Ia supernovae reach billions of light-years. Each method overlaps with the next, allowing calibration. If Cepheids and supernovae are both visible in the same galaxy, the supernova's brightness can be calibrated against the Cepheid distance. The ladder is not a single measurement but a series of cross-checks.

{{text}}
The Gaia satellite, launched by the European Space Agency in 2013, has measured parallaxes for more than a billion stars with unprecedented precision. Those measurements anchor the bottom of the ladder. Cepheids, calibrated against parallax distances, anchor the middle. Supernovae, calibrated against Cepheids, anchor the top. The entire structure depends on the consistency of physical laws across space and time. If the speed of light varied, or if atomic spectra changed, the ladder would collapse. No evidence suggests either has occurred.

### What to notice in the edit

{{checklist: EVIDENCE TYPES | +spectroscopy identifies elements | +standard candles measure distance | +redshift measures velocity | +resolved images show structure}}
The evidence for other galaxies is not a single observation but a convergence of independent methods. Spectroscopy identifies composition. Standard candles measure distance. Redshift measures velocity. Imaging resolves structure. Each method has been tested, refined, and cross-checked against others. The measurements are public. The Hubble Space Telescope's archive contains more than 100 terabytes of data, accessible to anyone. The Sloan Digital Sky Survey's data releases are freely available. The evidence is not locked behind closed doors. It is published, peer-reviewed, and reproducible.

### Evidence limit

{{ambient}}
What this evidence does not establish is the ultimate fate of the universe, or whether other universes exist beyond the observable horizon. The observable universe is limited by the speed of light and the finite age of the cosmos. Light from objects beyond a certain distance, accounting for expansion, has not had time to reach Earth. Anything beyond that distance is not observable, and claims about it are not based on direct evidence. The methods described here apply only to the observable universe. They say nothing about what lies beyond.

### Closing

{{text}}
Other galaxies are known through light that has traveled across space, carrying information that instruments decode. The distances are measured, the compositions are identified, and the motions are tracked. The evidence is electromagnetic radiation, analyzed and cross-checked. That is how scientists know.
