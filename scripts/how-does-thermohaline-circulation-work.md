## Direct-answer lock

Thermohaline circulation is a slow, planet-wide overturning of the ocean driven by differences in seawater density, which depends on temperature and salt. Cold, salty water sinks in a few polar regions, spreads through the deep basins, and is eventually lifted back to the surface, largely by winds and mixing rather than by heat and salt alone. NOAA estimates the full circuit takes about 1,000 years.

### Cold open

{{stat: 1,000 | years | one lap of the global conveyor belt | NOAA}}
Water sinking in the North Atlantic may not see sunlight for about 1,000 years. That is NOAA's estimate for how long a single parcel of seawater takes to finish one lap of the deep current often called the global conveyor belt. Thermohaline circulation is a slow, planet-wide overturning of the ocean driven by differences in seawater density, which depends on temperature and salt. That part is textbook. What the textbook picture tends to hide is the harder half of the problem. Making water sink is easy to explain. Bringing all that cold, heavy water back up to the surface is not, and the answer turned out to involve forces the word thermohaline leaves out.

### Title card

How does thermohaline circulation work?

### Density, not temperature alone, decides what sinks

{{define: thermohaline | driven by heat and salt | not by wind | NOAA}}
The word itself is the first clue. Thermo means heat and haline means salt, so thermohaline circulation is circulation driven by heat and salt, not by wind. Both change how dense seawater is. Colder water is denser than warmer water. Saltier water is denser than fresher water. Where water at the surface becomes denser than the water beneath it, it sinks, and it keeps sinking until it meets water of matching density. That is the whole engine in a sentence, and it is worth noticing how it is known. Density cannot be seen from a ship. It is calculated. Oceanographers lower instruments that measure conductivity, temperature and depth, and from conductivity they derive salinity. Feed temperature, salinity and pressure into the equation of state for seawater, and density comes out. Map that density across an ocean basin, and the layers appear: light water on top, heavy water stacked below, sloping surfaces between them. The circulation was inferred from that layering long before anyone measured a deep current directly.

### Deep water forms in only a few places

{{contrast: deep water formation | is=a few polar regions | not=everywhere at once}}
If cold, salty water sinks, you might expect it to sink everywhere at once near the poles. It does not. Deep water forms in a few polar regions, and the reasons are specific. In the far North Atlantic, warm salty water carried north by surface currents gives up its heat to cold winter air. It keeps its salt, so it becomes both cold and salty, and heavy enough to sink. That water spreads south at depth as what oceanographers call North Atlantic Deep Water. Around Antarctica, a second process does the work. When seawater freezes, the ice that forms is mostly fresh. Much of the salt is pushed out into the water left behind, a process called brine rejection. That leftover water is extremely cold and extra salty, and it becomes Antarctic Bottom Water, the densest water in the open ocean, which hugs the seafloor as it spreads north. Between them, these two sources fill most of the deep ocean. The North Pacific, by contrast, is too fresh at the surface to form deep water of its own.

{{chain: Watching water sink | ships measure winter columns | floats record deep mixing | >dense water spreads at depth}}
The evidence for where sinking happens comes from looking in winter, exactly when ships least want to be there. Research ships measure winter columns of water in the Labrador Sea and the seas around Greenland, and they have found the surface layer stirred down deep, with temperature and salinity nearly uniform from top to bottom. Floats record deep mixing in the same places. Then the water can be followed. A tongue of slightly saltier, oxygen-rich water extends south through the deep Atlantic, visible in density sections taken across the basin decade after decade. The dense water spreads at depth, and its fingerprint travels with it.

### Sinking is the easy half; rising is the puzzle

{{text}}
Here is the problem that took oceanographers decades to state clearly. If water only ever sank, the deep ocean would slowly fill with the coldest, densest water available and then stop moving. It does not stop. So something must be lifting deep water back toward the surface, against its own weight, and that takes energy. Heat and salt alone cannot supply it. The oceanographer Walter Munk worked through the arithmetic and argued that slow turbulent mixing, stirred by tides and by internal waves breaking deep inside the ocean, could gradually blend cold deep water with warmer water above and let it rise. It was a careful argument, and it set up a test. When researchers later released a harmless tracer into the open ocean interior and watched it spread, the mixing they measured was weaker than his calculation required. Mixing turned out to be patchy: weak in the quiet interior, much stronger over rough seafloor such as mid-ocean ridges and seamounts.

{{steps: The return path | sinks near the poles | spreads through the deep basins | rises in the Southern Ocean | >winds and mixing supply the lift}}
The other part of the answer lies around Antarctica. Strong westerly winds circle the continent with no land to stop them, and they push surface water away to the north. Water from below rises to replace it, climbing along density layers that slope upward toward the surface in the Southern Ocean. Much of the deep water that sank in the North Atlantic is thought to return this way. So the full route runs like this: water sinks near the poles, spreads through the deep basins, rises in the Southern Ocean and elsewhere, and is warmed and freshened again at the surface. Winds and mixing supply the lift. That is why many oceanographers now prefer the name meridional overturning circulation. It describes what the water does without claiming that heat and salt alone are doing it.

### Chemistry inside the water records its age

{{chain: Dating deep water | surface water takes up carbon-14 | no new carbon-14 after sinking | less carbon-14 means older water | >the oldest water lies in the deep North Pacific}}
Much of what we know about the deep route was read from chemistry rather than measured with current meters. Surface water takes up carbon-14 from the atmosphere, the same radioactive form of carbon used to date wood and bone. Once that water sinks, it is cut off. There is no new carbon-14 after sinking, and what the water carries decays at a steady, known rate. So less carbon-14 means older water. When survey ships sampled the world's oceans basin by basin, the pattern lined up with the conveyor picture: younger deep water in the North Atlantic, progressively older water along the route, and the oldest water lies in the deep North Pacific, at the far end of the path. Other tracers tell the short end of the story. Chlorofluorocarbons, industrial gases that only entered the atmosphere in the twentieth century, turn up in deep North Atlantic water, which shows that water was at the surface recently. Dissolved oxygen falls steadily along the route too, used up by living things with no way to replenish it.

### Direct measurement came late, and it is still thin

{{sources: Measuring the flow | Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution=deep moorings | Scripps=Argo floats | NOAA=global conveyor belt}}
For most of the twentieth century, the deep circulation was a deduction. Measuring it directly is hard, because the flow is slow and the ocean is enormous. Two kinds of instrument changed that. The first is the deep mooring: a cable anchored to the seafloor, strung with current meters and sensors that record for months or years before a ship returns to collect them. Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution is among the groups that have run deep moorings in the Atlantic. The second is the Argo program, a global fleet of robotic Argo floats, with Scripps playing a leading role, which drift below the surface, sink and rise to measure temperature and salinity, and report by satellite. Lines of moorings strung across the Atlantic now give continuous estimates of how much water is overturning. NOAA's public explainer of the global conveyor belt summarises what this kind of evidence shows.

{{checklist: What direct measurement shows | +the overturning is real | +it varies from year to year | -a settled long-term trend | ?how the deepest layers change}}
Those records confirmed some things and complicated others. The overturning is real, and it can be measured. But it varies from year to year, and even from month to month, by more than anyone expected from the smooth conveyor diagram. That variability is the reason a settled long-term trend has been hard to pin down. A record that swings widely needs to run for a long time before a slow change can be separated from noise. And because most floats sample only the upper ocean, how the deepest layers change remains thinly observed. Deep-capable floats are being added, but the abyss is still the least sampled part of the system.

### A crude model showed the circulation might switch states

{{define: two stable states | strong overturning or weak overturning under the same conditions | a model result, not a forecast | Stommel}}
One of the most important ideas about this system came from a deliberately crude model. Henry Stommel represented the ocean as two connected boxes of water, one warm and one cold, and let heat and salt compete to drive flow between them. Temperature differences pushed the circulation one way. Salt differences could push it the other. The striking result was that the model could settle into two stable states: strong overturning or weak overturning under the same conditions. Which one it chose depended on its history. That is a model result, not a forecast. But it suggested that adding enough fresh water at high latitudes, from rain, rivers or melting ice, could make surface water too light to sink and push the system toward its weaker state.

{{text}}
Evidence from the past suggests this is more than a thought experiment. Ocean sediment cores, layered mud drilled from the seafloor, preserve chemical signals of past deep water, and some of those records line up with abrupt climate shifts seen in Greenland ice. Many researchers interpret those episodes as times when the Atlantic overturning weakened sharply. Climate models generally project weakening this century as the high latitudes warm and freshen. How much, and whether any threshold lies within reach, remains actively debated, and the direct measurements are still too short to settle it.

### What to notice in the edit

{{contrast: the animation | is=a speeded-up summary | not=a real-time view}}
When you see this circulation animated, notice the speed. A glowing ribbon looping the globe in a few seconds is a speeded-up summary, not a real-time view. Real deep flow is slow and broad, more like a whole basin creeping than a river in a channel. Notice too that the ribbon is drawn as one continuous line. In the water, the route branches, recirculates in eddies and mixes along the way, so no single parcel follows the path you see. And watch where the arrows rise. If deep water rises with no wind or mixing shown to lift it, the drawing is simpler than the evidence.

### Evidence limit

{{text}}
Here is what all of this does not establish. The figure of about 1,000 years is an estimate for a typical circuit, not a clock carried by any real parcel of water; some water returns far sooner, and some lingers far longer. Radiocarbon gives an age, not a route, so the path between the North Atlantic and the North Pacific is partly inferred. The relative share of lift supplied by Southern Ocean winds versus deep mixing is still argued over. And the instrument record of the overturning is only a couple of decades long, too short to say with confidence how it is changing.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
The ocean's deepest water is moving now, slowly, on a schedule longer than any record we hold. We know its shape from chemistry, from moorings and floats, and from the honest gap between what the water should do and what it was measured doing. That gap is where the next lap of understanding begins.
