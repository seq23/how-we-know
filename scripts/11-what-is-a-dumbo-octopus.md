## Direct-answer lock

A dumbo octopus is a finned deep-sea octopus in the cirrate group. It swims mainly with ear-like fins and webbed arms, and confirmed observations extend to 6,957 meters.

### A name that hides a whole group

{{define: Dumbo octopus | A nickname for finned octopuses in the cirrate lineage | Not one tidy species with one fixed depth and one fixed appearance}}
Dumbo octopus is not one tidy species with one fixed depth and one fixed appearance. It is a nickname used for finned octopuses in the cirrate lineage. Their most obvious feature is the pair of fins on the mantle, positioned where a cartoon elephant might have ears. The resemblance gave them a friendly name, but the anatomy solves a serious problem.

{{chain: WHAT THE FINS SOLVE | Rapid movement is expensive in deep water | Prey may be scarce | Cirrates pulse the fins and reshape the web between the arms | >Small changes in posture, without a powerful jet every few seconds}}
In deep water, rapid movement is expensive and prey may be scarce. Cirrates can pulse the fins, reshape the web between the arms, and make small changes in posture without firing a powerful jet every few seconds. NOAA footage has shown these animals hovering above canyon walls and seafloor terrain thousands of meters down.

{{sources: WHY A LIVE OBSERVATION MATTERS MORE | A live observation=the body looks delicate because water supports it | A damaged specimen photograph=on a deck, soft tissue can collapse and distort}}
The body looks delicate because water supports it. On a deck, soft tissue can collapse and distort, which is why a live observation matters more than a damaged specimen photograph. The first open loop is simple: fins explain how it moves, but they do not explain how far down the design can work.

### The observation at 6,957 meters

{{magnitude: CONFIRMED IN-SITU OBSERVATIONS | metres | First 2020 observation=5,760 | Deepest 2020 observation=6,957}}
In 2020, Alan Jamieson and Michael Vecchione reported clear lander video of a cirrate octopus identified as Grimpoteuthis at 5,760 meters and again at 6,957 meters in the Indian Ocean. Before that work, the deepest unambiguous photographic evidence for a cephalopod was much shallower. The new observations extended confirmed in-situ cephalopod depth by more than 1,800 meters.

{{contrast: WHAT THE NUMBER PROVES | is=That a finned octopus can occupy hadal habitat | is=That it remains coordinated under conditions once treated as marginal | not=That all dumbo octopuses routinely live at 6,957 meters | not=A global depth distribution}}
That number needs discipline. It does not mean all dumbo octopuses routinely live at 6,957 meters. It does not turn one sighting into a global depth distribution. It proves that a finned octopus can occupy hadal habitat and remain coordinated under conditions once treated as marginal for cephalopods. The lander provided depth, time, and video context without hauling the animal through thousands of meters of changing pressure and temperature. The strange part is not that the octopus survived a descent. It was already home. The next unresolved question is what its soft body is doing at a molecular level to remain functional there.

### A body built around water, not air

{{pressure: 6957}}
Pressure is most dramatic when it compresses gas. A dumbo octopus does not carry lungs, and its tissues are mostly water, so the body is not a hollow object waiting to be crushed. Pressure still affects proteins, membranes, and biochemical reactions, but deep species evolve around those constraints. Cirrate octopuses also tend to abandon some familiar shallow-water defenses. An ink cloud has limited value in permanent darkness, and repeated jet propulsion can be costly. Their fins provide efficient motion; the arm web can form a bell-like surface; small sensory structures called cirri may help with feeding. Scientists can describe those structures, yet behavior is harder.

{{contrast: WHAT A FEW MINUTES OF VIDEO SHOWS | is=Hovering | is=Landing | is=Arm movements | not=An entire feeding sequence}}
A few minutes of video may show hovering, landing, or arm movements, but it rarely reveals an entire feeding sequence. This is where documentary storytelling often overreaches. A graceful clip invites a complete narrative, while the evidence may support only movement, depth, and morphology. The missing piece is not a monster fact. It is the daily ecology of an animal that is almost never watched for long.

### What the camera still cannot tell us

{{checklist: HOW SPARSE THE RECORD REMAINS | -No continuous survey of every trench | ?Different cirrate species occupy different depths | ?A single baited lander may attract or miss animals | -Species-specific timing and brooding behavior | -Even the word dumbo blurs distinctions between genera}}
The hadal sighting opened a larger habitat than researchers could confidently assign to cephalopods, but it also exposed how sparse the record remains. We do not have a continuous survey of every trench. Different cirrate species occupy different depths. A single baited lander may attract or miss animals in ways that change what the camera sees. Reproduction is another gap. Deep octopuses generally produce relatively few, large eggs, but species-specific timing and brooding behavior can be difficult to observe. Even the word dumbo can blur important distinctions between genera.

{{checklist: THREE DIFFERENT CLAIMS | ?Observed | ?Captured | ?Inferred}}
The responsible conclusion is narrow and still remarkable: finned octopuses are not confined to the upper deep sea, and at least one has been documented at 6,957 meters. When you see a viral caption calling it the deepest octopus ever, check whether it says observed, captured, or inferred. Those are different claims. The next episode turns from a soft-bodied cephalopod to a shark whose body looks like an eel, and whose reputation is older than the evidence.

### The record changes the map

{{ladder: Previous photographic record=5145 | First 2020 observation=5760 | Deepest 2020 observation=6957}}
Before the 2020 paper, the deepest unambiguous photographic evidence for a cephalopod was 5,145 meters. Jamieson and Vecchione reported HD lander video of Grimpoteuthis at 5,760 and 6,957 meters in the Indian Ocean.

{{magnitude: POTENTIAL BENTHIC HABITAT AVAILABLE TO CEPHALOPODS | percent of the global seafloor | Estimated before=75 | Estimated after=99}}
That extended the confirmed in-situ depth by 1,812 meters and increased the potential benthic habitat available to cephalopods from an estimated 75 to 99 percent of the global seafloor. The key word is potential. One observation does not prove that every finned octopus occupies every trench.

### What the record changes

{{stat: 99 | PERCENT | Potential benthic habitat available to cephalopods, up from an estimated 75 percent}}
The 6,957-meter observation mattered because it expanded the documented depth of octopus life by more than a kilometer. It also changed the estimated share of seafloor habitat potentially available to octopuses.
