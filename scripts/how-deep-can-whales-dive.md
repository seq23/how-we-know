## YouTube title

Whales can dive 2,992 m deep, a beaked whale's record — and how we know

## Direct-answer lock

The deepest dive ever recorded by a whale reached 2,992 metres, made by a Cuvier's beaked whale wearing a tag off southern California, in a study published in 2014. Guinness World Records lists it as the deepest dive by any mammal. It is a measured minimum, not a ceiling: it is the deepest dive a tag happened to catch, not the deepest any whale can go.

### Cold open

Nobody saw it happen: roughly three hundred times surface pressure, in total darkness. The deepest dive ever recorded by a whale reached 2,992 metres, made by a Cuvier's beaked whale wearing a tag off southern California, in a study published in 2014. Guinness World Records lists it as the deepest dive by any mammal. The only witness was a pressure sensor, which logged depth and relayed summaries by satellite. That settles one thing: a whale got there and came back. It does not settle how deep a whale can go. A record is a floor, not a ceiling.

### The record is a sensor reading, not a sighting

{{chain: How the record was made | tag attached at the surface | pressure sensor logs depth | data relayed by satellite | >2,992 metres}}
Nobody watched that whale reach the bottom of its dive. Nobody could. At that depth there is no sunlight, and Cuvier's beaked whales are hard enough to find at the surface, where they spend only short intervals between long dives. What researchers did instead was attach an electronic tag to the animal while it breathed at the surface, and then let the tag do the watching. The tag carried a pressure sensor. It logged pressure at regular intervals and, over the following weeks, relayed summarised records through satellites to researchers on shore. The 2,992 metres is what that sensor reported at the deepest point of one dive.

{{define: pressure sensor | a gauge that converts water pressure into depth | it reports depth, not what the whale was doing there | NOAA Ocean Exploration}}
Pressure is a very good proxy for depth, because seawater is heavy and its weight adds up predictably. As NOAA Ocean Exploration explains, pressure increases by roughly one atmosphere for every ten metres you descend. So a tag that knows the pressure knows the depth, within the accuracy of its sensor. At 2,992 metres, that arithmetic gives roughly three hundred times the pressure at the surface. What the sensor cannot tell you is why the whale went there, what it found, or whether it could have gone further.

{{text}}
It also helps to know that the record was not a lucky single reading. The study tagged several whales and followed them for weeks, logging a great many deep dives. Most of those dives were shallower than the record. That matters, because it tells you how to read the headline number. It is the extreme edge of a pattern, not the typical dive. When you see a single figure attached to a species, ask whether it is the usual behaviour or the most extreme thing ever caught on an instrument. Here, it is clearly the second.

### Before tags, depth was inferred from clues

{{steps: Older lines of evidence | entangled cables | stomach contents | timed disappearances | >depth inferred, not measured}}
For most of the history of whale science, nobody could follow a diving whale. Depth had to be inferred. One line of evidence came from the seafloor itself. In the era of submarine telegraph cables, repair crews occasionally hauled up dead sperm whales tangled in cable that had been laid in deep water. Wherever the cable lay, the whale had evidently been. Another clue came from stomach contents. Sperm whales taken by whalers were found to contain the hard beaks of squid, including kinds that live far below the sunlit surface. If the prey lives deep, the predator must reach it.

{{text}}
A third method was simply patience. Observers on ships timed how long whales stayed down between breaths. Long absences suggested deep dives, but a stopwatch measures time, not depth. A whale could spend an hour travelling sideways. Each of these clues was real, and each pointed downward, but none of them could put a single trustworthy number on a dive. They established that some whales go deep. They could not say how deep.

{{chain: From clue to measurement | stopwatch timing | stomach contents | electronic tag | >a depth reading}}
That changed when electronics became small and robust enough to ride on a whale. Early recorders had to be recovered to read their memory, which meant finding a small floating tag somewhere at sea. Later designs could compress their records and transmit them by satellite whenever the whale surfaced, so the data reached researchers even if the tag itself was never seen again.

### A deep diver carries its oxygen in its muscles

{{anatomy: Cuvier's beaked whale | muscle rich in myoglobin@0.5,0.5 | compressible lungs@0.35,0.4 | slowed heart@0.3,0.5}}
The obvious question is how a breathing animal survives a dive like that. The answer is not a giant lungful of air. Deep-diving whales store most of their oxygen in their blood and, above all, in their muscles, which are packed with a protein called myoglobin. Myoglobin binds oxygen and holds it where it will be used. In some diving mammals it is so concentrated that the muscle looks very dark. On the way down, the whale's heart slows, and blood flow is redirected toward the organs that cannot tolerate running short, such as the brain and the heart itself, while other tissues make do.

{{contrast: diving whale | is=oxygen stored in blood and muscle | not=a giant lungful of air}}
The lungs do something that sounds alarming. As pressure rises, they compress, and the air in them is squeezed into stiffer airways where little gas exchange takes place. That is protective. Less nitrogen dissolves into the blood under pressure, which lowers the risk of the gas coming out of solution on the way up, the problem human divers know as decompression sickness. Much of this physiology was worked out in other diving mammals, such as seals, and is inferred for beaked whales, which are far too elusive to study in a laboratory.

### Depth and duration are different records

{{text}}
The 2014 study reported more than depth. That same record dive lasted more than two hours, which was also reported as a record for a mammal at the time. Depth and duration are related but not identical. A whale's oxygen budget limits how long it can stay down, and depth is part of that cost, because the longer the descent and the ascent, the less time remains for hunting at the bottom.

{{steps: Shape of a foraging dive | descent | hunting at depth | ascent | >recovery at the surface}}
Tag records show the typical shape. Cuvier's beaked whales descend steeply, spend time hunting at depth, and then rise, often more slowly than they went down. Other studies, using tags that record sound, have picked up the whales' echolocation clicks during the deep part of the dive, which is how researchers infer that this is where the searching for prey happens. Between deep dives, the whales make a series of shallower ones and spend time near the surface. Later tagging studies have recorded even longer dives, which tells you the 2014 duration was not an absolute limit either.

### Other whales dive deep, but their numbers are softer

{{checklist: How solid is the evidence | +Cuvier's beaked whale: a tag-measured record | ?sperm whale maximum: estimates vary | -baleen whales as record divers}}
Cuvier's beaked whale is not the only deep diver. Sperm whales, the largest toothed whales, hunt squid in deep water and have long been the textbook example of a deep diver. NOAA's species profiles describe both sperm whales and beaked whales as deep-diving hunters. But published maximum depths for sperm whales vary, and the range in the literature is wide. Some figures come from tags, some from older acoustic tracking, and some from inference. That variation is a property of the evidence, not of the whales.

{{ambient}}
At the other end, baleen whales such as blue and humpback whales mostly feed much shallower, filtering swarms of small animals that gather in the upper layers of the water. Their dives are impressive as feats of size, but they are not contenders for the depth record. Size, in other words, is not depth. The deepest known diver is a medium-sized whale most people have never heard of.

### The deepest known dive is a floor, not a ceiling

{{chain: Why the record understates the limit | few animals tagged | tags fall off within weeks | sensors have a rated maximum depth | >the true limit is unknown}}
Here is the most important thing to understand about any record like this. It is a lower bound. Only a small number of Cuvier's beaked whales have ever been tagged, and each tag stays attached for a limited time, usually days to weeks, before it falls off or stops transmitting. Each sensor also has a rated maximum depth it can measure. A whale that dives beyond what has been sampled leaves no trace in the data.

{{text}}
The ocean also sets a local limit. A whale cannot dive deeper than the seafloor beneath it, so a tagged animal living over moderately deep water may never show what it could do over a deeper basin. And there may be a physiological cost that rises steeply with depth, so that a whale able to go deeper rarely chooses to. Researchers can describe what tagged whales did. Describing what they could do is a different and harder problem.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +a whale reached 2,992 metres | -the maximum depth any whale can reach | ?how often beaked whales dive that deep}}
This evidence establishes that at least one Cuvier's beaked whale reached 2,992 metres and returned. It does not establish the maximum depth any whale can reach, the depth limit of the species, or how often such dives happen. It rests on a small number of tagged animals in a few regions. It also says nothing about what the whale found at the bottom of that dive. The sensor recorded pressure, and pressure is all it recorded.

### How tag data become depth profiles

{{steps: From raw pressure to depth | pressure readings logged | temperature correction applied | satellite‑compressed summary transmitted | >depth‑time profile reconstructed}}
The tag does not output depth directly; it records pressure at set intervals. Researchers first correct the raw pressure for water temperature and salinity, because the same pressure can correspond to slightly different depths if the water is denser or lighter. After correction, the pressure‑to‑depth conversion (roughly 1 atm per 10 m of seawater) yields a depth value for each timestamp. The compressed summaries sent via satellite are then decompressed on shore, giving scientists a full dive profile that shows descent, bottom time, and ascent.

{{stat: 1 500 | metres | median foraging depth for Cuvier’s beaked whales | NOAA Fisheries}}
NOAA’s species profile for Cuvier’s beaked whale reports that their typical foraging dives centre around 1 500 metres, with a common range of 1 000–2 000 metres. This figure comes from the same tagging efforts that produced the extreme record, showing that while individuals can reach nearly three kilometres, most of their hunting occurs at roughly half that depth.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So how deep can whales dive? At least 2,992 metres, because a tag says so. Beyond that, the honest answer is that the ocean has not been asked often enough. Every tag that rides down with a whale is a small question put to the dark, and each answer is only as deep as the dive it happened to catch.
