## YouTube title

Deep sea creatures are creepers built by darkness — and how we know

## Direct-answer lock

Deep sea creatures look creepy because they are built for a place with no sunlight, little food and crushing pressure, and NOAA puts the start of that sunless midnight zone at about 1,000 metres down. Their huge mouths, glowing lures, long teeth, black or red skin and soft bodies are working solutions to darkness, hunger and pressure, not menace. Part of the creepiness is also ours: some animals, like the blobfish, only look grotesque after being hauled up and decompressed, and many are photographed in ways that hide how small and delicate they are.

### The creepy features are answers to darkness

{{zones: midnight zone}}
Start with the light, because almost everything else follows from it. NOAA divides the open ocean into layers by depth. The sunlight zone runs to roughly 200 metres, where there is enough light for photosynthesis. Below it, the twilight zone fades from dim blue to nothing. By about 1,000 metres you are in the midnight zone, and from there down through the abyssal and hadal zones, the only light is light that animals make themselves. That is most of the ocean's living space. NOAA and the Smithsonian both describe the deep sea as the largest habitat on Earth, and almost all of it is permanently dark.

{{light}}
Water does not dim all colours equally. Red light is absorbed first, in the upper layers, and blue travels furthest. That single physical fact explains one of the strangest-looking habits of deep animals: many of them are red. Shrimp, jellies and some fish carry deep red pigment, which seems like the opposite of camouflage until you remember that, where they live, there is no red light to reflect. Red becomes black. Other species skip the trick and are simply black. Smithsonian researchers have described ultra-black fish whose skin absorbs almost all of the light that hits it, so that a predator's glow or a searchlight finds almost nothing to bounce back. The colours that look lurid in a photograph are, at depth, invisibility.

### Light is the deep sea's main language

{{chain: How MBARI counted glowing animals | years of video | identified each animal | which groups produce light | >about three quarters of the animals}}
If the deep is dark, you might expect animals to give up on light altogether. The opposite happened. Researchers at MBARI, the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute, went through years of video from their remotely operated vehicles. They identified each animal on screen and checked it against what is known about which groups produce light. Their result was that about three quarters of the animals they observed in the water column can make light. That is not a census of every ocean, and it relies on classifying whole groups as glowing or not. But it is direct observation, in place, over a long record, and it makes the point plainly. Down here, light is not absent. It is rare, and it belongs to the animals.

{{contrast: lure | is=bait, camouflage, alarm or signal | not=decoration}}
That is why so many deep sea faces come with a lamp attached. The anglerfish's lure is a modified spine tipped with an organ full of light-producing bacteria. In a place where food is scarce and swimming after it costs energy, it is cheaper to make prey come to you. Other animals use light the opposite way. Many twilight-zone fish have light organs along their bellies that match the faint glow from above, erasing their silhouette for anything looking up. Some shrimp spew glowing clouds to startle a predator. The eerie dots and filaments that make these animals look alien are each a working tool, not decoration: bait, camouflage, alarm or signal.

### A big mouth is what scarce food looks like

{{define: marine snow | a slow drift of dead plankton, waste and mucus | a real meal can be a rare event | NOAA}}
The second pressure on deep bodies is hunger. With no sunlight, there are no plants. Most food in the deep arrives from above as marine snow, a slow drift of dead plankton, waste and mucus, or as the occasional large carcass. NOAA describes marine snow as a primary food source for deep-sea life. It is a dependable supply for the ecosystem as a whole, but for any one animal, a real meal can be a rare event.

{{steps: Building a predator for rare meals | mouths become enormous | teeth grow long | stomachs that stretch | >Nothing that arrives can be wasted}}
Now look at a gulper eel or a viperfish with that in mind. If meals are rare, you cannot afford to miss one, and you cannot afford to refuse one because it is too big. So mouths become enormous relative to the body. Teeth grow long and often curve inward, working less like knives and more like a cage that stops a slippery catch from escaping. Some species have stomachs that stretch to take prey close to their own size. Nothing that arrives can be wasted. Many deep fish have weak muscles and watery flesh, and they spend long periods drifting rather than hunting. The fangs that make them look ferocious belong, in many cases, to animals that move very little and wait.

### Eyes go to extremes, not to average

{{anatomy: Barreleye | Tubular eyes@0.5,0.4 | Transparent shield@0.5,0.2 | Nostrils@0.4,0.55}}
Eyes are where deep animals look most unfamiliar, and where the evidence is clearest about why. In the twilight zone there is still a faint glow from above, so many animals there have enormous eyes to catch it. Deeper, where the only light is bioluminescence, some species keep large sensitive eyes for spotting flashes, while others have small eyes or have lost them in favour of touch, smell and the sense of vibration. The barreleye is the case most people remember. For a long time, specimens brought up in nets suggested an odd fish with tubular eyes fixed pointing upward. Then MBARI filmed living barreleyes with remotely operated vehicles and showed that the head is covered by a transparent shield filled with fluid, and that the tubular eyes can rotate inside it to look up or forward. The two dark spots where you would expect eyes are its nostrils. A creature that looked like a broken design turned out, alive, to make complete sense.

{{text}}
Some deep animals have gone further still. A few dragonfish produce red light from organs near their eyes, and can see it, while most of their neighbours cannot. In a world tuned to blue, that gives them a private searchlight. It is a reminder that strange-looking is often just a sign of solving a problem nobody at the surface ever had to solve.

### Pressure explains the soft bodies, and the blobfish

{{stat: 100 | times | Pressure near 1,000 metres compared with the surface | NOAA}}
The third force is pressure. NOAA notes that pressure rises by about one atmosphere for every 10 metres of seawater, so near 1,000 metres it is roughly 100 times what you feel at the surface, and it keeps climbing all the way to the trenches. Pressure does not crush a body that is mostly water, because water barely compresses, and there is no air space to collapse. What pressure does is disturb molecules: it can stop proteins folding properly and make cell membranes stiff. Deep animals answer with chemistry rather than armour. Fish from greater depths tend to carry more of a small molecule called TMAO, which helps proteins hold their shape, and their bodies often lean towards watery, gelatinous tissue with light skeletons. That softness is part of why they look unfinished to us. It is not weakness. It is what a body looks like when it is not fighting the water.

{{contrast: blobfish | is=a fairly ordinary, if soft, deep-water fish | not=a symbol of ugliness}}
Which brings us to the most famous creeper of all. The blobfish became a symbol of ugliness from photographs of specimens hauled up in trawl nets. But those images show a fish that has been dragged out of high pressure into the air. Its loose, gelatinous body, built to be supported by water, slumps and sags once that support is gone, and its skin is often damaged by the net. In its own habitat, the blobfish looks like a fairly ordinary, if soft, deep-water fish. The face that made it a meme is largely a decompression injury. That is worth holding onto, because it is not the only case where the creepiness was manufactured on the way up.

### Finding a mate in the dark rewrites the body

{{define: sexual parasitism | fuses with her permanently | Not every anglerfish does this | Smithsonian}}
Darkness and scarcity also shape how deep animals meet. In a vast dark volume with few individuals, finding a partner at all is hard. Some deep sea anglerfish solved this with a habit called sexual parasitism, which sounds like horror and works like logistics. The males are much smaller than the females, and in some species a male bites onto a female and fuses with her permanently, their blood supplies joining, so that he becomes a source of sperm she carries with her. The Smithsonian has described this among the anglerfish it studies. Not every anglerfish does this, and the details vary between groups. But it follows the same rule as the teeth and the lures. When encounters are rare, bodies evolve to make each one count.

### The creepiness is partly in the camera

{{checklist: What makes a deep animal look creepy | +dead specimens, brought up in nets | +Photographs were taken under flash | +Close framing hides scale | ?why a particular viewer finds those teeth unsettling}}
Now the part of the answer that belongs to us. For most of the history of deep sea science, people met these animals as dead specimens, brought up in nets, often damaged, discoloured or collapsed. Photographs were taken under flash, which lights up red skin that is invisible at depth and throws hard shadows across teeth. Close framing hides scale: many of the fiercest-looking deep fish are small enough to sit in your hand. And human brains are quick to read faces into anything with two eyes and a mouth, so a gaping jaw reads as aggression even in an animal that is simply waiting.

{{sources: Who watches the deep alive | MBARI=footage from remotely operated vehicles | NOAA Ocean Exploration=streams live video from its expeditions}}
That is changing because of how the deep is now studied. NOAA Ocean Exploration streams live video from its expeditions, and MBARI has built a long record of footage from remotely operated vehicles. Seen alive, in their own water, under controlled light, many of these animals look less like monsters and more like what they are: slow, delicate, finely tuned specialists. The barreleye and the blobfish are both cases where seeing the living animal changed the story.

### Evidence limit

{{contrast: the MBARI figure | is=a strong indication | not=a global count}}
This evidence does not show that every strange feature is an adaptation. Some traits may be side effects, or leftovers from ancestors, and for many species we have only a handful of observations, sometimes only damaged specimens. The MBARI figure on bioluminescence comes from one region and from classifying animals by group, so it is a strong indication rather than a global count. And creepy is not a biological category at all. Science can explain why a deep fish has long teeth. It cannot settle why a particular viewer finds those teeth unsettling. That part sits in us, and the evidence there is thinner.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So the deep sea is not full of monsters. It is full of answers to darkness, hunger and pressure, worked out over a very long time by animals that never needed to look friendly to anyone. The creepiness is real as a feeling. It is just not the animals' purpose.
