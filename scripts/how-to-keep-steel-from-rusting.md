## Direct-answer lock

You keep steel from rusting by denying the reaction one of its ingredients: seal water and oxygen away with a coating, alloy the steel with at least 10.5 percent chromium so it grows its own protective oxide, or let a more reactive metal such as zinc corrode in its place. All three methods work by interrupting the same electrochemical circuit of iron, water and oxygen. None of them is permanent, because coatings get breached, sacrificial zinc gets used up, and even stainless steel can pit where chlorides attack it.

How do you keep steel from rusting?

### Rust is an electrical circuit running across a wet surface

{{chain: RUST AS A CIRCUIT | anode | electrolyte | cathode | >rust}}
Start with what rust actually is. When iron sits under a film of water, some spots on the surface give up electrons, and the iron there dissolves as ions. Those spots are the anode. The electrons travel through the metal to other spots, the cathode, where oxygen dissolved in the water picks them up. The water itself is the electrolyte. It carries ions between the two sites, so the circuit closes. Where the dissolved iron meets the products of the oxygen reaction, they combine into hydrated iron oxides. That reddish-brown material is rust.

{{contrast: RUST | is=an electrochemical reaction | not=simple contact between iron and air}}
Rust is an electrochemical reaction, not simple contact between iron and air, and that is known from experiment rather than assumed. Corrosion scientists can separate the two halves of the reaction, hold a sample at a controlled electrical potential, and measure the current that flows. The results line up with what you see outdoors. Dry air causes very little rusting. Water with the oxygen removed causes very little. Put water, oxygen and something that carries ions together, especially dissolved salt, and the rate climbs. That is why a car driven on salted winter roads, or parked by the sea, rusts faster than the same car kept in a dry garage.

### Rust fails as armour where other oxides succeed

{{define: PASSIVE FILM | a thin, tight oxide layer that slows further reaction | rust is porous and flakes away | ASM International}}
Many metals oxidise. What matters is what the oxide does next. Aluminium forms a skin of oxide almost instantly, and that skin clings to the metal and seals it. Metallurgists call a protective oxide like that a passive film: a thin, tight oxide layer that slows further reaction. Rust behaves differently. It takes up more space than the iron it came from, it holds water, and rust is porous and flakes away. Each flake exposes fresh metal underneath, so the reaction never runs out of surface to attack. That is the core problem every protection method is trying to solve. You either keep the electrolyte away from the iron, change the iron so its oxide behaves more like aluminium's, or change which metal gives up electrons.

{{steps: THREE STRATEGIES | barrier | alloy | sacrifice | >interrupt the circuit}}
Call them barrier, alloy and sacrifice. Each one is a different way to interrupt the circuit.

### A coating protects only while it stays unbroken

{{contrast: COATING | is=a barrier against water and oxygen | not=a cure for rust already started}}
The oldest strategy is the barrier. Paint, oil, wax, powder coat and polymer films all work the same way: they keep water and oxygen off the steel. A coating is a barrier against water and oxygen, not a cure for rust already started. Paint brushed over existing rust traps moisture against the metal, which is why surface preparation matters as much as the paint itself: removing rust and mill scale, and roughening the surface so the coating grips. Even a good coating is slightly permeable. Water slowly diffuses through most organic films, so serious coating systems stack layers. A primer bonds to the steel and often carries corrosion inhibitors, intermediate coats add thickness, and a topcoat resists sunlight and abrasion.

{{checklist: WHERE COATINGS FAIL FIRST | +edges | +welds | +bolt holes | +places where water pools}}
The weakness of any barrier is the breach. A stone chip or a scratch exposes a small patch of steel, and the circuit restarts there. Rust can then creep sideways under the paint, lifting the film from beneath, so the visible blister is often smaller than the damage. That is why inspectors of painted structures look first at edges, welds, bolt holes and places where water pools, rather than at the broad flat faces that look fine.

### Zinc protects steel by corroding first

{{chain: SACRIFICIAL PROTECTION | zinc gives up electrons | steel becomes the cathode | >the steel is spared}}
The sacrificial strategy is stranger, because it keeps working where the coating is broken. Zinc is more reactive than iron. When zinc and steel touch in the same electrolyte, zinc gives up electrons more readily, so the zinc becomes the anode and steel becomes the cathode. The zinc corrodes, and the steel is spared. This is galvanizing. In hot-dip galvanizing, cleaned steel is dipped in molten zinc, which bonds metallurgically to the surface as layers of zinc-iron alloy topped with nearly pure zinc. A scratch through that layer does not start rust the way a scratch through paint does, because the surrounding zinc keeps supplying electrons to the exposed steel.

{{sources: ZINC AND STEEL | USGS=galvanizing is a leading use of zinc | ASTM International=standards for galvanized coatings}}
This is not a niche technique. USGS, the United States Geological Survey, which tracks mineral commodities, reports that galvanizing is a leading use of zinc. ASTM International publishes standards for galvanized coatings, setting how much zinc different products must carry. The same principle scales up. Ship hulls, buried pipelines and offshore platforms carry blocks of zinc, aluminium or magnesium alloys bolted on as sacrificial anodes, and those blocks are replaced as they waste away. Some large structures use impressed current instead: a power supply pushes electrons onto the steel so it stays the cathode. Either way, the protection is consumed. A sacrificial anode that is never replaced eventually protects nothing.

### Stainless steel heals itself, until chlorides break through

{{stat: 10.5 | percent | chromium by mass | ASM International}}
The alloy strategy changes the steel itself. Add chromium, and the chromium at the surface reacts with oxygen to form a passive film of chromium oxide only a few nanometres thick. The usual definition of stainless steel, as ASM International describes it, is at least 10.5 percent chromium by mass. Below that level the film is not reliably continuous. Above it, the film forms and, critically, repairs itself. Scratch stainless steel in air and the exposed chromium reoxidises, sealing the wound. That self-healing behaviour, not hardness or shine, is what the word stainless really describes.

{{contrast: STAINLESS | is=stain-resistant | not=stain-proof}}
Stainless steel is stain-resistant, not stain-proof. The passive film needs oxygen to repair itself, and chloride ions, from seawater, road salt or some cleaning products, attack it at weak points. The result is pitting: small, deep holes that can penetrate a part while most of the surface still looks bright. Crevices are worse, because a narrow gap starves the repair of oxygen while chlorides concentrate inside. Grades meant for marine service add molybdenum, which makes the passive film more resistant to chloride attack. Choosing a stainless grade is a judgement about environment, not a single yes-or-no property.

{{text}}
Weathering steel takes a middle path. Small additions of copper, chromium and other elements make its rust form a denser, more adherent layer that slows further attack. But that layer only develops where the surface regularly dries out. Kept permanently wet, weathering steel simply rusts.

### Corrosion rates are measured, not assumed

{{steps: MASS-LOSS TESTING | weigh the coupon | expose it | strip the rust | weigh again | >metal lost per year}}
Everything so far rests on measurement, and it helps to know which kind. The simplest method is the coupon, a small flat sample of the metal. You weigh the coupon, expose it on a rack at a test site or in a tank, strip the rust chemically, and weigh again. The difference, divided by the exposed area and the time, gives metal lost per year. Exposure sites in industrial, coastal and rural settings run these racks for years, because the atmosphere cannot be hurried.

{{chain: ELECTROCHEMICAL TEST | potential | current | >corrosion rate}}
The faster route uses the circuit itself. Place a sample in an electrolyte, control its potential, and measure the current. Current is a flow of electrons, and every electron released corresponds to iron dissolving, so the current converts directly into a corrosion rate in hours rather than years. Laboratories including NIST have long studied how to make corrosion measurements like these reliable and comparable.

### Salt fog ranks coatings but cannot tell you how long they will last

{{define: ASTM B117 | a standard practice for operating salt spray apparatus | it does not predict service life | ASTM International}}
Manufacturers cannot wait years to qualify a new paint, so they use accelerated tests. The best known is ASTM B117, a standard practice for operating salt spray apparatus. Panels sit in a closed cabinet in a continuous warm fog of salt water, and inspectors record when blisters, creep from a scribed line, or red rust appear. The test is reproducible, which is why so many specifications cite it. But it does not predict service life, and the standard itself cautions that its results have seldom been correlated with performance in natural environments. Real exposure alternates wet and dry, sun and shade, and zinc layers and many coatings depend on those dry spells to form stable corrosion products. A constant fog can rank two systems in the wrong order. Cyclic tests, which alternate spray, humidity and drying, were developed to narrow that gap, and engineers still check them against outdoor racks.

### What to notice in the edit

{{text}}
When the close-up footage of scratched panels appears, watch the scribed line rather than the open face. On the painted panel, rust spreads sideways beneath the film. On the galvanized panel, the scratch stays grey for longer, because zinc around it is doing the corroding. On the stainless sample, look for isolated dark specks rather than a general bloom. Those specks are pits, and they are the reason a bright surface can still hide a failing part.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: WHAT THE EVIDENCE SETTLES | +the mechanism | +the ranking of methods | ?service life at a given site}}
Laboratory chemistry has settled the mechanism, and decades of exposure racks have settled the broad ranking of methods. What this evidence does not establish is service life at a given site. Corrosion rate depends on how often a surface stays wet, how much salt or pollution reaches it, whether water pools in a joint, and how well a coating was applied on one particular day. A test cabinet cannot reproduce all of that, and published exposure data describe the sites where racks stood, not your bridge, railing or boat trailer. For any real structure, the honest answer combines the right method with inspection over time.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
Rust is patient. It needs only iron, water and oxygen, and it waits for the one scratch, crevice or neglected joint where all three meet. Every method that stops it is a way of standing between those ingredients, and every one of them works only as long as someone keeps it standing.
