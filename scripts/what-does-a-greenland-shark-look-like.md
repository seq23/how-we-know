### Its body is built for slowness, not for pursuit

{{define: Somniosus microcephalus | the scientific name of the Greenland shark | microcephalus means small head | Science}}
Start with the outline. The scientific name is Somniosus microcephalus, and microcephalus means small head. Once you see a whole animal laid out, the name makes sense. The body is long, thick and close to cylindrical, and the head looks undersized against the bulk behind it. The snout is short and rounded rather than pointed. The overall impression is less a torpedo than a heavy, slightly flattened log that happens to carry fins.

{{checklist: What the fins show | +two small dorsal fins | +neither dorsal fin carries a spine | -no anal fin | ?whether shape alone proves a slow lifestyle}}
The fins are where expectations break. On many familiar sharks, the first dorsal fin is a tall triangle you could pick out from a boat. A Greenland shark has two small dorsal fins, low and set well back, and neither dorsal fin carries a spine. There is no anal fin, a trait it shares with the dogfish-like sharks it is related to. The pectoral fins are modest for an animal this size. All of it points the same way, toward a body that is not built for speed. Tagging studies have recorded very slow cruising speeds, which fits. But whether shape alone proves a slow lifestyle is a separate question. Shape suggests. The tag records are what carry the weight.

{{contrast: Colour | is=a range from grey to blackish-brown | not=a fixed pattern you can use as a fingerprint}}
Colour is harder than it sounds. Described specimens run in a range from grey to blackish-brown, sometimes with paler patches, scars and scrapes. But colour in an image depends on the light it was made in. The same shark lifted onto a deck under an overcast sky, lit by a diver's lamp, or filmed under a deep-sea camera's lights can look like three different animals. Treat colour as a range, not a fixed pattern you can use as a fingerprint.

{{text}}
Up close, the skin is rough. Like other sharks, it is covered in dermal denticles, small tooth-like scales. That is a family resemblance rather than a special feature, but it shapes what you see on camera: a matte surface that scatters light instead of shining. That is part of why these animals so often look dull, almost velvety, on footage.

### The eyes are small, and often not alone

{{light}}
The eyes are small for the head and sit on its sides. As Smithsonian Ocean's overview of the deep sea explains, sunlight fades quickly with depth, and colours drop out one by one as the water absorbs them. In the darkness where much of this species lives, small eyes are not unusual. What is unusual is what is often hanging from them.

{{chain: How the parasite is identified | sharks examined in hand | the parasite removed and studied | >Ommatokoita elongata}}
Many Greenland sharks carry a copepod called Ommatokoita elongata. A copepod is a small crustacean, a relative of animals at the base of many ocean food webs, but this one has taken a parasitic path. It anchors itself into the surface of the eye and trails away from it as a pale, ribbon-like body. That is the detail most people remember once they have seen it. It is known the plain way: sharks examined in hand, the parasite removed and studied, and the same species found on eye after eye across many catches. Not every shark carries it, and how common it is varies between the places that have been sampled.

{{contrast: Eye parasite | is=damage to the surface of the eye | not=proof the shark is blind}}
The next claim usually made is that the parasite blinds the shark. Be careful here. Examinations have found damage to the surface of the eye where the parasite attaches, and it is reasonable to think vision suffers. But damage to the surface of the eye is not proof the shark is blind, and nobody has run an eye test on a free-swimming shark in deep water. Where little sunlight reaches, the practical cost of poorer vision is also hard to judge. The honest position is that some harm is likely, and the size of that harm is not settled.

### The teeth do two different jobs

{{chain: Reading the jaw | narrow, pointed upper teeth | broad, angled lower teeth | >a grip above and a saw below}}
Open the mouth and the shark stops looking sluggish. The jaw holds two kinds of tooth. Along the top sit narrow, pointed upper teeth, built to hold. Along the bottom sit broad, angled lower teeth whose cusps lean sideways and line up edge to edge, forming something close to a continuous cutting blade. Together they give a grip above and a saw below. The shark can clamp with the top jaw and work its head to slice with the bottom one.

{{text}}
That jaw is why stomach contents matter so much to this story. Greenland sharks caught by researchers and fishers have been found with fish, and sometimes with the remains of seals, in their stomachs. The teeth show the shark can cut large prey. They do not show whether that prey was hunted or scavenged, and for a body this slow, that question remains open. Appearance can tell you what an animal is equipped to do. It cannot tell you how often it does it.

### Its size is measured; the biggest claims are not

{{steps: How a length becomes a record | land the animal | lay it straight | measure from snout to tail | >502 centimetres}}
Size is where that warning applies most directly. A length that deserves trust comes from a simple sequence: land the animal, lay it straight, measure from snout to tail, and record who did it. In the 2016 Science study led by Julius Nielsen, the longest shark was a female at 502 centimetres, just over five metres. That is a measured figure with a method behind it. It is not a claim about the maximum size the species can reach. It is only the largest animal in that sample.

{{contrast: Size claims | is=a measured specimen with a method | not=a repeated number with no specimen behind it}}
You will find larger figures in popular articles and older accounts. Some may be accurate. But a repeated number with no specimen behind it is not the same as a measured specimen with a method, and this script cannot trace those larger figures to animals someone measured and recorded. So you will not get a maximum here. Estimates vary, and the published record is thinner at the top end than the confident numbers online suggest.

### You cannot read its age from its appearance

{{chain: Eye lens dating | lens nucleus formed before birth | radiocarbon measured | compared with the bomb pulse | >an age estimate with a wide range}}
Here is a limit of looking that surprises most people. A Greenland shark gives you almost no visible clue to its age, and the growth bands used to age many other sharks are not usable in this species. Nielsen's team turned to the eye instead. The centre of the eye lens, the lens nucleus formed before birth, stays essentially unchanged for life. That tissue had its radiocarbon measured and compared with the bomb pulse, the spike in radiocarbon released by atmospheric nuclear weapons tests in the middle of the twentieth century, which marked ocean food webs. Sharks born after the spike carry its signal. Older ones do not. Combine that with body length, and you get an age estimate with a wide range.

{{uncertain: 392 | years | 120 | stated range | Estimated age of the largest female}}
The width of that range is the point. In the Science study, the estimated age of the largest female, the 502 centimetre animal, was 392 years, with an uncertainty of 120 years either way, a range from 272 to 512 years. The study's headline in Science is that Greenland sharks live for at least 272 years. Notice what the method cannot do. It cannot look at a shark in a video and tell you its age. A long body suggests an old animal, but growth in the cold is so poorly known that two sharks of the same length could differ in age by a great deal.

### A photograph does not always name the species

{{contrast: A sleeper shark on video | is=evidence of the genus | not=proof of the species}}
Everything so far assumes you know which shark you are looking at. Often you do not. The Greenland shark has a close relative, the Pacific sleeper shark, and the two look strikingly alike: the same heavy body, small fins, absent anal fin and muted colour. Genetic work has even reported animals with mixed ancestry where their ranges come near each other. So when a camera far from the Arctic films a large, slow, dark shark with small fins, the image is evidence of the genus, not proof of the species.

{{steps: How a species is confirmed | a body or tissue sample | genetic testing | comparison with known specimens | >a confident name}}
Confirming the species usually takes more than a picture. It takes a body or tissue sample, genetic testing, and comparison with known specimens. Only then do you get a confident name. That is why so many descriptions of what a Greenland shark looks like lean on landed animals rather than live footage. Footage shows behaviour and setting. Specimens settle identity.

### What to notice in the edit

{{sources: Who stands behind which claim | Science=the length and age figures | Smithsonian Ocean=how light fades with depth}}
As you watch, notice which images show dead animals and which show living ones. Landed sharks reveal proportions, fins and teeth clearly, but bodies sag out of water and eyes lose their shape. Living footage shows posture and slowness, but colour is at the mercy of the lights. Notice too where each number comes from. Science stands behind the length and age figures. Smithsonian Ocean stands behind how light fades with depth. Nothing on screen claims more than those sources do.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +the body plan, fins and teeth | +the eye parasite is a copepod | -the maximum size | ?whether the parasite impairs vision}}
This evidence settles the body plan, fins and teeth well, because those are visible on every landed animal and consistent across them. It settles that the eye parasite is a copepod. It does not establish the maximum size the species reaches, and it does not establish how colour varies across its range. It leaves open whether the parasite impairs vision in any meaningful way. And it cannot turn an image into an age. A careful description of this shark has edges, and the edges are part of the description.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So picture it plainly: a long, dark, heavy shark with a small head, small eyes, low fins and no anal fin, sometimes trailing a pale thread from one eye, armed with a jaw that grips above and cuts below. Its appearance is well described. Its age, its limits and much of its life in the dark are still being measured.
