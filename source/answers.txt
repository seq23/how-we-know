1. The first thing I picture is just black. Not blue water, not fish, not even the bottom. Just this massive amount of black space where you can’t really see what’s next to you or under you. It feels quiet but not peaceful quiet. More like you are somewhere you were not designed to be.

2. 36,000 feet by itself is just a number to me. Saying Everest could fit inside it and still be underwater is what makes it land. Then I start thinking about the amount of water above you and the pressure and how completely unreachable the surface is from down there. That part is insane.

3. What unsettles me is not just that the ocean is large. Space is large too. It is the fact that you can be inside the ocean and have no real orientation. No horizon, no light, no obvious way to tell what is above or below you. And you could have something enormous relatively close to you and never know it. I don’t like that.

4. Both. Definitely both. Some of them look completely wrong according to every normal standard we have for what an animal should look like. Transparent heads, huge teeth, detached-looking jaws, lights growing out of their bodies. That creeps me out, but I’m also fascinated because none of it is random. They look like that because something about that design works down there.

5. Probably the giant squid. Not because I think one is going to attack me. It’s the size plus the intelligence plus the fact that it can exist around us without us regularly seeing it. Anything with huge eyes and that many arms already has an advantage psychologically. I do not need to encounter that in person.

6. Both. The monster part is mostly imagination. Most deep-sea animals are not hunting humans and probably want nothing to do with us. But being afraid of the deep itself is completely rational. The pressure can destroy equipment. It is freezing, dark, hard to navigate and you cannot breathe. The environment is much more dangerous than whatever creature people imagine is waiting down there.

7. My first thought is that it is built for something. “Ugly” is a surface-level judgment. If an animal has survived in an environment that extreme for millions of years, then the weirdness probably has a function. I want to know what problem that body is solving.

8. Bioluminescence is probably the most impressive to me because they use it for everything. Hunting, hiding, communicating, attracting mates, confusing predators. Some animals even use light underneath their bodies to erase their own silhouette. That is not just surviving. That is basically manipulating the information available to everything around you.

9. Yes, honestly it reminds me of companies and systems. Something can look completely irrational when you judge it outside of the environment it was built for. Then you understand the pressure it had to survive and suddenly every strange feature makes sense. A lot of people judge the visible result without understanding the conditions that created it.

10. It excites me more than it bothers me, but it does bother the evidence-driven part of me. We talk about the ocean like it is a known thing because it is on every map, but most of what is under the surface is still barely documented. We are living on a planet we have not finished looking at.

11. It sounds absurd at first. Like how did we map another planet better than the floor of our own ocean? But I also understand that Mars can be mapped from orbit and the ocean blocks light and signals, so technically it is not as ridiculous as it sounds. Still, it is humbling. Our own planet is withholding an entire world from us.

12. Yes, I think we should spend more on it. But I mean serious scientific exploration, mapping, robotics, sensors and long-term data collection. Not rich people doing dangerous stunts so they can say they went somewhere. The ocean affects climate, weather, food systems and the basic functioning of the planet. Exploring it is not a random luxury.

13. Honestly? Probably not. At least not in some tiny experimental submersible where I am expected to trust one person’s confidence. I would need a ridiculous amount of independent testing, certification, redundancy and operating history. Even then, I might decide the footage is enough for me. I like discovery. I don’t necessarily need to physically place myself inside every dangerous environment to respect it.

14. I think they have a different relationship with risk. I respect the people who do it seriously because that work requires technical discipline, patience and the ability to function while knowing how little room there is for error. But I would want to know whether they respect the environment or whether they think courage makes them exempt from reality. Those are not the same person.

15. Titan made me think about the difference between exploration and recklessness. Vision does not cancel engineering. Confidence does not replace testing. And being willing to take a risk does not mean everyone else involved fully understands that risk. What bothered me was the idea that basic warnings and controls could be treated like obstacles to innovation. Sometimes the rule exists because physics does not care how disruptive you think you are.

16. I used to think the deep ocean was mostly empty. Just a huge dark floor with a few strange fish scattered around. It is much more structurally and biologically complicated than that. There are trenches, mountains, vents, currents and entire ecosystems operating under conditions that should make life seem impossible.

17. I think people misunderstand it as a collection of monsters. The creatures get attention because they look strange, but the real story is the system. Pressure, darkness, scarcity, temperature, chemistry and time all shape what can live there. A lot of deep-sea animals are also smaller and more fragile than the footage makes them look. The environment is the giant, not necessarily the animal.

18. The footage feels real, but the documentary experience is obviously constructed. The lights, music, editing and narration tell you what emotion to have. We see the most dramatic thirty seconds from what may have been hundreds of hours underwater. That doesn’t mean it is fake. It means I want to separate what the camera actually captured from the story built around it.

19. Yes, this may actually be one of the most interesting parts to me. How do we know an animal behaves a certain way when we have seen it three times? How much does an ROV’s light change its behavior? What happens to a specimen when you drag it through thousands of feet of pressure change? Are we looking at the animal as it naturally exists or at what is left after retrieval? Deep-sea science has to build conclusions from incomplete observations, damaged specimens, sonar, water samples and tiny windows of footage. I’m interested in the evidence chain, not just the final fact somebody puts on screen.

20. Because the deep ocean is not one subject. It is an entire world sitting underneath the one we think we understand. Every creature is a different survival problem. Every expedition is a technology story, a science story and a human-risk story. There is mystery, but there is also evidence. There are things we have recorded once, things we know only from fragments and probably things we have never encountered at all.

So 200 videos does not feel excessive to me. It almost feels small. The actual reason to keep making them is that the deep ocean exposes how limited our idea of “normal” is. Every time we think we understand what life needs, something down there shows us another way.
