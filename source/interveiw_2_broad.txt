1. When somebody gives me a fact with a number in it my first instinct is basically okay…where did the number come from? Not because I automatically think they are lying. I just know numbers can make almost anything sound authoritative. What was actually measured? By who? Over what period of time? How big was the sample? Is this the original number or has it been repeated through 50 articles that all trace back to the same study?

And then I want context because saying something increased by 300% sounds dramatic until you find out it went from one case to four. A precise number is not automatically a reliable number.

2. I trust sources that show me how they know what they claim to know. Give me the data, the method, the limitations and preferably the original source. I trust somebody more when they tell me what the evidence cannot prove.

I trust documentation over reputation. Reputation matters, obviously, but a respected institution can still publish weak work and some random person can uncover something real if they document it properly. I also look for whether independent sources reached the same conclusion or whether everybody is just citing each other in a circle.

3. Yes. Absolute confidence around something complicated makes me trust somebody less. Especially when they never qualify anything, never admit uncertainty and answer every objection immediately like they have been waiting to win an argument.

Real expertise usually sounds more precise than confident. An expert can tell you exactly what is known, what is likely, what is disputed and where the evidence stops. Somebody performing expertise usually just sounds certain about everything.

4. “Scientists say” is almost meaningless by itself. Which scientists? How many? What did they actually say? Was it one paper? Was it peer reviewed? Was it replicated? Is there a consensus or did a journalist take one cautious sentence and turn it into a definitive headline?

I am not anti-science at all. I’m anti using “science” like it is a magical authority word that means nobody has to show their work. Science is a process. It is not a person standing behind a curtain handing down permanent answers.

5. “We don’t know yet” is a good answer when somebody can explain what we do know, what is missing and what evidence would help answer the question. That is honest uncertainty.

It becomes a cop-out when people use uncertainty selectively. Like there is enough evidence for them to make a dramatic claim, raise money or create fear, but suddenly “we don’t know” when somebody asks them to defend it. Or when nobody has seriously tried to find out and the phrase is being used to end the conversation.

I would rather hear an honest “we don’t know” than a polished fake answer. But I still want to know why we don’t know.

6. Most big engineering disasters look like bad luck at the exact moment they happen, but when you go backward you usually find a chain of decisions. A warning was dismissed. Maintenance was delayed. Somebody normalized a problem because nothing terrible happened the first ten times. A deadline mattered more than a test. Nobody had the authority to stop the process, or the person who did have the authority had an incentive not to.

Bad design matters, but bad decisions are often what allow the bad design to stay in operation. The final failure might take seconds. The system usually spent months or years creating the conditions for it.

7. Titan stuck with me because it is such a clean example of confidence being treated like evidence. You can call yourself an innovator all day. The materials still have limits. Pressure still exists. Physics does not care about your vision, your money or how many successful trips you already completed.

The part that stays with me is that there were warnings. That changes the story. It was not simply an unpredictable tragedy. It raises the question of how many people saw pieces of the risk and why the system still kept moving forward.

8. “Move fast and break things” makes sense when the thing you might break is a website button. It is insane when the thing you might break is a person, an aircraft, a bridge, a financial system or anything people cannot easily recover from.

Speed is not automatically innovation. Sometimes speed just means you skipped the part where reality gets a vote. The acceptable failure rate depends entirely on what is at stake. You can test aggressively without pretending consequences do not exist.

9. I am much more interested in the years before it broke. The actual break is usually the most visually dramatic and the least surprising part once you understand the history.

I want to know when the first warning appeared. Who noticed it? Who documented it? Who had the power to act? What incentives made doing nothing easier? How many times did the system almost fail and get called successful because it survived?

That is where the real story is. Failure is rarely one bad moment. It is usually a long series of small permissions.

10. Millions of years is mostly abstract to me. I understand the number intellectually, but I cannot actually feel it. Comparisons help. Entire species appearing and disappearing before humans existed helps. But at a certain scale, numbers stop feeling like time and just become language.

11. Earth, probably. Space is fascinating, but Earth bothers me more because we are already here and still do not understand huge parts of it. The ocean, the interior of the planet, underground ecosystems, even parts of our own bodies. We live inside systems we cannot fully explain.

12. Yes. Ancient people built things that still exist while we build things expected to be replaced in 30 years. I’m interested in what knowledge they had that was never written down, what we underestimate because they lacked modern machinery and how much human capability disappears when a civilization disappears.

13. Octopuses genuinely impress me. The camouflage, problem-solving, flexibility and the fact that so much of their nervous system is distributed through their arms. They do not just look unusual. They seem to represent a completely different solution to intelligence.

Trees also impress me because they look passive but they are constantly responding, communicating, reallocating resources and surviving across timescales humans barely understand.

14. I think of evolution as whatever survived. “Designed” can make it sound like there was a plan or a perfect final objective. There wasn’t. Something varied, the environment applied pressure and whatever reproduced successfully remained.

That also explains why nature has so many strange compromises. Evolution does not start over with a clean blueprint. It keeps modifying what is already there.

15. It does not bother me that instruments extend what we can know. That is the whole point of technology. Human senses are extremely limited.

What interests me is the translation layer. An instrument does not simply hand us reality. It produces a signal, then humans calibrate it, process it and interpret it. I want to understand that chain before treating the final image or number like direct truth.

16. Yes, but I also expect it. Measurements are made by people using tools built by people inside systems with assumptions. Something can be accepted for decades because everybody trusts the calibration, the method or the previous work.

The disturbing part is not that mistakes happen. It is how resistant institutions can become once careers, textbooks and reputations depend on the old answer remaining true.

17. An honest documentary shows me where the evidence came from and separates fact from reconstruction. It does not use music, editing and selective footage to manufacture certainty that the evidence does not support.

A documentary starts to feel manipulative when every detail is arranged to force one emotion or conclusion. Dramatic music is not proof. A confident narrator is not proof. And reenacting something is fine, but tell me it is a reenactment.

18. Explaining something means making the underlying idea understandable without removing what makes it true. Dumbing it down means removing the complications until the explanation is technically easy but no longer accurate.

You can use simple language without treating people like they are stupid. The real skill is making complexity clear while keeping the important caveats intact.

19. I find questions worth chasing when the answer changes how I understand a system. Why did this fail? How do we actually know this? What had to happen for this thing to exist? What are people repeating without checking? What is hidden by the way the story is usually told?

I like questions where the obvious answer starts falling apart once you look closely.

20. It feels like the subject gets larger instead of smaller. One answer rearranges everything around it and suddenly I can see connections that were invisible before.

That is probably the feeling I am chasing. Not just collecting another fact, but realizing the model I had in my head was incomplete and now I have a better one.
