## YouTube title

The vampire squid is not a squid

## Direct-answer lock

The vampire squid is a small, soft deep-sea cephalopod, about 30 centimetres long, dark reddish-brown to black, with eight arms joined by a web of skin, a pair of fins that stick out like ears, and eyes that, relative to its body, are often described as the largest of any animal. Despite its name and its fearsome cape, it is neither a true squid nor an octopus. It is the only living species in its own order, and it feeds on drifting debris rather than hunting.

### Its body is a cloak, not a torpedo

{{anatomy: The body plan | fins@0.5,0.15 | eyes@0.5,0.35 | webbed arms@0.5,0.7 | cirri@0.35,0.8}}
Start with the body plan. A true squid is a streamlined tube built for speed. The vampire squid is closer to a small umbrella. Its body, called the mantle, is short and rounded, and eight arms hang from it. Those arms do not move freely the way an octopus's arms do. A sheet of skin, the web, runs between them almost to their tips. When the arms spread, the animal looks like it is holding open a dark cape. The vampire part of the name comes from that cape. Two fins sit on the mantle, one on each side, and from the front they look like ears. The animal flaps them to swim, and it swims slowly.

{{contrast: cirri | is=soft fleshy spines | not=teeth or hooks}}
Turn the cape inside out and the inner surface looks fierce. Rows of pale, pointed structures run down each arm. They look like fangs, and footage is often edited to make them seem like fangs. They are cirri, which are soft fleshy spines, not teeth or hooks. They do not bite and they do not grip. This is the first place where what the animal looks like and what it actually is come apart. A still photograph of the inner web reads as armour. Video of the living animal shows the cirri as flexible, almost limp. Their likely role is sensory. The word likely matters. That role is an inference from structure and behaviour. Nobody has measured it as a function.

{{text}}
Then there are the eyes. The animal is roughly a foot long, and its eyes are large enough to dominate its head. Relative to body size, they are often described as the largest of any animal. Their colour on film depends on the lighting. Under the white lamps of an underwater camera they can look blue or red. The skin behaves the same way. The animal is usually described as black to reddish-brown, but the colour you see in any image partly depends on the lamp. Deep water is dark. Every colour photograph of a deep animal records light that the camera itself brought down.

### The first pictures came from wrecked specimens

{{chain: How the animal was first known | trawl nets | preserved in a jar | drawn and described | >distance from the living animal}}
Here is how the animal was first known. For a long time, deep-sea animals were brought up in trawl nets towed behind ships. A net is a good way to learn that something exists. It is a poor way to learn what that thing looks like. The vampire squid is soft and gelatinous. A body like that, dragged up from the deep in a net full of other animals, arrives torn, collapsed and drained of colour. Each specimen was then preserved in a jar, drawn and described, and every step added distance from the living animal. Preservative changes colour and shrinks tissue. A collapsed web gives no hint of how the animal holds itself.

{{steps: What changed the picture | remotely operated vehicles | high-definition cameras | in its own posture | >checked against preserved specimens}}
What changed the picture was going down to the animal instead of pulling it up. MBARI, working off the coast of California, has spent decades sending remotely operated vehicles into the deep water column. These are robots tethered to a ship and fitted with high-definition cameras. A camera records the animal undamaged and in its own posture. It records minutes of movement instead of a single frozen moment. Most modern descriptions rest on this kind of evidence. A confident, detailed account of how a vampire squid looks usually comes from video, checked against preserved specimens. Specimens alone did not produce it.

{{sources: Each kind of evidence | MBARI=video records shape and behaviour | NOAA Ocean Exploration=broader surveys of the deep | Smithsonian Ocean=the wider ecosystem}}
No single source draws the whole picture. MBARI's video records shape and behaviour. Preserved specimens in natural history collections let anatomists dissect and measure the parts that video cannot reach. The broader surveys of the deep, from bodies such as NOAA Ocean Exploration and Smithsonian Ocean, place the animal within the wider ecosystem. Each kind of evidence covers a weakness in another. Video cannot show stomach contents. A jar cannot show posture. The modern description is trustworthy because these sources agree.

### It is not a squid, and not an octopus either

{{contrast: vampire squid | is=its own order | not=a squid}}
Despite the name, it is not a squid. It is not an octopus either. Its common name calls it a squid, but the first scientific description placed it among the octopuses, and later work moved it again. It shares features with both groups. Like an octopus, it has eight webbed arms. Unlike an octopus, it also carries two extra filaments tucked into pockets. Today it sits in its own order, Vampyromorphida, and it is the only living species in that order. Its formal name, Vampyroteuthis infernalis, translates roughly as vampire squid from hell. A name records first impressions. It is not a description.

{{checklist: Its own order | +eight webbed arms | +two extra filaments | -not a squid | -not an octopus}}
The classification has a second history. Early specimens differed from one another because of damage, age and preservation. Several were described under different names before researchers recognised them as a single species. The fins show why this happened. Young vampire squid pass through a stage with two pairs of fins, and adults have one pair. A biologist working from a few jars could reasonably conclude that a juvenile and an adult were different animals. A better argument did not fix the error. More specimens did, and eventually so did watching living animals.

### Two filaments explain the strangest part of its outline

{{anatomy: The feeding filaments | filaments@0.2,0.6 | filaments@0.8,0.6 | pockets@0.45,0.45}}
The feeding filaments are easy to miss in a photograph, because the animal can pull them back into pockets between its arms. Extended, they can reach much farther than the length of its body. For a long time, nobody was sure what they were for. The answer came from research at MBARI, which combined video of living animals with the stomach contents of preserved specimens. Together, these showed that the vampire squid uses its filaments for collecting marine snow. Marine snow is the steady fall of dead plankton, faecal pellets and other debris sinking from above. The animal gathers this material in mucus and passes it to its mouth with its arms.

{{contrast: collector | is=collecting marine snow | not=hunting live prey}}
That finding is why the fearsome look misleads. Most cephalopods are predators. Judged by what is in its stomach and what it is seen doing, the vampire squid is a collector. It is not hunting live prey. It drifts, lets the filaments trail, and waits for food to settle onto them. The cape, the cirri and the dark colour look like a hunter's equipment. They belong to an animal that lives on the ocean's sinking leftovers.

### Light is part of how it looks

{{light}}
Where it lives, sunlight has almost disappeared. Water absorbs red light first, close to the surface. At the depths this animal prefers, only a faint trace of blue remains. That is too little light for most of the colours in photographs to exist naturally. In that blue gloom, a dark red or black body is effectively invisible. An animal coloured this way is not displaying a colour. It is removing one. The light it does display is its own.

{{text}}
The vampire squid is covered in light organs called photophores, and the tips of its arms can glow. When it is disturbed, it can do something unusual. Other cephalopods release a cloud of dark ink. The vampire squid can instead release a sticky cloud of glowing particles from its arm tips. The cloud lingers in the water while the animal moves away. In another defensive display, it pulls its webbed arms up and back over its body and turns itself inside out, so that the cirri face outward. Researchers sometimes call this the pineapple posture. In that position the animal looks like a spiny ball, and the dark cape disappears entirely.

### Its habitat explains its build

{{zones: twilight}}
MBARI reports that the vampire squid most often lives between about 600 and 900 metres deep. That is in the twilight zone, in a layer called the oxygen minimum zone, where the water holds very little oxygen. Few predators can stay there for long. The vampire squid's body suits that setting. Its tissues are soft and watery, its muscles are weak compared with a squid's, and its metabolism is slow. An animal that spends little energy can survive on little oxygen and little food. That physical logic explains its appearance. A soft, slow, drifting body is the shape of a life lived on a tight budget.

### What to notice in the edit

{{ambient}}
When you watch footage of this animal, look for three things. First, look at the lighting, because the camera carried down every colour you see. Second, check whether the animal is swimming with its fins, drifting with its filaments out, or curled into its defensive posture. Those are different shapes of the same body. Third, look at the scale. On a large screen with no reference object nearby, a 30-centimetre animal can look enormous. The footage is accurate. Your sense of its size is not.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +posture and behaviour on video | +diet from stomach contents | -colour under natural light | ?how long an individual lives}}
Here is what the evidence settles and what it leaves open. The posture and behaviour on video, and the diet from stomach contents, are well supported. But most video of living vampire squid comes from a few regions where research vehicles dive regularly, and many observations come from off California. The animal lives across tropical and temperate oceans, so our picture of its behaviour comes from a narrow slice of a wide range. Every image is artificially lit, so its colour under natural light is inferred, not observed. The purpose of the cirri is inferred from their structure. Other basic facts remain poorly established, including how long an individual lives, how often it breeds and how many there are. Estimates vary, and the published evidence is thin.

### Closing

{{text}}
So the vampire squid is a small, dark, soft animal with a webbed cape, ear-like fins and very large eyes. The more interesting part is how scientists earned that picture: first from wrecked bodies in jars, then from cameras that went down to meet the animal where it lives.
