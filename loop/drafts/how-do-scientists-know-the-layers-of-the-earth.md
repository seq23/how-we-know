## Direct-answer lock

Scientists know Earth's layers mainly by timing earthquake waves as they cross the planet, because those waves bend, change speed and sometimes vanish where one material gives way to another. The top of the core, at about 2,900 kilometres down, the liquid outer core and the solid inner core were all located this way. Laboratory work on rock, the planet's overall density, iron meteorites and the magnetic field act as independent cross-checks, but no drill has ever reached the mantle where it sits.

### Earthquakes are the only signal that crosses the interior

{{define: P-wave | a compression wave that travels through solids and liquids | an S-wave cannot cross a liquid | USGS}}
When a fault slips, it sends energy outward in several kinds of wave. Two matter most here. The first is the P-wave, a compression wave, a push and pull in the direction of travel, like sound. It moves through solid rock, through liquid and through gas. The second is the S-wave, a shearing wave that shakes material side to side as it passes. Shearing needs something rigid to grip. A liquid has no rigidity, so an S-wave cannot travel through it at all. The USGS describes both, and that single difference, one wave that crosses liquid and one that cannot, ends up doing an enormous amount of work.

{{chain: Travel-time method | earthquake | seismometer | arrival time | >wave speed at depth}}
Here is the basic method. An earthquake happens at a known place and a known moment. Seismometers scattered across the world record when each kind of wave arrives. If you know where the wave started, where it was recorded and how long it took, you can work out how fast it must have travelled. Do that for thousands of earthquakes and thousands of stations, and you are no longer measuring one path. You are measuring a web of paths that cross the interior at every angle. From that web, you can solve for how wave speed changes with depth. Wave speed depends on the stiffness and density of the material, so a map of speed is, indirectly, a map of what the inside is made of and what state it is in.

{{text}}
One more property makes the method sharper. Waves do not travel in straight lines through the planet. Rock gets denser and stiffer with depth, so waves speed up as they go down, and they bend, curving back toward the surface. Where a wave meets a sudden boundary, part of it reflects and part of it bends sharply. Those reflections and kinks leave signatures in the records, and the signatures are where the layers show up.

### A jump in speed marks the base of the crust

{{contrast: Moho | is=a sudden change in wave speed | not=a surface anyone has seen}}
The first boundary was found in 1909 by the Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić. Studying records of a regional earthquake, he noticed that stations at a certain distance received two sets of arrivals instead of one. The simplest explanation was that some waves had gone down, entered a faster layer beneath the surface rock, run along it, and come back up ahead of the waves that stayed shallow. That faster layer is the mantle, and the boundary above it now carries his name, shortened to the Moho. Notice what the evidence actually was. Not a rock, not a core sample. A timing pattern that only made sense if speed jumped at some depth.

### A missing wave revealed a liquid core

{{timeline: 1906=Oldham | 1909=Mohorovičić | 1914=Gutenberg | 1936=Lehmann}}
The core came first in the record, from absence rather than arrival. In 1906 the British geologist Richard Dixon Oldham argued from earthquake records that waves passing through the deep centre were delayed, which pointed to a distinct core. In 1914 the German seismologist Beno Gutenberg estimated the depth of its boundary, and his estimate sits close to the figure still used today.

{{anatomy: Shadow zone | earthquake@0.5,0.05 | core@0.5,0.5 | shadow zone@0.85,0.75}}
The key observation is called the shadow zone. Stations on the far side of the planet from an earthquake, in a broad band, record no direct S-waves at all. P-waves do reach some of them, though bent and delayed. If the core were solid, S-waves should come through. They do not. The cleanest explanation is that the outer part of the core is liquid, and a liquid will not carry a shearing wave. The P-waves that do get through are refracted by the core boundary, which is what bends them away and leaves its own gap. So the outer core was not discovered by seeing liquid. It was inferred because a wave that cannot cross liquid stopped arriving exactly where geometry said it should.

### A faint arrival in the shadow proved a solid centre

{{steps: Lehmann's argument | faint P-waves arrive inside the shadow | a liquid-only core cannot send them there | a faster inner body can | >the inner core is solid}}
The tidy picture of a liquid core had a problem. Very faint P-waves were turning up inside the shadow zone, where a fully liquid core should have bent them away. In 1936 the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann proposed that these arrivals had been bent by a smaller, separate body at the centre, one in which waves travelled faster. That body is the inner core. Later work strengthened the case that it is solid. The striking part is the reasoning. Lehmann did not have a better instrument than her colleagues. She took a small, inconvenient signal seriously instead of filing it as noise, and found that the existing model could not produce it.

### The oceans leave holes in the network

{{sources: Instruments | USGS=global earthquake monitoring | WHOI=ocean-bottom seismometers}}
The travel-time method has a weakness that matters to anyone who studies the sea. It needs stations, and stations sit mostly on land. Most of Earth's surface is ocean, so for a long time, huge stretches of the planet had no ears at all. Waves crossing the interior beneath the oceans were recorded only at the edges. That leaves blind patches in the web of paths, and blind patches blur the map. One answer is the ocean-bottom seismometer, an instrument lowered to the seafloor, left to listen for months, then recalled to the surface with its data. Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution has developed and deployed these instruments for decades. They fill in the paths under the oceans, and they also show the crust beneath the sea floor in detail.

{{contrast: Oceanic crust | is=thin and young | not=the same as continental crust}}
What they reveal is that the crust is not one thing. Under the oceans it is thin, only several kilometres, and geologically young, created at mid-ocean ridges and recycled at trenches. Under continents it is far thicker and can be very old. That difference is why the sea floor has always been the obvious place to try to reach the mantle by drilling.

### Drilling tests only the shallowest answer

{{checklist: Direct sampling | +the crust has been drilled | -the mantle has not been reached in place | ?how deep ocean drilling can go}}
In the early 1960s, a project funded by the NSF, known as Project Mohole, drilled into the sea floor with that goal. It recovered rock from below the sediment and proved that deep-ocean drilling was possible, but it was ended long before reaching the Moho. On land, the Kola Superdeep Borehole went further than any other hole, to about 12 kilometres, and still stopped in the crust, where heat made further drilling impractical. Today, JAMSTEC operates the drilling vessel Chikyu, built partly with the ambition of one day reaching the mantle through oceanic crust. That goal remains open. Drilling, so far, checks the outermost skin of the model and nothing beneath it.

{{text}}
Rock from the mantle does reach the surface by other routes. Volcanoes sometimes carry fragments up from depth, and in a few places slabs of ancient sea floor and the mantle beneath it have been pushed up onto land. These samples are valuable, but they have been moved, cooled and altered on the way. They tell you what mantle rock can be like. They do not tell you exactly what sits at a given depth right now.

### Independent clues have to agree

{{chain: Cross-checks | planet's mass | average density | dense interior | >iron-rich core}}
Seismology does not stand alone, and its conclusions are believed partly because other lines of evidence point the same way. The first is mass. Measurements of gravity give the total mass of the planet, and dividing by its volume gives an average density well above that of the rock you find at the surface. Something deep inside must be much denser. The way Earth spins and wobbles adds a further constraint on how that mass is arranged, concentrated toward the centre.

{{define: Iron meteorites | fragments of metal-rich bodies from the early solar system | an analogy, not a sample of Earth | USGS}}
The second is composition. Iron meteorites are thought to be pieces of the metal cores of small bodies that broke apart early in the solar system's history. They suggest what a planetary core can be made of, mostly iron with some nickel. The third is magnetism. A planet-wide magnetic field is best explained by moving, electrically conducting liquid, and a liquid iron outer core supplies exactly that. Laboratory experiments that squeeze and heat minerals to extreme conditions then test whether candidate materials would carry waves at the speeds seismology measures. When four independent methods agree, the agreement is the evidence.

### What to notice in the edit

As you watch the cutaway diagrams in this episode, notice that each boundary is drawn as a crisp line. That is a convention, not an observation. What was measured is a change in wave speed over some depth range, sometimes sharp, sometimes gradual. The coloured shells are a summary of travel times, drawn cleanly so the eye can follow them. The cleanness belongs to the drawing.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What seismology settles | +the core boundary exists | +the outer core is liquid | -exact composition at depth | ?detailed structure of the inner core}}
This evidence firmly establishes that the boundaries exist, roughly where they are, and which layer is liquid. It does not establish the exact chemistry of the core, because waves measure stiffness and density, and different mixtures of elements can produce similar speeds. It does not give temperatures directly. Those come from experiments and models, and the published range is wide. The inner core in particular is still debated, including whether it has internal structure of its own. And under the oceans, where stations remain sparse, the map is less sharp than under well-instrumented continents.
