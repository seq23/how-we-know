## YouTube title

Seized chocolate needs liquid, not heat

## Direct-answer lock

Chocolate that stays thick and grainy over heat has usually seized or scorched, and more heat will not rescue it. Seized chocolate, where a little water has glued the sugar into a paste, can be loosened by stirring in more warm liquid until it becomes a smooth sauce, but scorched chocolate cannot be reversed. To melt it cleanly in the first place, keep every tool dry and warm it gently, stirring, rather than pushing the heat higher.

## Narration

### Cold open

{{stat: 34 | degrees Celsius | stable crystal form | American Oil Chemists' Society}}
A few drops of water can turn a bowl of glossy chocolate into stiff paste. Chocolate that stays thick and grainy over heat has usually seized or scorched, and more heat will not rescue it. That surprises people, because the fat in chocolate should be the easy part. Research published through the American Oil Chemists' Society places the melting point of cocoa butter's stable crystal form at roughly 34 degrees Celsius, just below the temperature inside your mouth. Warm water is more than enough to melt it. So when a bowl will not loosen, the problem is rarely too little heat. Something else in the bowl has changed. The evidence for what that something is comes from crystallography, from calorimetry, and from the ordinary behaviour of sugar.

### Chocolate is solid sugar suspended in fat

{{cross_section: dark chocolate | sugar and cocoa particles in cocoa butter}}
To see why heat is not the fix, picture the bar cut open. A piece of dark chocolate is mostly solid particles: finely ground sugar crystals and fragments of roasted cocoa solids, packed so closely that your tongue cannot feel them separately. Between them runs a continuous film of fat. So the honest picture is sugar and cocoa particles in cocoa butter, with very little water anywhere. Milk chocolate adds milk solids, and white chocolate leaves out the cocoa solids but keeps the butter. When you melt chocolate, only the fat actually melts. The sugar does not. It stays solid the whole time, and the melted bar flows only because each grain is coated and lubricated by liquid fat.

{{define: polymorphism | one fat able to crystallise in several distinct forms | describes structure, not the fat's chemical recipe | American Oil Chemists' Society}}
That fat is unusual. Cocoa butter is dominated by a small number of triglycerides built from palmitic, stearic and oleic acids, and because those molecules are so alike, they can stack together in several different ways. Chemists call this polymorphism: one fat able to crystallise in several distinct forms. Cocoa butter has six recognised forms, numbered one to six, each with its own melting range. The term describes structure, not the fat's chemical recipe. The molecules are the same across all six forms; only their packing differs. Form five is the one chocolate makers aim for when they temper, and it is the form that melts near 34 degrees Celsius.

### The crystal forms were found with X-rays and heat flow

{{chain: identifying cocoa butter crystals | X-ray diffraction | differential scanning calorimetry | >six forms with distinct melting ranges}}
You cannot see a crystal form with your eyes, so identifying cocoa butter crystals takes instruments. Two carry most of the weight. The first is X-ray diffraction. A beam of X-rays scattered by a crystal produces a pattern of angles that reflects the spacing between layers of molecules, and each cocoa butter form leaves its own fingerprint in that pattern. The second is differential scanning calorimetry. It warms a tiny sample alongside an empty reference and records how much extra energy the sample absorbs at each temperature. Melting soaks up energy, so each form shows up as a peak at its own position. Put the two together and you get six forms with distinct melting ranges, a result reported in work published through the American Oil Chemists' Society and reproduced in many laboratories since.

{{text: the fat is almost certainly liquid already}}
That finding matters for a stubborn bowl in one specific way. Every one of those forms melts well below the heat of a pan of hot water. If chocolate is sitting in a warm bowl and still holding together as a paste, it is not because the fat has found some form too stable to melt. The fat is almost certainly liquid already. Whatever is holding the bowl rigid is something the fat is carrying.

### A little water locks the sugar, not the fat

{{contrast: seized | is=sugar glued together by a little water | not=fat that has failed to melt}}
Here is where the kitchen comes in. Sucrose, ordinary table sugar, dissolves readily in water, and its entry in the PubChem chemical database, run by the National Institutes of Health, records that high solubility. Now let a little water into melted chocolate, from a wet spoon, a splash, or steam condensing on the side of the bowl. Those few drops wet the surfaces of a great many sugar grains at once. A thin syrup forms wherever grains touch, and it bridges them together, the way damp sand holds a shape that dry sand cannot. The fat is still liquid. But the particles it was lubricating are now stuck in clumps, and the whole mass turns stiff, dull and grainy. That is seizing: sugar glued together by a little water, not fat that has failed to melt.

### More liquid, not less, rescues a seized bowl

{{steps: rescuing seized chocolate | take the bowl off the heat | add warm liquid a spoonful at a time | stir until smooth | >the result is a sauce, not tempered chocolate}}
This is also why turning up the heat fails. Heat can drive off some water, but as it does, the syrup between the grains thickens and grips harder. The fix follows from the same physics, and it runs the other way. A small amount of water makes bridges between grains. A larger amount does something different: it dissolves enough sugar and surrounds enough particles that the mixture becomes a smooth, fluid sauce. That is the same principle behind a ganache, where cream and chocolate are combined on purpose. Rescuing seized chocolate therefore runs in the opposite direction from instinct. First, take the bowl off the heat. Then add warm liquid a spoonful at a time, cream, milk, water or a neutral oil, and stir until smooth after each addition. The paste breaks up and loosens into something glossy. The limit is worth saying plainly: the result is a sauce, not tempered chocolate. It will not set with a clean snap again, but it still works in a batter, a glaze or a drink.

### Scorched chocolate has changed chemically, and it stays changed

{{kitchen: 100 | where water boils}}
The second way chocolate refuses to melt looks similar but has a different cause. Chocolate scorches when parts of it get far hotter than they need to, usually the layer touching a hot pan or the edges of a bowl in a microwave. A water bath beneath the bowl tops out where water boils, 100 degrees Celsius at sea level, which is why it is gentler than direct heat, though its steam is also exactly the stray moisture that seizes chocolate. Milk and white chocolate are the most vulnerable. They contain milk proteins and the milk sugar lactose, and when proteins and sugars are heated together they begin Maillard reactions, the browning chemistry studied extensively in the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry. The result is thick, crumbly, darkened and tasting burnt.

{{checklist: reading the bowl | +stiff and grainy | +burnt smell | -reversible once scorched | ?both at once}}
Reading the bowl is mostly a matter of looking, then smelling. Seized chocolate is stiff and grainy but still smells like chocolate. Scorched chocolate has a burnt smell and often darker flecks through it. Unlike seizing, the change is not reversible once scorched. New compounds have formed, and no amount of added liquid unmakes them. The open case is a bowl showing both at once, seized by steam and then overheated as someone pushed harder to fix it, and for that bowl the only dependable answer is to start again.

### Some bars are built not to flow

{{contrast: compound coating | is=a confection made with vegetable fats other than cocoa butter | not=what may be labelled chocolate}}
There is a third case, and here nothing has gone wrong at all. Some products are simply designed to behave differently. In the United States, federal standards of identity define what may be labelled chocolate, and those standards are built around cocoa butter. Candy melts and many coatings are compound coatings: a confection made with vegetable fats other than cocoa butter. Those fats melt over different ranges and do not form cocoa butter's six crystal forms, so they set without tempering, but they also respond to heat in their own way. Chips are another case. Many are made to hold their shape in a hot oven, and a chip that keeps its outline in a cookie is doing its job. That does not mean it will not melt, only that it flows less. Formulations vary by brand, and labels rarely give figures, so the most general guidance is to read the ingredient list and expect thicker flow.

### Bloom changes the surface, not the melt

{{define: fat bloom | a pale film of fat crystals on the surface | not mould and does not stop melting | Royal Society of Chemistry}}
Old chocolate raises one last worry. A pale, streaky surface makes people think the bar has turned into something that will not melt. That film has a name. Fat bloom is a pale film of fat crystals on the surface, often linked to the slow shift from form five to the higher-melting form six, or to fat migrating outward through the bar. Sugar bloom is different: moisture dissolves surface sugar, which then recrystallises as a dry white dust. The Royal Society of Chemistry's education material uses chocolate as a classroom example of polymorphism. Either way, the boundary matters. Bloom is not mould and does not stop melting. The bar melts normally, and once it is fully melted, its old crystal history is erased.

### Gentle heat and dry tools prevent all of it

{{steps: melting cleanly | dry the bowl and spoon | chop the chocolate evenly | warm gently, stirring | stop while a few pieces remain | >residual heat finishes the job}}
Prevention follows from everything above, and none of it needs a number. Melting cleanly comes down to a few habits. Dry the bowl and spoon completely, because the failure starts with drops. Chop the chocolate evenly, so small shards do not overheat while large chunks are still solid. Warm gently, stirring, over a water bath that does not touch the bowl, or in short bursts in a microwave with a stir between each one. Then stop while a few pieces remain unmelted. Residual heat finishes the job, and stirring those last pieces through keeps any part of the bowl from overshooting. If you want the chocolate to set with a snap afterwards, that is tempering, a separate process with its own temperature curve, and recipes differ on its exact targets.

### Evidence limit

{{text: no published threshold}}
Most of what explains seizing is laboratory physics applied to a kitchen. The calorimetry and diffraction were done on purified cocoa butter and controlled samples, not on your bowl, with its particular brand, its unknown water content and its uneven heat. There is no published threshold for how many drops of water will seize a given amount of chocolate, because the answer depends on the sugar, the particle size and how quickly you stir. The figure of roughly 34 degrees Celsius describes form five in cocoa butter; a real bar, with milk fat or other fats mixed in, softens across a broader range. And the rescue with added liquid is well established in practice, but how much liquid a particular bowl needs is something you find by watching, not by formula.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So a bowl that will not melt is rarely asking for more heat. It is usually telling you that water reached the sugar, that heat reached the milk, or that the bar was never built around cocoa butter to begin with. The fat itself was ready all along, at the temperature of a warm mouth.
