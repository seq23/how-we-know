### Cold open

Most famous photographs of this animal show a pose that lasts an instant in life. A goblin shark is a soft-bodied, pinkish-grey deep-sea shark with a long, flat, blade-like snout projecting over a mouth of slender, needle-like teeth. In a living animal, the jaws sit tucked up beneath that snout. They swing forward only in the moment of a strike, a motion documented from rare video in the journal Scientific Reports, published by Nature. Yet the image most people carry is the opposite: jaws thrust out, teeth bared, frozen in place. That picture is real. It is also, in large part, what happens to the animal after it is hauled up from the dark. So the useful question is not only what the goblin shark looks like, but which of its faces you are actually looking at, and how anyone came to know the difference.

### The shape was first described from bodies, not living animals

{{chain: How the goblin shark entered science | someone catches it | someone preserves it | someone writes down what the body looks like | >a shape known from the dead}}
Almost everything we know about the goblin shark's appearance began with dead animals. The species was described for science in the late nineteenth century by the American ichthyologist David Starr Jordan, working from material obtained off Japan. He gave it the name Mitsukurina owstoni. The genus honours Kakichi Mitsukuri, a Japanese zoologist, and the species name honours Alan Owston, a collector based in Japan who supplied marine specimens to scientists. That is the normal route by which a deep-sea animal enters the record. Someone catches it, someone preserves it, and someone writes down what the body looks like. Each of those steps leaves its own fingerprint on the result, and the goblin shark carries all three.

{{contrast: the common name | is=a translation | not=a description of behaviour}}
The common name arrived as a translation, not as a description of behaviour. In Japanese the animal has long been called tenguzame, after the tengu, a long-nosed goblin figure from Japanese folklore. English speakers carried the idea over as goblin shark. The name points straight at the snout, which is the first thing anyone notices. It does not tell you what the snout is for, and for a long time nobody could watch it being used.

### The long snout is a sensor, not a blade

{{define: ampullae of Lorenzini | jelly-filled pores that sense weak electric fields | they detect prey; they do not strike it | Smithsonian Ocean}}
Start at the front, because that is where your eye goes. The goblin shark's snout is long, flattened from top to bottom, and extends well past the mouth, like the blade of a paddle held out ahead of the face. It looks as if it could cut. It does not appear to be used that way. Its underside is dense with ampullae of Lorenzini, jelly-filled pores that sense weak electric fields. Smithsonian Ocean describes these organs as part of how sharks sense prey they cannot see. In the goblin shark they are spread across an unusually large surface. They detect prey; they do not strike it.

{{steps: Reading a function from a body | examine the pores | compare their density | infer a use | >an inference, not an observation}}
If almost nobody has watched one hunt, that knowledge comes mostly from reading anatomy. Researchers examine the pores on preserved heads, compare their density and arrangement with sharks whose behaviour is better studied, and infer a use. In the deep, where eyes gather very little light, a broad electrosensory surface would let an animal pick up the faint fields produced by living prey hidden in the dark or buried in sediment. That reasoning is strong, and it fits everything else about the animal. It is still an inference, not an observation, and it is worth keeping the two apart as you hear the rest of this.

### The pink colour is blood showing through

{{contrast: goblin shark colour | is=blood vessels beneath it show through | not=a pigment laid down in the skin}}
The colour is the second surprise. A fresh goblin shark is usually described as pinkish, sometimes pinkish-grey or pinkish-white, which is not a colour most people expect of a predator. The explanation offered in descriptions of the species is that the skin is thin and somewhat translucent, so blood vessels beneath it show through. The pink is closer to a blush than to paint. It is not chiefly a pigment laid down in the skin, the way markings are laid down on many shallow-water sharks.

{{light}}
That has a consequence for what you see in pictures. After death and preservation, specimens tend to fade toward brown or dull grey, because the blood is no longer moving and preservatives change the tissue. So a museum jar and a fresh catch can seem to show two quite different animals. And in its own habitat, the colour may barely matter. Red light is absorbed quickly in seawater, long before it reaches the depths where this shark lives. With no red light arriving, a pinkish animal would reflect almost nothing, and in dim water it would look dark, close to invisible. The colour you find striking is, in a sense, a colour the deep never sees.

### The jaws are built to leave the head

{{chain: The slingshot strike | lie tucked close under the face | the jaws thrust forward | then retract | >gripping slippery prey}}
Now to the feature that made the goblin shark famous. Beneath the snout, the jaws are attached in a way that lets them be thrown forward, far out from the head, and then pulled back. In a resting animal they lie tucked close under the face, and the mouth looks fairly modest. During a strike, the jaws thrust forward to meet the prey, then retract. The front teeth are long and slender, closer to nails or needles than to the broad, serrated teeth of a great white. Teeth like that suit gripping slippery prey rather than slicing pieces from it. Stomach contents from examined specimens have included fishes, squids and crustaceans, which fits that kind of grip.

{{checklist: What the video settled | +the jaws move forward during feeding | +the strike is fast | ?how often it hunts this way | -what normal daily behaviour looks like}}
For a long time this mechanism was pieced together from dead specimens, whose jaws were often found pushed outward. The strongest evidence came from rare video of living goblin sharks feeding in waters off Japan, analysed in a paper in Scientific Reports. Studied frame by frame, the footage showed that the jaws move forward during feeding, and that the strike is fast. The researchers called it slingshot feeding. What the video could not show is how often it hunts this way in its own habitat, or what normal daily behaviour looks like at depth. A small number of filmed strikes is a window, not a census.

### The body is built for hovering, not chasing

{{contrast: goblin shark body | is=soft and flabby | not=a torpedo}}
Behind the head, the rest of the animal is easy to overlook, and it says a good deal. The body is soft and flabby rather than firm, with relatively small, rounded fins. The tail is long, with a dominant upper lobe and little or no lower lobe, so it trails behind more than it drives. This is not a torpedo. Sharks that chase prey at speed tend to be firm, muscular and sharply finned. The goblin shark's build points to an animal that moves slowly and spends energy sparingly, which fits life in deep water, where food is scarce and widely spread out.

{{text}}
That step is, again, an inference from shape. Biologists read body form the way an engineer reads a hull, and this reading is consistent with what is known of other deep-water sharks. Deep-water sharks often carry a large, oily liver that helps with buoyancy, and the goblin shark is described as having one too. A liver like that would let an animal hang in the water with little effort, waiting rather than pursuing. Whether the goblin shark spends most of its time hovering, cruising slowly over the seafloor, or doing something else entirely has not been watched directly over long periods.

### Where it lives is known only in outline

{{zones: twilight zone}}
Setting shapes how you should picture this animal. Goblin sharks are known mainly from deep water along continental slopes and near undersea features, with records scattered across several ocean basins and many from waters off Japan. Most of those records are animals caught incidentally in deep fishing gear. That means the map of where they live is partly a map of where people fish. Depths reported for the species run from the twilight zone, where faint sunlight still filters down, into the midnight zone below it, where none does. Published depth ranges differ, and this episode will not give you a single number, because the sources do not agree on one.

{{sources: Who the evidence comes from | NOAA Ocean Exploration=the layers of the deep | Smithsonian Ocean=how sharks sense prey | Nature=slingshot feeding on video}}
Put together, the picture rests on different kinds of source. NOAA Ocean Exploration describes the layers of the deep, from the twilight zone, where light fades but does not vanish, to the midnight zone beneath it. Smithsonian Ocean explains how sharks sense prey through the ampullae of Lorenzini. Nature's journal Scientific Reports carried the paper that documented slingshot feeding on video. Each answers a different part of the question. None of them alone gives you the whole animal, and none of them followed a single goblin shark through its life.

### What to notice in the edit

{{text}}
When you see goblin shark images in this episode, or anywhere else, check three things before you trust the face. Check whether the animal is alive or dead. Check whether the jaws are tucked or thrust out. And check whether the colour is fresh pink or the faded grey of preservation. Most still images are of caught or dead animals, and many show the jaws pushed forward, a posture that in a living shark lasts only an instant. The rare footage of live animals is the better guide to the resting face, which is quieter, smoother and far less monstrous than its reputation.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence leaves open | +shape is well documented | +the jaws move forward during feeding | ?how large the species can grow | -how many goblin sharks there are}}
Here is what all this does not establish. The goblin shark's shape is well documented, but that documentation leans heavily on animals that were dying or dead when examined, on bycatch from particular fisheries, and on a small number of filmed strikes. It does not tell us how many goblin sharks there are, how long they live, or how they behave across a normal day at depth. It does not settle how large the species can grow; size estimates vary, and the biggest figures rest on very few animals. And the work of the snout, while strongly supported by anatomy, has not been watched in action in the deep. The face is known. The life behind it is still mostly unseen.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So the goblin shark looks like this: pale pink and soft, a flat blade of a snout lined with sensors, a modest mouth held close, and a long tail trailing behind. The snapping, jaw-thrust face is real too. It just belongs to one fast moment in the dark, and to the deck of a boat.
