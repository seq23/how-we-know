## YouTube title

Meat loses juice long before boiling

## Direct-answer lock

When you cook meat, heat unfolds its muscle proteins, which then tighten and squeeze out water, while connective collagen shrinks and, with long moist heat, dissolves into gelatin. The red pigment myoglobin denatures to a dull gray-brown, and at a hot, dry surface the Maillard reaction builds a browned crust and aroma. Each change happens in its own temperature window, so cooking is a sequence of separate events rather than a single switch.

### Heat changes shape before it changes chemistry

{{cross_section: raw muscle | fibers, myosin and actin, collagen, myoglobin}}
Start with what raw muscle is. It is mostly water, held in bundles of long cells called fibers. Inside each fiber, two proteins, myosin and actin, are arranged in overlapping filaments that slide past each other when a living muscle contracts. Around the fibers runs a sheath of connective tissue built mostly from collagen. Dissolved in the cell fluid is myoglobin, the protein that stores oxygen and gives raw meat its red. Cooking is the story of what heat does to each of these, one after another.

{{define: denaturation | a protein unfolds as heat breaks its weak bonds | it does not break the chain into pieces | Royal Society of Chemistry}}
Proteins hold their shapes with weak bonds, hydrogen bonds and other attractions that fold a long chain into a working structure. Heat makes the chain vibrate until those bonds let go, and the protein unfolds. That is denaturation: a protein unfolds as heat breaks its weak bonds. On its own, it does not break the chain into pieces. What follows matters more. Unfolded proteins expose sticky regions that were buried inside, and they latch on to their neighbors. Strands that were separate become a tangled, tighter network. Nothing has been added, and very little has been destroyed. The meat has been rearranged.

### A calorimeter records each protein giving way

{{steps: differential scanning calorimeter | warms a tiny sample of muscle | an empty reference side by side | records how much extra energy | >every time a protein population gives way, the trace shows a bump}}
So how do you know which protein goes first? You do not watch it. You measure heat. The instrument is a differential scanning calorimeter. It warms a tiny sample of muscle and an empty reference side by side at a steady rate, and it records how much extra energy the sample needs to keep pace. Unfolding a protein absorbs energy. So every time a protein population gives way, the trace shows a bump, a peak of heat flowing in.

{{contrast: peak order | is=very stable | not=fixed constants}}
For muscle, food chemists repeatedly report three main peaks. The first is attributed to myosin, in roughly the mid-fifties Celsius. A middle peak is linked to collagen and to the dissolved proteins of the cell fluid. The last, attributed to actin, sits near eighty degrees Celsius. Those positions are not fixed constants. Published values shift by several degrees with the species, the cut, the acidity of the muscle and how fast the instrument heats. The order, however, is very stable. The calorimeter gives you a reliable sequence, and only an approximate degree for each step.

### Tightening fibers wring out the water

{{chain: what you can see on the plate | myosin and then actin unfold and bond | the fibers contract | Water that was held between the filaments has less room | >the juice that pools on the board}}
That sequence explains the timing of the next change, the one you can see on the plate. As myosin and then actin unfold and bond, the fibers contract, both across their width and along their length. Water that was held between the filaments has less room. The tightening network pushes it out, the way a fist wrings a wet cloth. That expelled liquid is the juice that pools on the board, and it carries dissolved proteins, salts and flavor with it.

{{define: cooking loss | the difference between raw and cooked weight | no single percentage describes meat in general | USDA Agricultural Research Service}}
This is measured with nothing more exotic than a balance. Meat scientists, including those at the USDA Agricultural Research Service, weigh a sample raw, cook it to a set internal temperature read by a probe, blot it and weigh it again. The difference between raw and cooked weight is called cooking loss. Across many such studies the pattern is consistent. Loss is modest at lower internal temperatures and rises steeply once actin begins to denature. The size of the loss varies widely between cuts and methods, so no single percentage describes meat in general. What holds is the direction and the timing, and both line up with the calorimeter's peaks.

{{text: fibers shrunken in diameter}}
Microscopes confirm the mechanism rather than just the outcome. Electron micrographs of cooked muscle show fibers shrunken in diameter and the spaces between them widened. That empty space is where the water was. The balance tells you how much left. The microscope shows you the route it took.

### Collagen shrinks quickly and dissolves only with time

{{text: three protein chains wound together}}
Connective tissue runs on a different clock. Collagen is a triple helix, three protein chains wound together and cross-linked, and it is strong because of that winding. When it is heated into the sixties Celsius, the helix loses its order and the sheath around the fibers shrinks sharply. That shrinkage squeezes the meat further, which is one reason a quickly cooked piece can tighten and curl in the pan.

{{contrast: tender | is=the collagen around them dissolves | not=its muscle fibers soften}}
But hold collagen hot and wet for long enough and something else happens. The cross-links and the helix come apart for good, and the chains dissolve into the surrounding liquid as gelatin. This conversion is slow. It takes hours rather than minutes, and it depends on moisture, which is why tough, connective-rich cuts are braised or stewed. Here the measurements correct an easy assumption. A tough cut becomes tender not because its muscle fibers soften, since they keep drying, but because the collagen around them dissolves. Your fork registers the sum of two opposite trends.

### Color change is a protein measurement too

{{checklist: myoglobin | +With oxygen attached, it is bright red | +Without oxygen, purplish | +When the iron is oxidized, brown | +the denatured pigment is a dull gray-brown}}
Myoglobin holds an iron atom at its center, and the color of meat depends on what that iron is bound to and how it is charged. With oxygen attached, it is bright red. Without oxygen, purplish. When the iron is oxidized, brown. Heat adds another state. It unfolds the globin protein wrapped around the iron, and the denatured pigment is a dull gray-brown that does not turn back to red.

{{chain: reflectance spectrophotometers | shine light on the cut surface | record which wavelengths come back | >Color reports on myoglobin, not on the whole piece}}
You can measure this rather than judge it by eye. Colorimeters and reflectance spectrophotometers shine light on the cut surface and record which wavelengths come back, and each form of myoglobin has its own signature. The reading tracks how much of the pigment has been denatured. Yet color is the least direct guide to what has happened elsewhere in the meat. Acidity, storage and how much oxygen reached the cut all shift the color at a given temperature. Researchers have documented meat that browns early and meat that stays pink after reaching higher temperatures. Color reports on myoglobin, not on the whole piece.

### Browning waits for the surface to dry

{{timeline: 1912=Louis-Camille Maillard | 1953=John Hodge}}
Now the surface. In 1912, the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard reported to the French Academy of Sciences that heating amino acids with sugars produced brown compounds. The reaction now carries his name. In 1953, the chemist John Hodge published a scheme organizing its steps, in a journal published by the American Chemical Society. What his scheme made clear is that this is not one reaction but a large network of them. A sugar and an amino group join, rearrange, fragment and recombine, and the products include the brown pigments of a crust and a great many aroma compounds.

{{kitchen: 100 | Maillard}}
Here the cold open's figure does its work. A wet surface is held near 100 degrees Celsius, because the incoming energy goes into evaporating water rather than raising temperature. The Maillard network runs slowly at that level. It accelerates once the surface dries and its temperature climbs well past the boiling point. That is the physical reason a wet piece of meat steams before it sears, and why a crust forms on the outside while the inside is still far cooler. Probes inserted at different depths map that steep gradient directly.

{{sources: Who recorded the crust | French Academy of Sciences=heating amino acids with sugars produced brown compounds | PubChem=Structures for many of them}}
How do chemists know what the crust is made of? Gas chromatography separates the vapors driven off a cooked sample, and mass spectrometry identifies each one by the way it breaks into fragments. Those methods have catalogued pyrazines, which carry roasted notes, along with sulfur-containing compounds and products of fat breaking down in the heat. Structures for many of them are recorded in public chemistry databases such as PubChem. Which compounds dominate a particular aroma is still debated, because the amount of a compound and the strength of its smell are not the same thing.

### Fat melts across a range, not at a point

{{contrast: fat melting | is=a broad range | not=a single temperature}}
Fat follows physics more than chemistry. Animal fat is a mixture of many different triglycerides, each with its own melting behavior, so it does not melt at a single temperature. It softens and liquefies across a broad range, which a calorimeter records as a wide smear rather than a sharp peak. As it melts, it renders out of the tissue, some lost to the pan and some coating the fibers. At the hottest surfaces, fats also break down and oxidize, adding their own volatile products to the aroma. The American Oil Chemists' Society publishes standard methods that laboratories use to characterize fats like these.

### What to notice in the edit

{{steps: different kind of measurement | Heat flow showed the order of unfolding | A balance showed where the water went | Reflected light tracked the pigment | Separated vapors named the aroma}}
As you watch the sequence back, notice that each change on screen was established by a different kind of measurement. Heat flow showed the order of unfolding. A balance showed where the water went. Reflected light tracked the pigment. Separated vapors named the aroma. None of these instruments sees the whole steak. Each one isolates a single process, and the picture of cooked meat is assembled from where they overlap.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: what is settled | +The mechanisms are well established | -a single temperature table that holds for every piece of meat | ?The precise numbers belong to the samples}}
What this evidence does not give you is a single temperature table that holds for every piece of meat. The calorimeter peaks move with species, age, acidity and heating rate. Cooking loss depends on the cut, its shape and how quickly heat reaches the center. Color can mislead in either direction. Most of the cleanest measurements come from small, uniform laboratory samples heated evenly, while a real roast holds a hot rim, a cool core and every stage in between at once. The mechanisms are well established. The precise numbers belong to the samples that produced them.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
Cooking meat, then, is not one event. It is a series of proteins letting go, each at its own point, while water leaves, collagen tightens or dissolves, and the surface waits to dry before it browns. The heat is simple. What it undoes is not.
