## Direct-answer lock

Blobfish look weird because nearly every picture you have seen shows one out of the water, after a trawl from the deep, its soft body slumped and damaged. In its own habitat, water supports a gelatinous body that is close to the density of seawater, so the fish keeps an ordinary, big-headed outline and hovers near the seafloor using very little energy. The drooping face is what happens when that support is removed, and when a net scrapes away the skin.

### The famous face is a collapse, not a design

{{text}}
Start with what the animal is. The name blobfish usually refers to Psychrolutes marcidus, a fathead sculpin that lives in deep water off southeastern Australia and New Zealand. Sculpins are a large group of mostly bottom-living fish, and many of them have broad heads, wide mouths and tapering bodies. Nothing about that outline is strange. If you drew an ordinary sculpin and then softened its edges, you would be close to what this fish looks like at home.

{{contrast: the famous face | is=a body slumped in air | not=the living shape of the fish}}
The face in the famous photo is something else. The nose-like flap is not a nose. It is loose skin and soft tissue from the head, sagging forward and down because nothing is holding it up. The downturned mouth is the same tissue pulled by gravity. The pink, glossy surface is partly the result of skin being scraped away in a net. So the blobfish face only exists in air. It is the shape a living body takes once it has lost the medium that was supporting it, which means the photo shows a body slumped in air, not the living shape of the fish.

### Water holds up a body that air cannot

{{chain: Why the body slumps | flesh close to the density of seawater | water carries the weight | air carries almost none | >gravity pulls soft tissue down}}
To see why, think about density. The flesh of this fish is close to the density of seawater. In the sea, that matters enormously. The water carries the weight of the body, so the tissue does not need much internal structure to keep its shape. Lift the same fish into the air, and air carries almost none of that weight. All of the soft tissue now hangs from whatever skeleton and muscle lies underneath, and in this fish there is not much of either. So gravity pulls soft tissue down, and the animal spreads out like something set down on a table.

{{text}}
You have probably seen a gentler version of this yourself. A jellyfish in the sea is a bell with a clear shape. The same jellyfish on a beach is a puddle. Nothing about its anatomy changed between the two. The only thing that changed is what was supporting it. A blobfish is not as watery as a jellyfish, but the principle is the same, and it explains most of the strangeness in a single step.

{{pressure: 1000}}
Depth adds a second factor. NOAA explains the rule that governs it: for every ten metres you descend in seawater, pressure rises by roughly one atmosphere. At the surface you are already under one atmosphere from the air above you. At 1000 metres the water adds about a hundred more, for a total of roughly 101 atmospheres. Blobfish have been caught from depths in this general range, though the published figures vary between sources. Deep-living animals are built for that load. Their tissues are mostly water, and water barely compresses, so the pressure itself does not crush them. The trouble comes when the pressure is taken away quickly, during a haul to the surface the body was never built to survive.

### A fish that looks inert is doing careful work

{{ambient}}
A blobfish has the same problem as anything people call passive. Resting on or just above the seafloor, it can look as if it is doing nothing at all. In fact its whole body is an answer to a hard question: how to stay at a chosen height in the water, in the cold and the dark, while spending as little energy as possible. The softness you laugh at in the photo is the answer.

### Gelatinous flesh is a solution, not a defect

{{anatomy: Blobfish at depth | large head@0.3,0.45 | gelatinous flesh@0.55,0.5 | loose skin@0.4,0.3 | tapering tail@0.85,0.5}}
Many fish control their depth with a swim bladder, a gas-filled organ they can inflate or deflate. Gas is costly to manage at depth, because the deeper you go, the more it is squeezed, and the more work it takes to keep that bladder filled. Like many deep-water fish, the blobfish does not rely on a gas-filled swim bladder. Instead, its buoyancy comes largely from its body. Its gelatinous flesh is slightly less dense than the water around it, so the fish can hover just above the seafloor without constantly swimming. Picture a large head, loose skin over soft tissue, and a tapering tail. That is the working animal.

{{checklist: What gelatinous flesh buys | +buoyancy without a gas-filled swim bladder | +little energy spent holding position | -strength for fast swimming | ?exactly how it feeds in the wild}}
There is a trade in this design. The fish gets buoyancy without a gas-filled swim bladder, and very little energy spent holding position. What it gives up is muscle, and with it the strength for fast swimming. That suits an animal thought to wait near the bottom and take small animals that pass close to its mouth. I say thought to, because exactly how it feeds in the wild has rarely, if ever, been watched directly. Most of what is said about its diet is inferred from trawled specimens and from related fish, rather than observed.

{{text}}
The setting explains why saving energy matters so much. The Smithsonian's ocean pages describe the deep sea as cold, dark and short of food, with much of what is eaten down there sinking from the waters above. In a place like that, an animal that burns little energy just to stay where it is has an advantage. A firm, muscular body would be stronger. It would also be more expensive to run.

### Pressure is the second injury, and the net is the third

{{steps: What a trawl haul does | time in a net among other animals and debris | scraping that strips skin | a rapid drop in pressure and rise in temperature | >the body arrives already damaged}}
Now follow a specimen up. Blobfish are usually brought up as bycatch in deep-water trawls, nets dragged near the bottom for other species. First comes time in a net among other animals and debris. Then comes scraping that strips skin, because the skin is thin and loose and tears easily. Then comes a rapid drop in pressure and rise in temperature, as the net climbs through water that is warmer and far less pressurised than home. By the time it reaches the deck, the body arrives already damaged.

{{contrast: decompression | is=damage from pressure falling too fast | not=the main cause of the slump}}
It is worth being careful about which injury does what. Popular explanations sometimes say the fish explodes or melts on the way up. That overstates it. Decompression is damage from pressure falling too fast, and it can harm tissue, especially in animals that carry gas inside them. But for a soft, low-muscle fish without a gas-filled swim bladder, decompression is likely not the main cause of the slump. The larger factor is the loss of support from water, made worse by skin damage from the net. Air and gravity do most of the reshaping.

### Living images tell a different story

{{sources: Who shows the living deep | NOAA=pressure at depth | MBARI=video of deep animals in place | Smithsonian=the deep sea as a habitat}}
So how does anyone know what a blobfish looks like alive? Mostly by taking cameras down to the animal instead of bringing the animal up. Remotely operated vehicles and towed cameras now film the deep seafloor routinely. Institutions such as MBARI have built large archives of video of deep animals in place, and that footage shows again and again how different soft-bodied animals look in their own water. NOAA explains pressure at depth, and the Smithsonian describes the deep sea as a habitat. Together they explain the pattern, even where a particular species has rarely been filmed.

{{text}}
Images of living blobfish and their close relatives do exist, and they show what the physics predicts: a pale, pinkish fish with a large head, a recognisable mouth, fins, and a body that holds its outline. It looks soft, and a little loose around the edges, but it looks like a fish. Set the trawled photo beside one of those images and you are looking at the same kind of animal in two states, one supported and one not.

### Ugly is a statement about where we stand

{{ambient}}
This is why the blobfish became famous for the wrong reason. A photograph of a trawled specimen spread widely online, and the fish was later voted the world's ugliest animal in a public poll. The vote was light-hearted, and it drew attention to deep-sea animals that rarely get any. But it was judging a damaged body in air. It was a little like judging a jellyfish by the puddle on the beach.

{{text}}
There is a broader lesson for how you read any deep-sea image. Almost every animal in the deep is adapted to conditions you never experience: cold, darkness, little food, and pressure many times what you feel at the surface. Photograph one on a ship's deck and you are photographing it outside every one of those conditions at once. The picture is accurate about the specimen and misleading about the species.

### What to notice in the edit

{{contrast: the trawled photo | is=a record of the specimen | not=a portrait of the species}}
When the two images sit side by side, look at the head first. In the trawled photo, the head has no edge; it pours forward over the mouth. In the living image, the head has a boundary, and the mouth sits where a fish's mouth should. Then look at the skin. Where it is glossy and raw, the net has been at work. The trawled photo is a record of the specimen, honest about what happened to one fish. It is not a portrait of the species.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +why the trawled body slumps | -how the living fish behaves day to day | ?how many blobfish there are}}
What this evidence settles is narrow: why the trawled body slumps, through support lost and skin stripped. It does not tell you how the living fish behaves day to day, because very few individuals have ever been watched in place for long. Its published depth range differs between sources. Its diet is mostly inferred. How long it lives, how it breeds and how many blobfish there are remain open, and the honest answer to each is that the observations are too thin to say. Knowing why a photo misleads is not the same as knowing the animal.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
The face that made this fish famous is the face of a fish that has left its world. Down where it lives, held in cold water under great pressure, it is simply a soft fish doing a quiet, efficient job. The strangeness was in where we stood to look.
