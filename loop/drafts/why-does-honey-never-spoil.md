## YouTube title

Honey is not immortal by nature

## Direct-answer lock

Honey resists spoiling because it holds so little water, and so much sugar, that microbes cannot draw enough water to grow. Its acidity, and the hydrogen peroxide made by an enzyme the bees add, raise further barriers. It is not truly immortal: honey that takes up moisture can ferment, and stored honey slowly darkens and changes flavour.

And it overturns a familiar assumption. Honey is not immortal by nature. It stays protected only as long as it stays dry.

### Microbes starve for water, not for food

{{define: water activity | how much of the water in a food is free to move and react | pure water sits at 1, a completely dry material sits near 0 | American Chemical Society}}
To a bacterium, a jar of honey looks like an enormous meal. It is almost entirely sugar, the fuel most microbes prefer. The problem is not food. The problem is water. Food scientists measure that with a quantity called water activity, the term now on screen. Food chemists, in research published by the American Chemical Society, treat it as the water in a food that is free to move and take part in reactions, rather than the total amount present. The scale is simple. Pure water sits at 1, and a completely dry material sits near 0. Every microbe has a floor on that scale below which it cannot grow. Most bacteria need conditions close to the wet end. Some molds and specialised yeasts can manage drier ground, but each still has a limit.

{{contrast: water content | is=how much water is present | not=how much water is available}}
Here is the distinction that matters, and the screen splits it in two. Water content is how much water is present. Water activity is how much of that water is actually available. Honey contains a fair amount of water by weight. But nearly all of it is held tightly by sugar molecules, which bind to water through hydrogen bonds. A water molecule clinging to a sugar is not free to cross into a cell. So a food can be wet to the touch and still be a desert to a microbe. Honey is exactly that kind of desert. Its water activity sits well below the line most bacteria need, although the published values vary from sample to sample and from one floral source to another.

{{chain: measuring water activity | sealed chamber | dew point | >water activity}}
How is that known, rather than assumed? On screen is the instrument chain. A small sample of honey goes into a sealed chamber. Water escapes from the sample into the air above it until the two reach balance. The instrument then measures the humidity of that trapped air, commonly by cooling a tiny mirror until dew forms on it. The temperature of that dew point tells you how much water vapour is present, and from that the device reports water activity. Total moisture, the figure in the grade standards, is usually measured a different way. A refractometer reads how strongly the honey bends light, and because sugar and water bend light differently, that reading converts to moisture. Two instruments, two different questions, one consistent picture.

### Bees do the drying before you ever see the jar

{{steps: from nectar to honey | collect nectar | add enzymes | fan to evaporate | cap with wax | >moisture falls before sealing}}
That dryness is manufactured, and the screen walks through the stages. Worker bees collect nectar, which is far wetter than finished honey. Back in the hive, they add enzymes from their own glands. One of them, invertase, splits sucrose into its two simpler sugars, glucose and fructose. Then the drying begins. Bees spread the liquid thinly through the comb and fan their wings, driving a current of warm air across the cells. Water evaporates. Only when the honey has thickened enough do the bees cap each cell with wax. The moisture falls before the seal goes on, not after.

{{text: The wax cap is a sign the drying is done}}
Beekeepers read that sign. A frame with most of its cells capped is usually ripe enough to harvest, and many beekeepers confirm it with a handheld refractometer before extracting. Honey taken too early, from open cells, can carry too much water. That is exactly the honey most likely to go wrong later. So the stability you find in a jar is partly the work of the colony and partly the judgement of whoever harvested it. Nothing about the sugar alone guarantees it. The process does.

### Sugar pulls water out of a living cell

{{cross_section: a bacterial cell in honey | water drawn outward through the membrane}}
Now follow a single microbe into the jar. The glowing cross-section shows a bacterial cell with a thin membrane around it. Inside, the cell's contents are watery. Outside, the honey is a concentrated sugar solution. Water moves across a membrane from the side where it is more available toward the side where it is less available. This is osmosis. So instead of the cell drawing water in, water is drawn outward through the membrane, into the honey. The cell shrinks. Its internal machinery, which depends on water to function, slows and stops. Many cells simply cannot recover.

{{contrast: osmotic stress | is=water leaving the cell | not=a poison}}
It is worth being exact about what is doing the work here, and the screen holds both sides. Osmotic stress is water leaving the cell. It is not a poison. Glucose and fructose, whose structures are catalogued by PubChem, are ordinary sugars that cells use for energy every day. Diluted, they would feed the very organisms they now suppress. That is why the same substance can be a preservative at one concentration and a growth medium at another. The protection is a property of the mixture, not of any single molecule in it.

### Acid and peroxide add a second wall

{{chain: glucose oxidase | glucose | gluconic acid | hydrogen peroxide | >a second barrier}}
Dryness is the main defence, but it is not the only one, and the chain on screen shows the next. Bees add an enzyme called glucose oxidase. It acts on glucose and turns it into gluconic acid, releasing hydrogen peroxide as a by-product. Gluconic acid is the main acid in honey, and it is a large part of why honey is acidic. Many bacteria grow poorly in acidic conditions. Hydrogen peroxide, also catalogued by PubChem, is reactive and damages cells. Together they form a second barrier behind the first.

{{sources: composition of honey | USDA Agricultural Research Service=surveys of honey composition | PubChem=structures of gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide}}
There is a subtlety in that chemistry, and it comes from careful measurement rather than intuition. Composition surveys from the USDA's Agricultural Research Service, and laboratory work since, found that glucose oxidase is fairly quiet in full-strength honey. It works much more actively once honey is diluted. That means the peroxide defence is strongest at precisely the moment the dryness defence weakens. It is a fallback, not a front line. The acidity, meanwhile, is real but variable. Published values differ across floral sources, so no single number describes every honey.

### The protection fails where water returns

{{checklist: what keeps honey stable | +low water | +concentrated sugar | +acidity | -protection against absorbed moisture | ?how long flavour lasts}}
The checklist on screen sorts what honey has from what it lacks. It has low water. It has concentrated sugar. It has acidity. What it does not have is protection against absorbed moisture. Honey is hygroscopic, meaning it pulls water vapour out of the air. Leave a jar open in a humid kitchen and the surface layer slowly takes on water. The sugar near the top is diluted, and the water activity there rises.

{{text: Fermentation is how honey spoils}}
That is the failure point. Some yeasts are specialists in sugary, dry environments, and they are often already present in honey in small numbers, dormant. Give them a slightly wetter layer and they can wake up. They ferment the sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. The honey can foam, smell sour, and taste of alcohol. This is the same basic chemistry brewers use to make mead on purpose. So the honest answer to whether honey never spoils is that honey spoils readily, by fermentation, once its water rises. The claim of permanence is really a claim about a sealed, dry jar.

### Crystals and darkening are change, not decay

{{contrast: crystallization | is=glucose coming out of solution | not=spoilage}}
Many people throw out honey that is not spoiled at all. The screen shows why. Over time, a lot of honey turns cloudy and grainy. This is crystallization, glucose coming out of solution as solid crystals. Glucose dissolves less readily than fructose, so honeys richer in glucose tend to crystallize sooner. It is not spoilage. The sugar is the same sugar, arranged differently. There is one catch worth knowing. As glucose locks into crystals, the remaining liquid holds a little more of the water, which can raise the fermentation risk in that liquid portion.

{{text: Change is not the same as spoilage}}
Stored honey also changes chemically, even sealed and dry. Its colour gradually darkens and its flavour shifts, especially when it is warmed. One of the compounds that builds up is hydroxymethylfurfural, a breakdown product of sugars in acidic conditions, also catalogued by PubChem. Laboratories track it with liquid chromatography, which separates the compounds in a sample so each can be measured. Rising levels indicate that honey has been heated or stored for a long time. That makes it a quality marker, not proof of microbial decay. A jar can be old, darker, and stronger tasting, and still be free of anything growing in it.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: what the evidence settles | +why microbes cannot grow in dry honey | +how honey ferments when wet | -how long any particular jar stays unchanged | -the age claims in popular stories}}
Here is what this evidence does not establish. It explains why microbes cannot grow in dry honey, and how honey ferments when wet. It does not tell you how long any particular jar will stay unchanged, because moisture, floral source, storage temperature and handling all vary. It also does not settle the popular stories of edible honey recovered from ancient tombs, repeated widely, including in a Smithsonian feature on honey's shelf life. Those accounts are mostly anecdotal, and the published record of laboratory analysis on such samples is thin. And nothing here is a statement about what is safe to eat. It describes chemistry in a jar.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So the honey on your shelf is not immortal. It is an engineered scarcity, a liquid made so short of free water that life cannot get a grip on it. Bees build that condition, and a sealed lid keeps it. Take the water back, and the oldest preservation trick in the kitchen quietly undoes itself.
