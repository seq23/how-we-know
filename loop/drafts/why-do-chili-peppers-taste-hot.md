## Direct-answer lock

### Heat is a nerve signal, not a taste

{{text: where the burn is not}}
Start with where the burn is not. Taste buds carry receptors for a small set of tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and savoury. Heat is not on that list, and the evidence for that needs no instrument. Chili juice on the skin of a hand, or near an eye, produces the same burn in tissue that has no taste buds at all. Whatever capsaicin is doing, it is not doing it through taste. That simple observation was the first clue, and it pointed researchers away from the tongue's taste machinery entirely.

{{contrast: chili heat | is=chemesthesis | not=taste}}
So the sensation travels by a different route. In the mouth, the burn is carried largely by the trigeminal nerve, the same nerve that reports touch, temperature and pain across the face. Researchers call this family of sensations chemesthesis: chemicals triggering senses that normally respond to physical events. The sting of mustard and the cool of mint belong here too. Chili heat is chemesthesis, not taste, and that distinction told researchers where to look. The answer would be found on a nerve ending, not inside a taste bud.

### The receptor was found by fishing with the molecule itself

{{steps: Fishing for the receptor | a library of genes | cells that normally ignore capsaicin | watched for calcium | >until one gene remains}}
For decades, physiologists knew capsaicin excited a particular class of pain-sensing neurons, but nobody knew what it actually bound to. The laboratory of David Julius approached the problem by fishing with the molecule itself. The team built a library of genes active in sensory neurons, then put pools of those genes into cells that normally ignore capsaicin. They added capsaicin and watched for calcium flooding into the cells, made visible by a dye that glows when calcium rises. A pool that lit up contained the gene they wanted. Split the pool, test again, and keep going until one gene remains.

{{define: TRPV1 | an ion channel | it does not respond to cold | Nature}}
The gene they isolated encoded an ion channel, a pore in the nerve cell's membrane that opens to let charged particles through. They called it the vanilloid receptor, after a chemical group capsaicin shares with vanillin, the main flavour molecule of vanilla. Today it is known as TRPV1. The paper, published in Nature in 1997, reported something that turned the whole question around. The channel did not only open for capsaicin. It also opened for heat.

### The nerve cannot tell the molecule from real heat

{{chain: Why the burn feels like heat | capsaicin binds TRPV1 | the channel opens | the nerve fires | >the brain reports heat}}
In the same experiments, cells carrying TRPV1 responded when the surrounding solution was warmed above about 43 degrees Celsius, a temperature at which contact begins to register as painful. That is the load-bearing fact. A nerve ending has one way to signal: it fires or it stays quiet. When capsaicin binds TRPV1, the channel opens, the nerve fires, and the brain receives the same message a scald would send. It has no separate line that says this came from a molecule. So the brain reports heat. Nothing in a chili warms your mouth, and no thermometer would ever show the burn.

{{checklist: What TRPV1 responds to | +capsaicin | +heat | +acidity | -cold}}
Later work filled in the receptor's other sensitivities. Alongside capsaicin and heat, TRPV1 also responds to acidity. It does not respond to cold; the cooling of menthol works through a different channel. And the receptor sits mostly on pain-sensing nerves, which is why the chili sensation arrives as a burn rather than a pleasant warmth. Capsaicin also explains the lingering. It is an oily molecule that dissolves poorly in water, so it clings to tissue and keeps the channel busy after the food is gone.

{{chain: The knockout test | lacked the TRPV1 gene | offered capsaicin water | drank it as if it were plain water | >a mechanism}}
Identifying a receptor is not the same as proving it is the one that matters. The decisive test came from mice, in work published in the journal Science in 2000. Researchers bred animals that lacked the TRPV1 gene and offered them capsaicin water, ordinary drinking water laced with the molecule. Normal mice avoid it. The mice without the receptor drank it as if it were plain water. Their response to high heat was also dulled, though not erased, which hinted that other heat sensors work alongside TRPV1. That combination, a molecule that opens a channel in a dish and an animal that stops caring once the channel is gone, is what turns a candidate into a mechanism.

### The plant puts the molecule exactly where it does the most work

{{cross_section: chili fruit | the placenta}}
A pepper plant fits that description. It is not a passive bowl of flavour; it is running chemistry. Capsaicin is made in the placenta, the pale tissue inside the fruit that holds the seeds. The seeds themselves make little of it, but they sit pressed against the placenta and pick it up, which is why the pith carries more heat than the outer flesh. Capsaicin is the best known of a small family of related compounds called capsaicinoids. PubChem, the public chemical database maintained by the National Institutes of Health, records its makeup: eighteen carbon atoms, twenty-seven hydrogens, one nitrogen and three oxygens.

{{contrast: bell pepper | is=zero heat | not=a mild chili}}
Bell peppers belong to the same species as many hot chilies, yet a bell pepper registers zero heat. It is not a mild chili. It does not make capsaicin at all. That single difference, a working pathway or a broken one, shows that heat is a property of the plant's chemistry, not of the species name, the shape of the fruit or its colour. Flavour and heat are separate traits, and a plant can keep one while losing the other.

### Birds carry the seeds because the alarm stays silent for them

{{text: directed deterrence}}
The obvious next question is why a plant would spend resources making this molecule. The leading explanation is called directed deterrence, and it comes from feeding studies, including one published in Nature, that watched which animals ate wild chilies. Rodents avoided the fruit. Birds ate it freely. That split matters because the two groups treat seeds differently. Rodents chew and digest seeds, destroying them. Birds tend to pass seeds intact and drop them far from the parent plant, which is exactly what a plant needs.

{{contrast: capsaicin for birds | is=largely silent | not=a deterrent}}
The receptor explains the split. Later work from the Julius laboratory compared the bird version of TRPV1 with the mammalian one. The bird channel still responds to heat, but it barely responds to capsaicin. For birds the molecule is largely silent, not a deterrent. So capsaicin works as a filter: it deters the animals that would destroy the seeds and stays quiet for the animals that spread them. The same molecule, meeting two versions of one channel, produces two completely different experiences.

{{checklist: What capsaicin may do | +deters mammals | ?slows fungi}}
Capsaicin may have more than one job. Field work on wild chilies has also suggested that it slows fungi that attack seeds, and that wild plants in wetter, fungus-heavy places tend to be hotter. That second role is less settled than the first. The firm part is narrow: it deters mammals. It may also slow fungi. Both are inferences about evolutionary pressure, and no experiment can rerun the history that produced them.

### Heat was measured by tongues before it was measured by machines

{{timeline: 1912=Wilbur Scoville | 1997=TRPV1}}
Long before anyone knew the receptor existed, people wanted to rank chilies. In 1912 a pharmacist named Wilbur Scoville published a method that used the only detector available, human tongues. An extract of dried pepper was diluted in sugar water, step by step, until a panel of tasters could no longer detect any heat. The amount of dilution needed became the score, now called Scoville heat units. A pepper that needed more dilution was hotter. It was clever, and it was entirely subjective. Not until the TRPV1 work of 1997 did anyone know what those tasters were actually detecting.

{{steps: From tongues to instruments | sugar water | a panel of tasters | high-performance liquid chromatography | >converted back into Scoville heat units}}
The weakness is built in. Tongues tire, people differ, and a panel's threshold drifts from day to day. So laboratories now measure capsaicinoids directly with high-performance liquid chromatography, which separates the compounds in an extract and quantifies each one. The result is a concentration, a chemical fact rather than a sensation. That concentration is then converted back into Scoville heat units, so the familiar scale survives even though the sugar water and the panel of tasters are gone.

{{contrast: Scoville heat units | is=a concentration | not=a measurement of experience}}
On that converted scale, pure capsaicin sits at the very top, in the millions, and the exact figure quoted for it depends on the conversion used. Treat any such number as a convention rather than a measurement of experience. Scoville heat units now report a concentration of a molecule. They do not measure how much a given person feels, which depends on their receptors, their history of exposure and the food the chili is cooked into.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +the receptor | +the heat threshold | -how much a given person feels | ?why chilies evolved capsaicin}}
Here is what this evidence does not establish. The receptor work settles the mechanism of the burn and the heat threshold behind it; it does not measure how much a given person feels, which varies and is shaped by exposure. Published Scoville figures for named varieties span wide ranges, because a plant's heat changes with growing conditions, so any single number for a variety is a midpoint, not a fact about every fruit. The 43 degree threshold is an approximate value from cells in a dish, and the channel's sensitivity shifts with its chemical surroundings. And the account of why chilies evolved capsaicin is the best supported inference, not a recorded event.

### Closing

A chili is a message written in the wrong language on purpose. The molecule speaks to a channel built to detect heat, and the nerve, with only one way to answer, says burn. Nothing is warming. You are hearing a plant's alarm, set for mammals, ignored by birds, and read by your brain as fire.
