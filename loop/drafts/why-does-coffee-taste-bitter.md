## YouTube title

Coffee stays bitter without caffeine

## Direct-answer lock

Coffee tastes bitter mainly because roasting breaks the bean's chlorogenic acids into new bitter molecules, which bind bitter taste receptors on the tongue; caffeine contributes, but only part of the effect. Light and medium roasts owe much of their bitterness to chlorogenic acid lactones, while dark roasts build harsher phenylindanes. How bitter a given cup tastes then depends on how much of that chemistry the brew extracts and on the receptors of the person drinking it.

### Decaf is the experiment that rules caffeine out

{{contrast: caffeine | is=a contributor | not=the main source}}
Start with the cheapest experiment in food science: a cup of decaffeinated coffee. Decaffeination removes most of the caffeine from green beans before they are roasted, and the result is still unmistakably bitter. If caffeine were the main source, decaf should taste noticeably flat. It does not. Caffeine is genuinely bitter on its own. Dissolve the pure compound in water and you get a clean, sharp bitterness. So caffeine is a contributor, but it is not the main source. Coffee stays bitter without caffeine, because its bitterness was never mostly caffeine to begin with.

{{define: caffeine | a bitter alkaloid in the raw bean | its bitterness does not change much with roasting | PubChem}}
Stability is the second clue. Caffeine is a bitter alkaloid in the raw bean, and it is fairly heat-stable, so its bitterness does not change much with roasting. PubChem, the chemical database run by the National Institutes of Health, records its structure: a small, ring-shaped molecule built from carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and oxygen. Yet anyone who has compared a pale roast with a very dark one knows the bitterness changes a great deal. A molecule whose amount barely moves cannot explain a taste that moves that much. Whatever drives the difference has to be made, or destroyed, in the roaster.

### Bitterness is built in the roaster, not grown on the tree

{{cross_section: green coffee bean | chlorogenic acids}}
A green coffee bean, taken from the fruit and dried, tastes grassy and astringent rather than like coffee. Among its most abundant soluble compounds is a group called chlorogenic acids. Despite the name, they have nothing to do with chlorine. Each one is an ester, a joining of caffeic acid, or a close relative, with another molecule called quinic acid. PubChem holds entries for members of the family, with their structures. In the raw bean they are more astringent than bitter. They are better thought of as raw material, waiting for heat.

{{kitchen: 200 | MAILLARD}}
Then comes the heat. The American Chemical Society, describing the chemistry of a roast, puts the beans above 200 degrees Celsius. At those temperatures the bean behaves like a small pressure vessel. Water flashes to steam, sugars and amino acids run through Maillard reactions that make brown pigments and aroma, and the chlorogenic acids start to come apart. Some of their mass simply breaks down. Some of it is rebuilt into molecules that were never in the raw bean. Roasting is not cooking the bitterness out. It is cooking it in.

### A light roast and a dark roast are bitter for different reasons

{{steps: From acid to bitterness | chlorogenic acids | chlorogenic acid lactones | phenylindanes | >harsher with darker roasts}}
The path from acid to bitterness runs in stages. The first is a loss of water inside the molecule. When a chlorogenic acid loses water, its quinic acid portion closes into a tighter ring, and the products are called chlorogenic acid lactones. Lactones taste distinctly bitter, and research reported by the American Chemical Society identified them as major bitter compounds in light to medium roasts. Push the roast further, and the lactones themselves break down. Their caffeic acid portions are freed, fragment, and link together into compounds called phenylindanes. Those carry a harsher, more lingering bitterness, and they accumulate in dark roasts. So the bitterness is harsher with darker roasts, and it carries a different chemical signature.

{{checklist: What the roast pathway explains | +darker roasts taste more bitter | +decaf stays bitter | ?the exact share of each compound}}
Notice what this explains at once. It explains why darker roasts taste more bitter, since the pathway keeps generating harsher products as the heat continues. It explains why decaf stays bitter, because decaffeination targets caffeine and leaves the chlorogenic acids behind to be transformed in the roaster. What it does not hand you is the exact share of each compound in a given cup. That depends on the bean, the roast, and the brew, and published figures vary from one study to the next.

### The culprits were named by tasting fractions, not by guessing

{{chain: sensory-guided analysis | liquid chromatography | tasting panels | mass spectrometry | >the structure is confirmed}}
How do chemists know which of the many molecules in a cup is bitter? A brewed coffee contains hundreds of compounds, and an instrument alone cannot taste anything. The method used is often called sensory-guided analysis. First, liquid chromatography separates the brew into fractions, each holding a narrower set of molecules. Then trained tasting panels sample each fraction, diluting it step by step until the bitterness disappears. A fraction that stays bitter through many dilutions holds something potent. Only then do the instruments take over, and mass spectrometry weighs the molecules and their fragments, while nuclear magnetic resonance maps how their atoms connect, until the structure is confirmed.

{{define: dilution threshold | the weakest concentration at which a trained panel still detects bitterness | it varies between tasters | Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry}}
The next step closes the loop. Once a structure is proposed, chemists make the compound from scratch, purify it, and taste the synthetic version. If it reproduces the bitterness at the expected strength, the identification holds. A key measure here is the dilution threshold, the weakest concentration at which a trained panel still detects bitterness. Comparing that threshold with how much of the compound is actually in a cup tells you whether it matters, or is merely present. This kind of work fills the pages of the Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, published by the American Chemical Society. The threshold is not a law of nature: it varies between tasters, which is why panels are trained and results are averaged.

{{text}}
There is a stricter test still, and it is the closest thing this field has to a controlled experiment. Researchers rebuild the cup. They take each identified compound at the concentration measured in a real brew, dissolve the set in water, and ask a panel to compare the mixture with the coffee it came from. If the rebuilt version tastes about as bitter, the list is reasonably complete. Then they leave compounds out, one group at a time. When removing a group makes the mixture noticeably less bitter, that group is pulling its weight. Omission tests of this kind are how caffeine's modest role was measured, rather than assumed.

### Bitter is a verdict reached by receptors

{{cross_section: taste bud | bitter receptors}}
Molecules are only half of the story. Bitterness is something a cell decides. On the tongue, and on the soft palate, taste buds hold receptor cells, and some of those cells carry bitter receptors. These are proteins that span the cell membrane. When the right molecule docks on the outside, the receptor changes shape and triggers a signal inside the cell, and a nerve carries the message on. As research supported by the NIH has described, humans carry about 25 types of bitter receptor, and many of them respond to several different molecules.

{{steps: receptor assay | receptor gene | cultured cells | calcium signal | >which molecule activates which receptor}}
The way scientists learned which molecule triggers which receptor is not by tasting. The method is a receptor assay. They take a single receptor gene and insert it into cultured cells that do not normally taste anything. They load those cells with a dye that glows when calcium floods in, which is what happens when the receptor fires, producing a calcium signal. Then they add a test compound. If the cells light up, that receptor responds. Repeating this across the family maps which molecule activates which receptor. Caffeine, tested this way, activates several of them. Roast compounds can be screened the same way, though fewer have been fully mapped.

{{contrast: bitterness | is=a response in the taster | not=a fixed property of the molecule}}
This is why bitterness is a response in the taster, not a fixed property of the molecule. The receptor genes vary between people. Some versions of a receptor bind a given compound strongly, others weakly, and some people barely detect certain bitter molecules at all. The same cup can register as harsh to one person and mild to another, without either of them being wrong. The chemistry in the cup is the same. The sensors are not.

### Brewing decides how much of the bitterness reaches you

{{chain: extraction | a finer grind | longer contact time | hotter water | >over-extraction}}
The roaster makes the bitter molecules. The brew decides how many of them leave the grounds. Hot water is a solvent, and different compounds dissolve at different rates. Bright acids and many aroma compounds come out early. Some of the heavier, more bitter roast products come out more slowly. So anything that pushes extraction further, whether a finer grind, longer contact time or hotter water, tends to pull more bitter compounds into the cup. Coffee professionals call the extreme over-extraction, and its usual signature is a dry, lingering bitterness.

{{text}}
There is also what goes in afterwards. Sugar does not remove a single bitter molecule from the cup. It lowers how bitter the cup seems, because sweet and bitter signals interact as the brain combines taste information. Milk is a different case. Proteins in milk can bind some of coffee's polyphenols, and fat changes how compounds spread across the tongue, though how much each effect matters in a real cup is still debated. In both cases the bitterness is changed in perception, not deleted from the chemistry.

### What to notice in the edit

{{sources: Who showed what | ACS=roast compounds drive bitterness | NIH=about 25 bitter receptor types | PubChem=structures of caffeine and chlorogenic acids}}
Watch how the edit keeps three kinds of evidence apart. The structures of caffeine and chlorogenic acids come from PubChem. The identification of the roast products comes from sensory-guided chemistry of the kind the American Chemical Society, the ACS, has reported, showing that roast compounds drive bitterness once the fractions are tasted. The receptor side comes from cell assays, with research supported by the NIH counting about 25 bitter receptor types. Each strand answers a different question. Notice, too, that no single instrument tells the whole story. The chromatograph separates, the panel tastes, the spectrometer identifies, and the cells report.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What remains open | +roasting creates bitter compounds | -a fixed recipe for bitterness | ?the share from melanoidins}}
This evidence does not establish a fixed recipe for bitterness. Most studies work on particular beans, roasted in particular ways, brewed by particular methods, and the proportions they report do not transfer cleanly to every cup. The contribution of other roast products is less settled. Melanoidins, the large brown polymers built during roasting, are proposed as contributors, but the share from melanoidins is hard to pin down because they are too large and varied to isolate cleanly. Receptor maps come from cells in dishes, not from a living mouth, where saliva, aroma and temperature all intervene. What is firm is narrower: roasting creates bitter compounds that the raw bean lacks, and caffeine alone cannot account for the taste.

A green bean holds the raw material. Heat rebuilds it into molecules the plant never made, water carries some of them out, and a family of receptors renders its verdict. The bitterness in the cup is not one substance. It is a record of the roast.
