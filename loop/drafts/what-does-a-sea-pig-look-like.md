## Direct-answer lock

A sea pig is a small, plump, translucent pinkish sea cucumber that walks across the deep ocean floor on several pairs of fluid-filled legs, with a few longer projections standing up from its back. It belongs to the genus Scotoplanes, an echinoderm related to sea stars and sea urchins, and it feeds by pushing seafloor sediment into its mouth with a ring of short tentacles. Its living appearance is known mainly from cameras on remotely operated vehicles, because specimens brought to the surface lose their shape and colour.

### A sea pig is a sea cucumber, and the name points the wrong way

{{define: sea pig | a deep-sea sea cucumber in the genus Scotoplanes | a common name, not a classification | Smithsonian}}
Start with the name, because it points you the wrong way. Sea pig is a common name, not a classification. The animal is a deep-sea sea cucumber in the genus Scotoplanes, and there is more than one species in that genus. The comparison to a pig is about looks and habits alone: a rounded pinkish body, a blunt front end, and a life spent nosing through mud. Nothing about it is mammalian. It has no bones, no lungs, no eyes and no brain of the kind a vertebrate has. Smithsonian Ocean's deep-sea pages describe a seafloor where much of the food arrives as debris sinking from the sunlit water far above, and a sea pig is one of the animals built to make a living from that slow rain.

{{contrast: sea pig | is=a sea cucumber | not=a pig}}
Sea cucumbers are echinoderms, the group that also includes sea stars, sea urchins and brittle stars. That family tree explains most of what you see. Echinoderms build their bodies around a water vascular system: a network of internal canals filled with fluid and connected to many small tube feet. On a sea star, tube feet line the underside of each arm. On a typical sea cucumber, they run along the body in rows. A useful way to picture a sea cucumber is as a stretched-out echinoderm lying on its side, with its mouth at one end and its anus at the other. A sea pig takes that plan and enlarges one part of it until that part becomes the most obvious thing about the animal.

### The legs are tube feet, inflated rather than jointed

{{steps: A tube foot works by hydraulics | Fluid is pushed into the foot | the foot extends | muscles in its wall pull it back | >hydraulics, not bones}}
That question applies to sea pigs more than you might expect, because the same handful of images and descriptions circulate widely. So it helps to go back to what the anatomy itself shows. The legs are the feature people notice first, and they are not legs in the way yours are. They are enlarged tube feet. A tube foot works by hydraulics. Fluid is pushed into the foot, the foot extends, and muscles in its wall pull it back and steer it. There are no joints. Sea pigs walk on legs without bones. A sea pig crossing the mud is walking on soft, pressurised tubes, coordinated well enough to carry the body forward. In video, the gait looks slow and slightly wobbly, which is what you would expect from hydraulics, not bones.

{{text}}
You will see different counts given for how many of these legs a sea pig has. That variation is worth taking seriously rather than smoothing over. Species within the genus differ, individuals differ, and counting soft, moving appendages from video or from a shrunken specimen is harder than it sounds. The safe description is several pairs, arranged along the sides of the flattened underside. If a source gives you a single confident number without saying which species or how it was counted, that is exactly the kind of figure worth tracing back before you repeat it.

### The "antennae" on its back are tube feet too

{{contrast: the projections on its back | is=modified tube feet | not=antennae}}
On top of the body, near the front, a sea pig carries a few longer structures. In photographs, the projections on its back stand up like antennae or the ears of a cartoon animal, and many popular descriptions call them that. Anatomically, they are modified tube feet, often called papillae, belonging to the same water vascular system as the legs underneath. They are not antennae in the insect sense, and they are not ears. Their identity is better established than their purpose. They may help the animal sense the water around it, and some researchers have suggested a role in orientation or movement. The evidence for any one function is limited, so treat confident claims about what they do with caution.

{{ambient}}
This is a pattern that runs through deep-sea biology. Describing a structure from images and dissections is far easier than showing what it does. Function usually needs repeated observation of living animals in their own environment, and for an animal on the deep seafloor, those observations are expensive, brief and few.

### Its colour is partly a product of the camera's lights

{{light}}
Now the colour. Images usually show a translucent body in shades of pink, sometimes leaning toward violet. But the deep seafloor where sea pigs live receives no sunlight at all. Seawater absorbs red light quickly and blue light least, and below a certain depth, sunlight is gone entirely. NOAA Ocean Exploration's published ocean facts cover how light fades as you descend. In the sea pig's own world, there is no ambient light for pink to show up in. Every colour you have seen in a sea pig image was revealed by lamps carried down on a vehicle. The colour belongs to real pigment and tissue, but how it reads on your screen depends on those lights and on the camera's settings.

### A specimen on deck is not what the animal looks like

{{checklist: What a specimen can and cannot show | +internal anatomy | +the body plan | -the living shape | -the living colour | ?the function of the projections}}
That brings back the opening problem. For a long time, the main way to know a deep-sea animal was to drag a net or dredge across the bottom and haul up whatever came with it. For a sea pig, that journey is damaging. Its body is soft and watery, supported largely by internal fluid rather than a rigid frame. Brought up through warming water, handled on deck, and preserved in a jar, it can lose its shape and much of its colour. A specimen is still valuable. It shows the internal anatomy and confirms the body plan, and it lets taxonomists compare one animal against another in detail. What it cannot reliably show is the living shape or the living colour. That is what a specimen can and cannot show. And neither specimens nor brief video settle the function of the projections.

{{chain: what a sea pig looks like | remotely operated vehicles | video of living animals | >the living shape}}
Remotely operated vehicles changed this. A vehicle tethered to a ship can hover above the seafloor with lights and high-resolution cameras, recording animals where they are: upright, inflated and moving. MBARI has used vehicles like these off California for decades, and its archive of remotely operated vehicle footage is one of the reasons sea pigs became familiar to the public at all. NOAA Ocean Exploration's expeditions add footage from other regions. When you see a plump, pink animal standing on its legs with its projections raised, you are seeing video of living animals, not a reconstruction from preserved ones.

### Its mouth is built for eating the seafloor

{{define: deposit feeder | an animal that eats organic material from sediment | it eats the seafloor, not prey | Smithsonian}}
Look at the front of the underside and you find the mouth, ringed by short tentacles. A sea pig is a deposit feeder: an animal that eats organic material from sediment. The tentacles push mud into the mouth, the gut extracts whatever nutritious material is mixed into it, and the rest passes through. In other words, it eats the seafloor, not prey. That food comes from above. Smithsonian Ocean describes the deep sea as depending heavily on marine snow, the drift of dead plankton, waste and other organic particles sinking from the surface. Where that material settles on the bottom, a sea pig can graze it, and its blunt front end is suited to working along the surface of the mud.

### Video shows sea pigs gathering in groups

{{sources: video of living animals | MBARI=remotely operated vehicle footage | NOAA Ocean Exploration=deep-sea expeditions | Smithsonian Ocean=deep-sea ecology}}
Video also shows something a specimen never could: sea pigs are often seen in groups, sometimes many animals spread across one patch of seafloor. Observations from MBARI's remotely operated vehicle footage, and from other deep-sea expeditions, record these aggregations. Researchers have reported that sea pigs tend to face in a similar direction, often into the current, which may help them detect food arriving on the flow. Juvenile crabs have also been reported sheltering on or beneath sea pigs. These are observations of behaviour, and they come with a caution built in. A camera sees only where the vehicle happens to go, so how common these patterns are, and what drives them, is still a question for deep-sea ecology.

### What to notice in the edit

{{text}}
As you watch the footage in this episode, notice three things. First, watch the edges of the light. The pool of illumination around the vehicle is the only reason anything is visible. Second, look for scale. Many research vehicles project a pair of laser dots a known distance apart, so that scientists can measure animals on screen; without a reference like that, a sea pig can look any size. Published sizes vary by species and by source, but descriptions generally put a sea pig at roughly hand-sized. Third, notice whether an image shows a living animal on the seafloor or a specimen on a tray. The two can look like different creatures, and that difference is the whole subject of this episode.

### Evidence limit

{{contrast: the evidence | is=appearance | not=how many sea pigs there are}}
Everything here describes appearance, and appearance is the part of sea pig biology the evidence handles best. It does not establish how many sea pigs there are, how far their populations extend, how long they live, or how the different species compare in life rather than in preserved collections. Video is gathered where expeditions choose to dive, so it samples only a small part of the deep seafloor. Specimens over-represent animals that survive being collected. The images you know are real, but they are a narrow window, and the most familiar picture of a sea pig may be a picture of one population, under one set of lights, in one region.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So a sea pig looks like a small, soft, pinkish sea cucumber on fluid-filled legs, carrying a few raised tube feet on its back. That much is solid. It is solid because people learned to stop judging deep-sea animals by what arrived on deck, and started lowering lights and cameras to meet them where they live.
