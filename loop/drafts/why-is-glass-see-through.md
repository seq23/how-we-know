### Crystalline order scatters light while amorphous networks let it pass

{{steps: GLASS FORMATION | DISSOLVE SILICA MELT | COOL RAPIDLY TO PREVENT CRYSTALLIZATION | LOCK DISORDER IN AMORPHOUS SOLID | >UNIFORM REFRACTIVE INDEX}}
To understand how light interacts with glass, material scientists at MIT DMSE look first at how atoms arrange themselves when a liquid cools into a solid. Most solid materials in nature, such as metals or minerals, form rigid geometric structures known as crystalline lattices. As a liquid metal or molten quartz cools slowly, microscopic individual crystals sprout in different orientations, creating internal interfaces where these distinct crystalline grains meet. When a wave of light encounters a boundary between two crystalline grains, the sudden change in atomic orientation shifts the speed and direction of the wave. The light reflects, refracts, and scatters in countless random directions, rendering the bulk material opaque or milky white. In contrast, researchers examine how to dissolve silica melt, cool rapidly to prevent crystallization, and lock disorder in amorphous solid form to produce a uniform refractive index.

{{define: AMORPHOUS SOLID | A SOLID LACKING LONG-RANGE CRYSTALLINE ORDER | UNIFORM MOLECULAR DENSITY | ASTM INTERNATIONAL}}
Glass behaves entirely differently because of the specific thermal pathway used during production. When molten silicon dioxide is cooled rapidly, individual silicon and oxygen atoms cannot move quickly enough to organize into a repeating crystalline lattice. Instead, the liquid structure freezes in place. According to material classification definitions published by ASTM International, an amorphous solid is defined as a solid lacking long-range crystalline order while maintaining uniform molecular density. Because an amorphous network contains no grain boundaries, phase interfaces, or micro-crystalline boundaries, light encounters a continuous atomic density throughout the material. On spatial scales larger than the wavelength of visible light—between 400 and 700 nanometers—the refractive index remains constant across the entire volume, allowing visible light waves to propagate in straight lines without structural scattering.

### Electronic band gaps determine which light frequencies pass through

{{chain: PHOTON ABSORPTION BARRIER | INCIDENT VISIBLE PHOTON | INSUFFICIENT ENERGY FOR ELECTRON JUMP | >UNABSORBED OPTICAL TRANSMISSION}}
Eliminating structural scattering explains why light is not bounced around inside glass, but it does not explain why light is not absorbed by the atoms themselves. Optical absorption depends on quantum mechanics and the behavior of subatomic electrons. In any solid material, electrons reside in specific allowed energy levels grouped into distinct bands. The lower energy band occupied by bound valence electrons is called the valence band, while the higher energy band where electrons move freely is called the conduction band. The energy gap separating these two states is known as the electronic band gap. When an incident visible photon hits the structure, if it brings insufficient energy for electron jump across the gap, the result is unabsorbed optical transmission through the material.

{{magnitude: ELECTRON VOLT GAP COMPARISON | electron-volts | VISIBLE LIGHT MINIMUM=1.65 | VISIBLE LIGHT MAXIMUM=3.1 | SILICA BAND GAP=9}}
Reference measurements established by NIST demonstrate that pure silica glass has an exceptionally wide electronic band gap comparison threshold of 9 electron-volts. By comparison, visible light occupies a much lower energy range on the electromagnetic spectrum. Optical physics data from NIST shows a visible light minimum of 1.65 electron-volts at the red end of the spectrum and a visible light maximum of 3.1 electron-volts at the violet end. Because even the highest-energy violet photon carries barely one-third of the 9 electron-volts required to jump the electronic band gap, visible light lacks the energy to excite electrons in pure silica. The electrons remain locked in their covalent valence bonds, and visible light photons travel directly through the atomic matrix without loss.

### Chemical impurities alter energy absorption to create visible tinting

{{checklist: SODA-LIME GLASS COMPOSITION | +SILICON DIOXIDE SEVENTY PERCENT | +SODIUM OXIDE FIFTEEN PERCENT | +CALCIUM OXIDE NINE PERCENT}}
While pure silica glass is completely clear across all visible wavelengths, ordinary window glass often exhibits a noticeable greenish tint, particularly when viewed edge-on. Standard window pane material is soda-lime glass, which according to soda-lime glass composition guidelines from USGS Minerals consists of silicon dioxide seventy percent, sodium oxide fifteen percent, and calcium oxide nine percent. During commercial glass manufacturing, raw materials such as sand contain trace amounts of iron oxide impurities. Even when present in concentrations of less than one-tenth of one percent, iron ions introduce intermediate energy levels inside the 9 electron-volt band gap of the silica host matrix.

{{contrast: GLASS PURITY TYPES | is=ultra-clear low-iron glass | not=standard green-edge soda-lime glass}}
These iron-induced intermediate energy states allow the material to absorb specific visible wavelengths that pure silica would normally ignore. Specifically, divalent iron ions absorb light in the red and near-infrared regions around 700 nanometers. Because red photons are selectively absorbed as light passes through thick sections of glass, the light that successfully exits the material is shifted toward the blue and green portions of the spectrum. How glass purity types behave differs from standard green-edge soda-lime glass when produced as ultra-clear low-iron glass. To manufacture ultra-clear glass, producers refine raw materials to reduce iron content below 0.01 percent by weight, restoring uniform transmission across all visible wavelengths.

### Invisible spectrum limits reveal the boundaries of glass transparency

{{stages: ELECTROMAGNETIC TRANSMISSION REGIMES | FAR ULTRAVIOLET ABSORPTION | VISIBLE LIGHT TRANSPARENCY | FAR INFRARED OPACITY}}
Glass is transparent only within a specific window of the electromagnetic spectrum. If human vision operated in the ultraviolet or far infrared regions, standard glass windows would appear completely dark and opaque. In the short-wavelength ultraviolet spectrum, photon energy increases rapidly as wavelength decreases. Photons with wavelengths below 200 nanometers carry energies above 6 electron-volts, approaching the electronic band gap threshold of silica. Electromagnetic transmission regimes transition across far ultraviolet absorption, visible light transparency, and far infrared opacity. Optical data compiled by IEEE shows that standard glass absorbs ultraviolet light with wavelengths shorter than 350 nanometers, explaining why glass blocks sunburn-causing rays while remaining clear to visible light.

{{chain: INFRARED ABSORPTION MECHANISM | INCIDENT INFRARED PHOTON | RESONANCE WITH SILICON OXYGEN BOND | MOLECULAR VIBRATION HEAT LOSS | >FAR INFRARED OPACITY}}
At the long-wavelength end of the spectrum, in the far infrared region, glass becomes opaque through an infrared absorption mechanism. An incident infrared photon carries very low energy—well under 1 electron-volt—which is far too weak to excite electrons across the 9 electron-volt band gap. However, infrared frequencies match the natural mechanical vibration frequencies of the covalent bonds connecting silicon and oxygen atoms. An incident infrared photon creates resonance with silicon oxygen bond structures, leading to molecular vibration heat loss and resulting in far infrared opacity at wavelengths longer than 2500 nanometers. This mechanical absorption converts light energy into heat within the solid atomic network.

### Modern fiber optics push silica transparency to theoretical limits

{{steps: HIGH PURITY OPTICAL FIBER | SYNTHESIZE SYNTHETIC FUSED SILICA | ELIMINATE TRACE METAL IMPURITIES | DRAW HIGH PURITY FIBER | >MINIMAL OPTICAL ATTENUATION}}
The upper boundary of transparency in solid matter is revealed by high purity optical fiber technology. In standard window glass, light intensity decays after traveling through only a few meters because of scattering from microscopic density fluctuations and absorption by trace metal contaminants. Technical developments documented by Fraunhofer Institute and published by IEEE demonstrate how to synthesize synthetic fused silica using chemical vapor deposition, eliminate trace metal impurities, and draw high purity fiber with minimal optical attenuation. By removing structural defects and chemical impurities down to parts-per-billion levels, engineered silica glass allows optical signals to travel across vast distances without signal degradation.

{{uncertain: OPTICAL FIBER LOSS | decibels per kilometer | 0.025 | HIGH | IEEE AND NIST MEASURED DATA}}
In these specialized optical fibers, signal loss—known as optical attenuation—is reduced to extraordinary levels. At an infrared wavelength of 1550 nanometers, optical fiber loss is measured at 0.175 decibels per kilometer with a range of plus or minus 0.025 decibels per kilometer, confirmed by IEEE and NIST measured data. At an attenuation rate of 0.20 decibels per kilometer, light can travel through 20 kilometers of solid glass before losing half of its original intensity. The minute amount of remaining loss is not caused by impurity absorption, but by fundamental density fluctuations frozen into the glass matrix during cooling. This physical limit, known as Rayleigh scattering, represents the ultimate structural boundary of transparency in an amorphous solid.
