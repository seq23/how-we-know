## Narration

### Cold open

{{stat: 65,000 | kilometres | A continuous range of underwater volcanoes | NOAA}}
Earth's longest mountain range was built without a single collision. A mid-ocean ridge forms where two tectonic plates are pulled apart: the mantle beneath rises to fill the gap, melts as its pressure drops, and erupts as fresh basalt that cools into new ocean crust. NOAA describes the result as a continuous range of underwater volcanoes stretching nearly 65,000 kilometres around the globe, like the seams on a baseball. That figure tells you how big the system is. It does not tell you why rock rises, why it melts without being heated, or why the ridge stands tall at all. And it hides an older assumption that this evidence overturned: that mountains are what you get when the crust is squeezed. This one is what you get when it is stretched.

### Title card

How is a mid-ocean ridge formed?

### The ridge is a crack being filled, not a fold being pushed up

{{contrast: mid-ocean ridge | is=a crack being filled | not=a fold being pushed up}}
Start with what a mid-ocean ridge is not. The Alps and the Himalayas are folds, crust crumpled and stacked where plates converge. A mid-ocean ridge is a crack being filled, not a fold being pushed up. Along its crest, two plates move away from each other, and the gap between them never stays open. Something from below always arrives to close it. The ridge is the shape that filling takes.

{{text}}
That changes how you should picture the process. It is tempting to imagine molten rock forcing its way upward and shoving the plates aside, like a wedge driven into a log. The mainstream view, which you will find in the plate tectonics material published by USGS, runs mostly the other way. The plates are pulled apart by forces acting elsewhere, chiefly the weight of old, cold plate sinking back into the mantle at subduction zones, often thousands of kilometres from the ridge. The ridge responds to that pull. It is less an engine than an opening that keeps healing.

{{steps: Building new seafloor | plates pulled apart | mantle rises | rock melts | basalt erupts | >new ocean crust}}
Here is the sequence, in short form: plates pulled apart, mantle rises, rock melts, basalt erupts. The mantle beneath the crest rises passively into the space the plates leave behind. As it rises, some of that rock melts. The melt, lighter than the solid rock around it, collects and climbs. It erupts onto the seafloor or freezes just beneath it, and the cooled basalt becomes new ocean crust, welded onto the trailing edges of both plates. Then the plates keep moving, and the cycle begins again on the same seam.

### The mantle melts because pressure falls, not because it heats up

{{define: decompression melting | rock melting because the pressure on it drops | not melting caused by added heat | USGS}}
The step that surprises most people is the melting. The mantle beneath a ridge is mostly solid rock. It is extremely hot, but it is also under enormous pressure, and pressure raises the temperature at which rock melts. Hold that rock deep down and it stays solid. Let it rise, and the pressure on it falls faster than the rock can lose its heat. At some point it crosses its own melting point without any heat being added at all. Geologists call this decompression melting: rock melting because the pressure on it drops, not melting caused by added heat.

{{chain: How melting is inferred | lab experiments on mantle rock | composition of ridge basalt | seismic images beneath ridges | >the mantle partly melts as it rises}}
Nobody can watch this happen, so it is inferred from three lines of evidence. First, lab experiments on mantle rock squeeze and heat samples to map the conditions where they begin to melt. Second, the composition of ridge basalt, dredged and drilled from the seafloor, matches what partial melting of mantle rock should produce. Only a fraction of the rock melts, and that melt carries a recognisable chemical signature. Third, seismic images beneath ridges show zones where sound waves slow down, which is what you would expect where some melt is present. None of these alone proves the process. Together they point the same way: the mantle partly melts as it rises.

### The ridge stands tall because its rock is young

{{contrast: ridge height | is=hot rock taking up more room | not=rock piled up by force}}
Now the height. If nothing is being squeezed, it is fair to wonder why there is a mountain range at all. The answer is temperature. Freshly made crust, and the mantle just beneath it, are hot, and hot rock takes up more room than the same rock cold. So the newest seafloor rides high. As the plates carry it away from the crest, it cools, contracts and grows denser, and it settles lower over millions of years. The ridge is a long, broad swell of warm, young rock, sloping down on either side into the older, colder, deeper floor of the abyss. Its height is hot rock taking up more room, not rock piled up by force.

{{ambient}}
That explanation has a consequence you can test. If the height comes from cooling, then seafloor depth should track seafloor age in a predictable way: shallow at the crest, deeper with distance, and flattening out as the rock approaches the temperature of its surroundings. Depth surveys across the major ocean basins show broadly that pattern. It is one of the quieter confirmations that the ridge is a thermal feature, not a structural one.

### The first clue was a valley nobody expected

{{chain: Mapping the Atlantic floor | echo-sounding depth profiles | hand-drawn cross-sections | a valley along the crest | >a rift where the crust splits}}
The modern story begins with maps. In the years after the Second World War, research ships crossed the Atlantic running echo sounders, timing sound pulses to the seafloor and back to measure depth. Marie Tharp, working with the geologist Bruce Heezen, turned those echo-sounding depth profiles into hand-drawn cross-sections of the ocean floor. Lined up side by side, the profiles showed something consistent: a valley along the crest of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, running down its centre like a notch. Tharp argued it was a rift, a place where the crust was splitting. Heezen was at first sceptical, because continental drift was then a minority view among geologists. But the pattern held as more soundings came in, and it lined up with where earthquakes clustered beneath the ocean. A rift where the crust splits was the reading that survived.

{{text}}
A map, though, shows shape. It cannot show motion. A valley along a ridge is consistent with spreading, but it does not prove it. For that, someone needed a way to read time off the seafloor itself.

### The seafloor recorded its own age in magnetism

{{steps: Reading the magnetic stripes | basalt cools at the ridge | iron minerals lock in the field | the field reverses | stripes mirror on both sides | >the seafloor is spreading}}
The key came from magnetism. When basalt cools at the ridge, iron-bearing minerals inside it align with Earth's magnetic field, and that alignment is fixed as the rock drops below what is called the Curie temperature. The iron minerals lock in the field as it was at that moment. And Earth's field does not stay put. Over geological time, the field reverses, with magnetic north and south swapping places. Ships towing magnetometers had already found long stripes of seafloor magnetised alternately one way and the other. Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews, and independently Lawrence Morley, proposed that the stripes mirror on both sides of a ridge because new crust forms at the centre and carries each reversal outward in both directions. In other words, the seafloor is spreading.

{{define: seafloor spreading | new crust forming at a ridge and moving away on both sides | it does not say what drives the plates | Nature}}
Vine and Matthews published their argument in the journal Nature, and it turned an earlier hypothesis into something testable. Harry Hess had already proposed seafloor spreading: new crust forming at a ridge and moving away on both sides. The magnetic stripes were the record that idea predicted, a symmetrical tape running outward from the crest. Notice what the idea did not explain on its own. It does not say what drives the plates. That part came later, and it is still being refined. That the seafloor spreads is one finding. Why it spreads is a separate question.

### Drilling and diving checked the prediction

{{checklist: What spreading predicted | +sediment thickens away from the ridge | +crust grows older away from the ridge | +hot water venting at the crest | ?what drives the plates}}
A good hypothesis sticks its neck out, and spreading made predictions that could have failed. If the crust is born at the ridge, then sediment thickens away from the ridge, because older floor has had longer to collect the particles that drift down from above. And the crust grows older away from the ridge. The drilling ship Glomar Challenger, working for the Deep Sea Drilling Project, recovered cores across the South Atlantic and found exactly that progression. The oldest sediment lying directly on the basalt got older with distance from the crest, on both sides. The pattern fit. The question of what drives the plates stayed open.

{{stat: 1977 | year | Hydrothermal vents found at the Galápagos Rift | WHOI}}
There was another prediction. If seawater seeps into hot, freshly made crust, it should be heated and pushed back out, with hot water venting at the crest. In 1977, scientists diving in the submersible Alvin, operated by Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, known as WHOI, found hydrothermal vents on the Galápagos Rift, a spreading centre in the eastern Pacific. They expected hot water. They did not expect the dense communities of animals living around it. The geology was confirmed, and the biology opened an entirely new field.

### Spreading speed shapes the ridge

{{contrast: slow-spreading ridge | is=a deep central rift valley | not=a smooth central high}}
Not every ridge looks like the one Tharp drew. Speed matters. USGS describes spreading at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge as slow, on the order of a few centimetres a year, roughly the pace at which your fingernails grow. Ridges that spread faster, such as the East Pacific Rise, receive magma more steadily. A slow-spreading ridge tends to show a deep central rift valley, not a smooth central high. Fast ridges show the reverse: a broad swell with little or no valley at its crest. Exact rates differ along each ridge and between studies, so treat any single figure as an average over a long stretch of seafloor.

{{text}}
Fractures also break the ridge into offset segments. These transform faults, where the plates slide past each other sideways, are why the ridge zigzags on a map instead of running in one clean line. And in one place the ridge rises above the waves. Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where an unusually generous magma supply, usually attributed to a mantle hotspot, has built the crest high enough to form land. There you can stand inside a rift valley in open air and see the process the deep sea normally hides.

### Evidence limit

{{checklist: What the evidence settles | +new crust forms at ridges | +the ridge is high because it is hot | -exactly how melt gathers beneath the crest | ?how much each force drives the plates}}
Here is the edge of it. The evidence firmly establishes that new crust forms at ridges, and that the ridge is high because it is hot. It does not establish exactly how melt gathers beneath the crest: how it migrates upward through mostly solid rock and focuses into a narrow band at the axis is still modelled and argued over. Nor does it settle how much each force drives the plates. Slab pull is widely judged the largest, but the balance between it and other forces is estimated, not measured directly. Most of the ridge has never been seen up close, and the mantle beneath it has never been sampled in place.

### Closing

{{ambient}}
So ocean ridges are not pushed up. The longest mountain range on Earth is not a wall raised by collision but a seam that never finishes closing, built from rock that melted because the weight above it lifted, and that will sink again as it cools. Every stretch of ocean floor began there, at a crest, as something briefly molten.
