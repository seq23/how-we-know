On January 23, 1960, Jacques Piccard and U.S. Navy Lieutenant Don Walsh descended in the bathyscaphe Trieste. The vehicle used a gasoline-filled float for buoyancy and an attached pressure sphere for the crew. They reached the floor of Challenger Deep and remained there briefly before returning. The onboard depth indicator produced a value that was later corrected through additional calculations. This is not a flaw in the achievement. It shows that reaching the place and measuring the place are different engineering problems. Water density changes with temperature and salinity. Gravity varies slightly with location. The unresolved question becomes surprisingly technical: when a sensor reports pressure, how does that number become meters below mean sea level?

A 2021 peer-reviewed analysis used submersible transects and pressure observations to revise the maximum depth of Challenger Deep. The deepest observed seafloor value was 10,935 meters, with an uncertainty of plus or minus six meters at 95 percent confidence. The team corrected for water-column properties, atmospheric pressure, gravity, gravity gradients, and water-level effects. An acoustic altimeter measured the vehicle's height above the seafloor as it crossed the terrain. That combination is stronger than reading one gauge at one landing point.
